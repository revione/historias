# The Israel-Palestine conflict: actors, failures, and geopolitics

> Second part. Covers Israel's internal political dynamics, the origin and logic of Hamas, why all peace attempts failed, Iran's role, and why the United States still supports Israel today.

---

## 1. Israel's internal political dynamics

To understand why Israel makes the decisions it makes, one must understand how its political system works internally. Many things that seem irrational or extreme from outside become coherent when seen through the structure of power.

### Electoral system: why small parties rule

Israel uses pure proportional representation with a single national district. A party that receives 5% of the vote receives roughly 5% of the seats in the Knesset. No party usually wins a majority alone, so governments must be built through coalitions that reach 61 seats.

This gives small parties disproportionate bargaining power. A party with 6% of the vote can demand territorial, religious, or security policies as a condition for joining a coalition. Ideologically extreme minorities can therefore exert influence far beyond their share of votes.

### Main blocs

**Likud and the secular right**:
Likud, Netanyahu's party, is nationalist, pro-market, hardline on security, and skeptical of agreements requiring significant territorial concessions. It is secular in the sense that it does not govern by Torah law, but it has systematically depended on religious parties to form coalitions.

Netanyahu is a political phenomenon in his own right: Israel's longest-serving prime minister. His survival depends on exceptional coalition management, which means constant concessions to more extreme partners.

**Religious Zionism and the far right**:
This bloc has grown enormously in influence. Its leading figures include **Bezalel Smotrich**, finance minister and key figure in West Bank civil administration, and **Itamar Ben Gvir**, national security minister. Smotrich denies Palestinian peoplehood and supports unlimited settlement expansion. Ben Gvir has a history of incitement and ideological proximity to Meir Kahane.

**The declining center and left**:
The Labor Party, which founded Israel and governed for decades, won only 4 of 120 seats in 2022. The Israeli left that promoted Oslo has nearly disappeared electorally. The failure of Oslo, the Second Intifada, rocket attacks from Gaza, and October 7 all pushed the Israeli center rightward.

**Ultra-Orthodox parties (Haredim)**:
Parties such as Shas and United Torah Judaism are not religious Zionists in Smotrich's sense. Many Haredi sectors were historically anti-Zionist or skeptical. Over time they reached a modus vivendi with the state: support governments in exchange for exemptions from military service, funding for religious schools, community subsidies, and control over marriage and divorce through religious courts.

This creates a paradox: ultra-Orthodox communities contribute less economically and militarily, yet hold enormous political leverage. The 2023 judicial reform crisis exposed that tension dramatically.

### The 2023 judicial reform

Netanyahu's government tried to limit the Supreme Court's ability to strike down parliamentary laws. In a country without a formal constitution, weak separation of powers, and coalition dominance, the Supreme Court is one of the few real checks on government power.

Hundreds of thousands protested for months. Air force reservists threatened not to report. Economists warned of capital flight. Secular Israelis saw the reform as a move toward authoritarianism benefiting religious parties. October 7 suspended the debate, but the social fracture remained.

## 2. Hamas: origin, logic, and ideology

Understanding Hamas does not mean justifying it. It means analyzing it accurately.

### The origin: Israel helped create it

Hamas emerged in 1987, at the beginning of the **First Intifada**, as the Palestinian branch of the **Muslim Brotherhood**. The First Intifada began as a mass civilian uprising in Gaza and the West Bank against twenty years of Israeli military occupation. It was largely unarmed: stones, strikes, boycotts, civil disobedience, and local self-organization.

Israel responded harshly. Yitzhak Rabin, then defense minister, spoke of a policy of "breaking bones." The uprising lasted until the Oslo Accords and killed roughly 1,100 Palestinians and 160 Israelis. Its major political consequence was to show that occupation had an unsustainable long-term cost.

In its early years, Israel tolerated and facilitated the growth of Hamas as a counterweight to the PLO. The PLO was secular, leftist, internationally recognized, and politically dangerous to Israel because it had state ambitions. Hamas appeared religious and charitable, building mosques and schools, and seemed useful in dividing Palestinian politics. Israeli officials later acknowledged this strategy. It became a classic case of creating a tactical counterweight without thinking through long-term consequences.

### Hamas charter and ideological evolution

Hamas's 1988 charter was openly antisemitic, called for Israel's destruction, and cited the Protocols of the Elders of Zion. In 2017 Hamas issued a new political document that attempted to soften its image: it distinguished Jews from Zionists, Judaism from Zionism, and suggested accepting a Palestinian state on the 1967 borders as an interim step. Some saw pragmatism; Israel saw public relations. Hamas has never formally recognized Israel's right to exist.

### How Hamas took power in Gaza

In 2006, one year after Israel withdrew from Gaza, Hamas won Palestinian legislative elections with 44% of the vote. The United States and Israel, which had pushed for elections, rejected the result, cut funding, and supported Fatah.

In 2007, armed conflict broke out between Hamas and Fatah. Hamas expelled Fatah from Gaza and took full control. Since then, Fatah controls the Palestinian Authority in the West Bank, while Hamas controls Gaza. Palestinians have had no unified government since 2007.

### The Gaza blockade and the cycle of violence

After Hamas took control, Israel and Egypt imposed a blockade restricting movement of people and goods. The stated goal was to pressure Hamas economically until it collapsed or was rejected by the population. Instead, Gaza became one of the most densely populated and economically desperate places in the world.

Before the current war, unemployment was above 45%, 80% of the population depended on humanitarian aid, and 97% of water was undrinkable. In that context, Hamas persisted partly because it was government, service provider, employer, and the only organization claiming not to accept the situation. That does not justify terrorism, but it explains why the blockade did not eliminate Hamas.

### October 7, 2023

The Hamas attack on October 7 was the deadliest attack against Jews since the Holocaust. Around 1,200 people were killed, mostly civilians, and around 250 were taken hostage. The attack included deliberate cruelty designed for psychological and media impact.

From Hamas's perspective, one objective was to break regional normalization, especially possible Israel-Saudi normalization. From Israel's perspective, it confirmed the foundational trauma: that Jews can again be massacred. The Israeli military response in Gaza cannot be understood without that psychological weight.

## 3. Why all peace attempts failed

The conflict did not remain unresolved for lack of attempts. Each attempt collided with structural obstacles no negotiator could overcome.

### Oslo (1993): hope and cracks

Oslo was the most hopeful moment in the conflict. Israel recognized the PLO as the legitimate representative of the Palestinians, and the PLO recognized Israel's right to exist. The agreement created the Palestinian Authority and set up a five-year process to negotiate borders, Jerusalem, and refugees.

The core problem: Oslo was an agreement about process, not final outcome. The hardest issues were postponed. During the Oslo period, Israeli settlements in the West Bank nearly doubled. Israel argued Oslo did not explicitly forbid this; Palestinians argued negotiating land while building settlements on it was like dividing a pizza while one side kept eating it.

In 1995, Rabin was assassinated by Yigal Amir, an Israeli ultranationalist who saw Oslo as religious betrayal. Rabin was the only Israeli leader with enough military legitimacy to carry the right toward peace. His death changed history.

### Camp David (2000): the myth of the "generous offer"

In 2000, Clinton brought Ehud Barak and Yasser Arafat to Camp David. The dominant Western narrative became that Barak made a generous offer and Arafat rejected it without counteroffer.

The offer was more complicated: 91-95% of the West Bank, with major settlement blocs retained by Israel and land swaps; Palestinian sovereignty in East Jerusalem with exceptions; no real right of return for refugees.

For Palestinians, the retained settlement blocs fragmented the future state, refugee return was politically and morally central, and Jerusalem remained unresolved. Arafat's failure to present a detailed counteroffer was a serious political mistake, but the idea that a perfect offer was rejected out of pure intransigence is a simplification.

### The Second Intifada

After Camp David failed, Ariel Sharon visited the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif with a heavy police presence. The Second Intifada erupted. Unlike the First Intifada, it was far more violent. Hamas and Islamic Jihad carried out suicide bombings in buses, restaurants, and markets, killing more than a thousand Israeli civilians over five years.

The effect on Israel's peace camp was devastating. Many Israelis concluded there was "no partner for peace." Labor collapsed, and the Israeli left has never recovered.

### Annapolis and Abbas-Olmert (2007-2008)

Ehud Olmert and Mahmoud Abbas held the most advanced negotiations since Camp David. Olmert offered 93.7% of the West Bank plus land swaps, sovereignty over East Jerusalem and the Arab Old City, and limited refugee return. Abbas asked for time to study the map. Olmert was then indicted for corruption and resigned. The next government did not continue from the same point. It was perhaps the closest opportunity to a final agreement.

### The Abraham Accords: peace without Palestine

In 2020, the Trump administration promoted normalization between Israel and several Arab states: UAE, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan. Saudi Arabia began similar talks.

This reversed the logic of the **Arab Peace Initiative of 2002**, in which the Arab League offered full recognition and normalization with Israel in exchange for withdrawal to pre-1967 borders, an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as capital, and a negotiated solution for refugees. Israel did not respond with a serious counteroffer.

The Abraham Accords showed that some Gulf states were willing to normalize without solving Palestine, motivated by fear of Iran and economic/technological interests. For Palestinians it felt like betrayal. For Israel it proved regional integration was possible without concessions. For Hamas it showed that normalization could bury the Palestinian issue, and October 7 was partly a response to that process.

### The repeated pattern

Whenever peace advances, actors on both sides have incentives to sabotage it. On the Israeli side: settlers, religious parties, and those who see territorial withdrawal as sin or strategic suicide. On the Palestinian side: Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and those who see recognizing Israel as betrayal. Leaders who approach compromise pay enormous personal costs: Rabin was murdered, Arafat demonized, Olmert fell while negotiating. Jerusalem, refugees, and exact borders carry such symbolic weight that leaders cannot easily concede them.

## 4. Iran's role

Iran is central and indirect at the same time. It does not border Israel and has no Palestinian citizens, yet it funds, arms, and trains many of the groups fighting Israel.

### From ally to existential enemy

Before 1979, Iran and Israel were allies under the Shah. The Islamic Revolution changed everything. Ayatollah Khomeini made hostility to Israel a founding principle of the new Iranian state. Ideologically, Iran presented itself as defender of the oppressed Muslim world; politically, anti-Israel positioning allowed Persian Shiite Iran to claim leadership in an Arab and Sunni region.

### The Axis of Resistance

Iran built a network of allied armed groups around Israel:

**Hezbollah** in Lebanon was created with Iranian Revolutionary Guard support in 1982. It now possesses tens of thousands of rockets and missiles, operates as a political party, social network, army, and terrorist organization according to the US and EU.

**Hamas and Islamic Jihad** in Gaza have a more complex relationship with Iran because Hamas is Sunni and Iran is Shiite, but strategic interests reconciled them. Iran funds Hamas and transfers rocket and tunnel technology. Islamic Jihad is even more directly dependent on Iran.

**The Houthis** in Yemen joined the post-October 7 pressure campaign by launching missiles and drones toward Israel and attacking Red Sea shipping.

**Militias in Iraq and Syria** provide additional pressure against US and Israeli interests.

### The multipolar front

Iran's concept is "unity of fronts": if Israel fights one member of the axis, others apply pressure simultaneously. After October 7, Israel fought in Gaza while Hezbollah opened a northern front, Houthis attacked shipping, and Iraqi militias attacked US bases.

### Iran's nuclear program

Iran's nuclear capability changes the strategic equation. Iran has enriched uranium to high levels and could, if it chose, move toward weapons-grade enrichment rapidly. Israel's stated position is that it will not allow Iran to obtain nuclear weapons. Israel previously destroyed nuclear reactors in Iraq (1981) and Syria (2007), and has sabotaged Iran through Stuxnet and assassinations of nuclear scientists.

A nuclear Iran would place Iranian-backed groups under a potential nuclear umbrella and make deterrence far more unstable.

### The regional paradox

Fear of Iran also explains the Abraham Accords. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and other Sunni states see Iran as a primary threat. This has created a paradox: Arab states that historically rejected Israel now cooperate informally with it because they share an enemy. Iran, while presenting itself as defender of Palestinians, has accelerated Israeli normalization with Sunni Arab states by making them fear Tehran more.

## 5. Why the United States still supports Israel

US support for Israel is one of the most stable elements of American foreign policy. The public reasons are real, but incomplete.

### Declared reasons

**Shared values**: Israel is described as the only full democracy in the Middle East, with elections, press, judiciary, and minority protections within its recognized borders.

**Strategic alliance**: Israel shares intelligence, tests American weapons in real combat conditions, and projects US power in the Middle East without requiring American troops on the ground.

### AIPAC and the pro-Israel lobby

**AIPAC** is one of Washington's most powerful lobbies. Its main mechanism is not only direct funding but tracking congressional votes and activating donor networks to reward or punish politicians. Voting against pro-Israel measures can have concrete electoral costs.

### Defense industry

The US gives Israel about **$3.8 billion annually** in military aid, much of which must be spent on American weapons. That makes aid to Israel also a subsidy to US defense contractors such as Lockheed Martin, Boeing, and Raytheon. Their lobbying reinforces support.

### American Jewish community

American Jews are about 2% of the population but are concentrated in key states and have high political participation. They are also influential in media, academia, entertainment, and donations. They are not monolithic: younger American Jews are increasingly critical of Israeli government policy, and groups such as J Street represent a pro-peace alternative to the older establishment.

### Evangelical Christians

The largest pro-Israel constituency in the US is not Jewish but evangelical Christian. Tens of millions support Israel for theological reasons tied to premillennial beliefs: the return of Jews to the Holy Land and the rebuilding of the Temple are seen as conditions for Christ's return. This made support for Israel a core Republican principle. Trump's recognition of Jerusalem as Israel's capital and of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights reflected that base.

### Limits of US support

The US has pressured Israel at key moments: Eisenhower forced withdrawal from Sinai in 1956; Carter pushed Camp David; George H. W. Bush conditioned loan guarantees over settlements; Obama clashed with Netanyahu. But military aid was never suspended, the UN veto was never removed, and basic support for Israel's security remained intact.

### The UN veto

The American veto in the UN Security Council is the mechanism that turns political support into international legal protection. The US has vetoed more Israel-related resolutions than any other topic in its Security Council history, including resolutions condemning settlements, calling for investigations, demanding ceasefires, or opposing annexation.

Binding international sanctions require Security Council resolutions. Without them, General Assembly votes are symbolic. This means Israel can continue building settlements considered illegal under international law without facing binding sanctions, because the US veto functions as a permanent shield.

The real US debate is not whether to support Israel, but how far and under what conditions. That debate has shifted, especially among younger voters and the Democratic left.

## 6. The thread connecting everything

These five themes form a system:

**Israeli internal politics** explains why territorial concessions are so hard: settler-linked parties can veto coalitions.

**Hamas** exists partly because Israel helped it grow, partly because the Gaza blockade created conditions for it to persist, and partly because **Iran** funds and arms it as part of a broader strategy.

**Peace attempts** failed because actors with incentives to sabotage them had more operational capacity than actors seeking success.

**The United States** has structural economic, electoral, and ideological incentives to keep supporting Israel. As long as those incentives remain, American pressure on Israel will remain limited.

The result is an incentive system in which almost every powerful actor has more to gain from maintaining the conflict than from resolving it. Not because there is a single villain, but because there is an architecture.
