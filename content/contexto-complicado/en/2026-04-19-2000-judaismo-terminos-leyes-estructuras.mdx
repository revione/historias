# Judaism: terms, laws, and structures

---

## Goy / Goyim

**Goy** (גּוֹי) is singular. **Goyim** (גּוֹיִם) is plural.

### Origin and literal meaning

In Biblical Hebrew, the word simply means "nation" or "people." In the Torah it is used to refer to any nation, including Israel. In Genesis, God tells Abraham that He will make him into a great *goy*. It was not originally a negative term.

### Evolution of the term

Over time, within rabbinic Judaism, *goy* came to mean specifically "non-Jew." In that usage it is descriptive. However, in modern colloquial use, the term can sound derogatory depending on the speaker, tone, and context. It is not automatically neutral or automatically offensive: it depends on the concrete use.

| Context | Use |
|---|---|
| Biblical Hebrew | Neutral, means "nation" |
| Classical rabbinic Judaism | Descriptive, "non-Jew" |
| Modern colloquial use | Can be neutral or derogatory depending on context |
| Ultra-Orthodox communities | Often implies a sharper religious and legal distinction |

### Relevant legal categories

The Jewish legal system does not treat "goy" as a monolithic category. It distinguishes between:

| Term | Legal meaning |
|---|---|
| Yisrael | Jew, subject to the full Jewish law |
| Ger Toshav | Non-Jew who lives among Jews and accepts the Noahide laws |
| Goy / Nochri | Non-Jew in general |
| Akum | Talmudic term for idolaters specifically |

The term **Akum** requires precision. It is not synonymous with "non-Jew" in general. In Talmudic and medieval texts it referred to idolaters in very specific technical senses. Different authors and periods applied it in different ways. Using it outside that technical context is imprecise.

## Legal Distinctions Between Jews and Non-Jews

This is one of the most sensitive and frequently misrepresented subjects. What follows is neither a defense nor a condemnation of the system, but a description of what the texts say and where the real debate lies.

### What the Torah says

**Loans with interest — Ribbit (רִבִּית)**

Exodus 22:24:
> "If you lend money to My people, to the poor person with you, you shall not be to him as a creditor; you shall not impose interest upon him."

Deuteronomy 23:20-21:
> "You shall not charge interest to your brother... To the foreigner you may charge interest, but to your brother you shall not."

The distinction is in the original text. The prohibition against charging interest applies explicitly between Jews. With non-Jews it is permitted according to the standard reading.

**Lost property — Aveidah**

Deuteronomy 22:1-3 obligates returning lost property to one's "brother." The rabbinic discussion is not uniform in scope, basis, or practical application. The positions are multiple and depend on author and period.

### The internal tension of the Talmud

Here is what the original text omitted: the Talmud is not uniform on these issues. There are positions that extend legal protections to all human beings, positions that restrict them, and debates that were never definitively resolved. Some examples:

- The Babylonian Talmud contains statements that protect the property of the non-Jew for reasons of *Kiddush Hashem* (sanctification of the divine name) and to avoid public disgrace.
- Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah* has formulations that limit certain protections to non-Jews. Other *poskim* (legal decisors) read him differently or contextualize him historically.
- The *Shulchan Aruch* (sixteenth century) preserves distinctions, but later jurists debated their applicability in contexts where non-Jews are no longer idolaters.

Simply saying that "the distinctions do not imply superiority" is a reasonable starting position, but it leaves out the fact that this debate exists within Judaism itself and is not closed.

### The historical context

All ancient cultures had legal systems that distinguished between members of the group and outsiders. Greeks, Romans, Arab tribes. Ancient Judaism was not exceptional in this.

What is specific to the Jewish context is that these laws developed and were codified while Jews lived as a minority without a state of their own, in closed communities out of the need for survival. The internal legal system was the only thing they controlled. That neither justifies nor condemns anything, but it is the framework without which the laws cannot be understood.

## Shabbat

**Shabbat** (שַׁבָּת) is the weekly day of rest. It runs from Friday at sunset until Saturday at nightfall, when three stars appear.

### The textual origin

**Genesis:** God created in six days and rested on the seventh. That rest becomes the model.

**Exodus 20:8-10:**
> "Remember the day of Shabbat to sanctify it. Six days you shall work... but the seventh day is Shabbat to the Lord your God. You shall not do any work."

**Deuteronomy 5:** Repeats the commandment and adds another reason: remembering slavery in Egypt. Rest is also an act of freedom, not only imitation of the divine.

### What it forbids and what it permits

The Torah does not define what "work" is. It only says not to work and not to kindle fire (Exodus 35:3, the only concrete example it gives).

The development of what "working" means is the task of rabbinic Judaism, codified in the Mishnah and the Talmud.

In general terms, rabbinic tradition understands Shabbat as prohibiting acts of creation or material transformation. It does not prohibit physical effort as such. Walking many kilometers is permitted. Lighting a match is not.

The 39 melachot apply **only** during the approximately 25 hours of Shabbat. The rest of the week they have no effect. This does not mean that all halachah disappears during the rest of the week, but specifically that these 39 categories are Shabbat restrictions.

On work during the other six days: the Torah says "six days you shall work," which some authors read as a positive obligation and others simply as permission. This is not a unanimous formulation among the *poskim*.

### The three normative layers

| Category | What it is | Level of severity |
|---|---|---|
| D'oraita | The 39 melachot and their direct derivatives, from the Torah | Very grave to violate intentionally |
| D'rabbanan | Rabbinic prohibitions added as a preventive "fence" | Less grave, but obligatory |
| Minhag | Customs adopted by communities | Variable |

### The positive side of Shabbat

Shabbat is not only a list of restrictions. It has an affirmative dimension:

- Lighting Shabbat candles before sunset on Friday
- **Kiddush**: blessing over wine
- Festive family meals
- Torah study
- Real rest: sleeping, walking, conversing
- **Havdalah**: ceremony at the close of Shabbat with wine, spices, and fire

### Modern solutions

| Solution | Function |
|---|---|
| Eruv Chatzerot | Symbolic boundary that turns a public area into a shared private domain, allowing objects to be carried within that perimeter |
| Grama | Indirect action whose result is not immediate; some electronic devices are designed under this principle |
| Shabbos clock | Timer programmed before Shabbat that turns lights on and off automatically |

### Differences between communities

| Community | Level of observance |
|---|---|
| Orthodox | Follows the 39 melachot and their modern extensions strictly |
| Conservative | Follows the basis with some leniencies, such as driving to synagogue |
| Reform | Shabbat is more a spiritual concept than a list of technical prohibitions |

## The 39 Melachot (מְלָאכוֹת)

**Melachah** does not simply mean "work." It means creative and transformative work.

The Talmud derived the 39 categories from the kinds of work performed to build the **Mishkan** (Tabernacle in the desert). The logic: if those acts were important enough to build the dwelling place of God, they define what "working" means. The textual connection is structural: the Torah places the commandment of Shabbat immediately before and after the instructions for building the Mishkan.

The 39 are first listed in the **Mishnah, Tractate Shabbat, chapter 7, mishnah 2**:
> "The primary categories of labor are forty minus one..."

The Mishnah was compiled by Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi around 200 CE. The canonical formulations are specific and technical: "writing two letters," "erasing in order to write two letters," "making two loops," "weaving two threads," "tearing in order to sew two stitches." The descriptions that follow preserve that precision where possible.

| Text | Period | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Torah | ~1200 BCE (according to Jewish tradition; exact dates are debated in modern academic scholarship) | The general commandment |
| Mishnah | ~200 CE | Lists the 39 for the first time |
| Babylonian Talmud | ~500 CE | Explains each one, cases and exceptions |
| Jerusalem Talmud | ~400 CE | Parallel version with variations |
| Maimonides — Mishneh Torah | 12th century | Codifies and organizes the system |
| Shulchan Aruch | 16th century | Standard practical norm to this day |

### Group 1: Bread (11 melachot)

They follow the full process of making bread from seed to oven.

#### 1. Zore'a — זוֹרֵעַ — Sowing
Any act that promotes plant growth: planting, watering, grafting.  
*Modern applications:* watering the garden, planting in a pot.

#### 2. Choresh — חוֹרֵשׁ — Plowing
Preparing the soil to receive seeds.  
*Modern applications:* turning soil, making a hole in the ground.

#### 3. Kotzer — קוֹצֵר — Reaping
Separating a plant from its source of growth.  
*Modern applications:* cutting flowers, picking fruit from a tree, mowing the lawn.

#### 4. Me'amer — מְעַמֵּר — Gathering the harvest
Gathering what has been harvested into one place.  
*Modern applications:* collecting fallen fruit and piling it up.

#### 5. Dash — דָּשׁ — Threshing
Separating grain from straw or husk.  
*Modern applications:* squeezing fruit for juice, milking cows, wringing wet clothing.

#### 6. Zoreh — זוֹרֶה — Winnowing
Using wind to separate grain from chaff.  
*Modern applications:* blowing on something to separate parts, using a fan to separate materials.

#### 7. Borer — בּוֹרֵר — Selecting
Separating the unwanted from the wanted within a mixture. One of the most complex and everyday categories. The technical formulation requires doing it for immediate use, without a tool, taking what is desired (not discarding what is undesired).  
*Modern applications:* removing fish bones, separating mixed cutlery, removing the bad part of a fruit.

#### 8. Tochen — טוֹחֵן — Grinding
Reducing something solid into smaller parts.  
*Modern applications:* grinding coffee, grating cheese, chopping vegetables finely, crushing medication.

#### 9. Meraked — מְרַקֵּד — Sifting
Separating fine particles from coarse ones with a sieve.  
*Modern applications:* sifting flour, filtering liquids.

#### 10. Lash — לָשׁ — Kneading
Combining solid particles with liquid to form a cohesive dough.  
*Modern applications:* making dough, mixing cement or mud. Mixing puree with liquid can enter this category according to some *poskim*.

#### 11. Ofeh / Bishul — אוֹפֶה / בִּישּׁוּל — Baking / Cooking
Transforming food through heat. One of the broadest categories.  
*Modern applications:* cooking, boiling, frying, heating. There is detailed debate over what temperature activates the prohibition.

### Group 2: Clothing (13 melachot)

They follow the process of making a garment from the animal to the sewn fabric.

#### 12. Gozez — גּוֹזֵז — Shearing
Separating something that grows from the body.  
*Modern applications:* cutting hair, nails, shaving.

#### 13. Melaben — מְלַבֵּן — Whitening / Washing
Cleaning fibers or clothing.  
*Modern applications:* washing clothes, scrubbing fabrics. Rubbing a stain with water can enter here.

#### 14. Menafetz — מְנַפֵּץ — Carding / Untangling fibers
Separating and softening fibers so they can be spun.  
*Modern applications:* combing wool, separating textile fibers.

#### 15. Tzove'a — צוֹבֵעַ — Dyeing
Changing the color of something permanently.  
*Modern applications:* dyeing fabric or hair, applying makeup (debated), writing with a colored marker.

#### 16. Toveh — טוֹוֶה — Spinning
Turning loose fibers into thread by twisting them.  
*Modern applications:* twisting ropes, braiding threads.

#### 17. Meisach — מֵסֵךְ — Preparing the loom
Setting the warp threads on the loom. A technical preparatory step.

#### 18. Oseh Shtei Batei Nirin — עוֹשֶׂה שְׁתֵּי בָּתֵּי נִירִין — Preparing the loom heddles
Inserting the threads into the guide loops of the loom. Another technical step in the weaving process.

#### 19. Oreg — אוֹרֵג — Weaving
Interlacing threads to form fabric. The technical formulation of the Mishnah specifies "weaving two threads."  
*Modern applications:* knitting, braiding, macrame.

#### 20. Potzea — פּוֹצֵעַ — Separating woven threads
Undoing woven fabric, separating interlaced threads. The opposite of weaving. The Mishnah specifies "tearing in order to sew two stitches."  
*Modern applications:* unstitching, undoing woven material.

#### 21. Kosher — קוֹשֵׁר — Tying
Making a permanent or durable knot. The Mishnah speaks of "making two loops."  
*Modern applications:* tying with a permanent knot. A simple everyday shoelace knot is generally permitted.

#### 22. Matir — מַתִּיר — Untying
Undoing permanent knots. The opposite of tying.

#### 23. Tofer — תּוֹפֵר — Sewing
Joining two materials permanently.  
*Modern applications:* sewing, gluing with permanent glue, stapling papers. There is debate over whether attaching a new button enters here.

#### 24. Kore'a — קוֹרֵעַ — Tearing / Unsewing
Separating sewn materials or cutting them in a meaningful way.  
*Modern applications:* unstitching, tearing fabric intentionally, opening an envelope (debated).

### Group 3: Leather (7 melachot)

They follow the process of turning an animal into worked leather.

#### 25. Tzad — צָד — Trapping
Capturing a living being that was free.  
*Modern applications:* trapping an insect, fishing, catching a domestic animal that escaped.

#### 26. Shohet — שׁוֹחֵט — Killing / Slaughtering
Taking the life of a living being.  
*Modern applications:* killing any living being, including insects. There is debate about very small insects.

#### 27. Mafshit — מַפְשִׁיט — Skinning
Separating the skin from the animal.  
*Modern applications:* peeling fruit is generally permitted under conditions; it depends on the *posek*.

#### 28. Me'abed — מְעַבֵּד — Tanning
Treating a hide to preserve it.  
*Modern applications:* softening leather, applying cream to shoes (debated), salting foods to preserve them (debated).

#### 29. Mesartet — מְשָׂרְטֶט — Drawing lines / Marking
Making lines or marks as a guide for cutting or writing. According to Rashi, it means drawing lines on leather in order to cut it to exact size, though it is not limited to leather and applies to marking any material with that function. Technical note: Mesartet does not appear directly in the Mishnah on 73a; the Talmud (Shabbat 75b) incorporates it because salting and tanning turned out to be the same melachah, freeing a place in the list in order to keep the total at 39.  
*Modern applications:* scratching a surface to mark where to cut, drawing guide lines before writing.

#### 30. Memachek — מְמַחֵק — Scraping / Smoothing
Smoothing a surface until it is perfectly smooth.  
*Modern applications:* sanding, polishing, smoothing. Applying cream to skin and spreading it is debated.

#### 31. Mechatech — מְחַתֵּךְ — Cutting to size
Cutting material precisely to a specific measurement.  
*Modern applications:* cutting paper or fabric to exact size. Cutting food is generally permitted.

---

### Group 4: Writing (2 melachot)

#### 32. Kotev — כּוֹתֵב — Writing
Creating marks with permanent meaning. The technical formulation is "writing two letters."  
*Modern applications:* writing with any instrument, typing, printing. Photography is debated: some derive it from *Kotev* (creating a permanent image), others from electrical categories; it is not a universally accepted application.

#### 33. Mochek — מוֹחֵק — Erasing
The exact formulation of the Mishnah is "erasing in order to write two letters." Purpose matters: erasing to leave clean space where one can write is what is forbidden.

---

### Group 5: Construction, fire, and completion (5 melachot)

#### 34. Boneh — בּוֹנֶה — Building
Any act of construction or permanent improvement of a structure.  
*Modern applications:* driving a nail into a wall, assembling furniture permanently. Some cases, such as inflating an air mattress or opening a large fixed umbrella, are debated.

#### 35. Soter — סוֹתֵר — Demolishing
Destroying a structure with constructive intent, that is, in order to rebuild it better. Purpose is defining. Demolishing without intent to rebuild is considered less severe by some *poskim*.

#### 36. Mav'ir — מַבְעִיר — Kindling fire
Creating or increasing a flame or heat source. It is the only example the Torah gives explicitly (Exodus 35:3).  
*Modern applications:* lighting a match, candle, lighter. Electricity is prohibited in Orthodox practice, but there is no single consensus on the exact halachic basis: different authorities derive it from *Mav'ir* (kindling fire), from *Makeh BaPatish* (completing a circuit), or from other categories. Starting a car is also prohibited according to various halachic interpretations.

#### 37. Mechabeh — מְכַבֶּה — Extinguishing fire
Extinguishing a flame or reducing its intensity.  
*Modern applications:* turning off electric light, lowering the heating. Closing an app that consumes processor power is debated.

#### 38. Makeh BaPatish — מַכֶּה בַּפַּטִּישׁ — The final hammer blow
The most conceptual of the 39. It represents the act that completes and finalizes an object, making it ready for use. It is not a literal hammer blow.  
*Modern applications:* finishing the assembly of a piece of furniture with the final step, activating an electronic device for the first time, folding a cardboard box so it is ready for use.

---

### Group 6: Transport (1 melachah)

#### 39. Hotza'ah — הוֹצָאָה — Transporting / Carrying
Moving an object from private domain to public domain or vice versa, or carrying it more than four cubits in public domain. One of the most complex categories in modern urban life.  
*Modern applications:* carrying keys in a pocket on the street, pushing a baby stroller, carrying a book.

The rabbinic solution is the **Eruv**: a symbolic boundary that turns a public area into an extension of private domain.

---

### The unifying principle

All the melachot have something in common: they are acts of creation, transformation, or dominion over the material world. Shabbat does not prohibit physical effort, but transformative intervention in reality. That is why moving a piano may be permitted while lighting a match is not.

## Jewish Marriage

### The basis in the Torah

**Genesis 2:24:** Establishes marriage as a union between man and woman.

**Deuteronomy 7:3-4:** Prohibits marriage with the seven nations of Canaan for reasons of religious assimilation. Rabbinic tradition extended this principle to non-Jews in general, though the exact scope of that extension is a matter of debate among *poskim*.

### Kiddushin: marriage as a legal act

Jewish marriage is called **Kiddushin** (קִדּוּשִׁין), from the same root as *Kadosh* (holy). It is not merely a civil contract. It is an act of sanctification with concrete legal implications.

Historically it had two separate stages:

| Stage | Name | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Erusin (אֵרוּסִין) — Legal betrothal | Legally binding, without cohabitation |
| 2 | Nissuin (נִישּׂוּאִין) — Full marriage | Cohabitation, consummation |

In the Middle Ages these two stages were merged into one ceremony.

### The ceremony under the Chuppah

The **Chuppah** (חֻפָּה) is the wedding canopy. It represents the new home.

**The central legal act:** The groom places a ring on the bride's index finger and says:

> *"Harei at mekudeshet li b'tabaat zo k'dat Moshe v'Yisrael"*  
> "By this ring, you are consecrated to me according to the law of Moses and Israel"

Without this formula, there is no valid *Kiddushin*.

The ring must be: owned by the groom, of known value, without precious stones, smooth.

**The Sheva Brachot:** Seven blessings over wine. They celebrate creation, joy, and union.

**Breaking the glass:** The groom breaks a glass with his foot. It remembers the destruction of the Temple even at the moment of greatest joy. The *Mazel Tov* comes immediately afterward.

### The Ketubah: the marriage contract

The **Ketubah** (כְּתוּבָּה) is a contract signed by two witnesses in which the groom assumes specific obligations: to feed his wife, clothe her, provide for her needs, and pay a specified sum in case of divorce or death.

In the ancient world it represented a significant legal innovation in its historical context: the woman had legally enforceable rights, the man had formal obligations, and divorce had an economic cost. That discouraged impulsive divorce and gave real protection to the woman.

### Divorce: Get

The **Get** (גֵּט) is Jewish divorce. The Torah establishes it in Deuteronomy 24:1.

The historical and current legal problem: the Get must be given voluntarily by the husband. The woman cannot initiate it unilaterally according to Orthodox law.

This created the figure of the **Agunah** (עֲגוּנָה), literally "chained woman": a woman whose husband refuses to grant the Get. Without it:

- She cannot remarry according to Jewish law
- Her children from a new relationship would be considered *mamzerim* (illegitimate)

This remains one of the most urgent unresolved problems of modern Orthodox Judaism. There are communities and rabbis seeking solutions within the halachic framework; none has been universally accepted.

### The prohibition of mixed marriage

According to Orthodox Jewish law, marriage with a non-Jew is not valid as *Kiddushin*. The implications for children depend on who is Jewish in the couple (see matrilineality).

### Matrilineality

Jewish identity is transmitted through the mother, not the father. It is one of the most debated features of the system.

The historical origin of this rule is not unanimous:

- One position: it derives from the Talmudic interpretation of specific verses
- Another position: it arose in periods of persecution when the identity of the mother was always verifiable
- Another position: it has roots in the older tribal structure

The Reform movement also adopted patrilineality in the twentieth century. This means that there are people recognized as Jewish by part of the Jewish community but not by the Orthodox rabbinate, and vice versa. It is a real division with concrete legal consequences.

**Note on Kabbalistic explanations:** Linking matrilineality with the sefirah of Binah or with the principle of Saturn is a later Kabbalistic reading, not a historical explanation for the origin of the rule. Presenting it as a causal explanation would be imprecise.

### Conversion: Giyur

For a non-Jew who wants to marry within the Jewish legal framework, conversion exists.

**Orthodox process:**

- Intensive study (minimum one year)
- Full adoption of Jewish law
- For men: circumcision
- Immersion in the Mikveh (ritual bath)
- Appearance before a rabbinic court (*Bet Din*)

Conversions performed by Conservative or Reform courts are not recognized by the Orthodox rabbinate of Israel. This creates serious legal conflicts for those who want to marry in Israel, where there is no internal civil marriage, although the State does recognize civil marriages performed abroad.

### Differences between communities

| Aspect | Orthodox | Conservative | Reform |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marriage with non-Jews | Forbidden and not recognized | Discouraged but occurs | Accepted with conditions |
| Jewish identity | Matrilineal only | Matrilineal | Matrilineal and patrilineal |
| Get | Required; only the husband can give it | Required; seek solutions for Agunah | Civil divorce is sufficient |
| Same-sex marriage | Forbidden | Permitted in many communities | Fully accepted |

### The problem of who decides

In Israel there is no internal civil marriage. There is only religious marriage. The Orthodox rabbinate has legal power to decide who may marry whom, which creates conflicts with non-Orthodox Jews and with people who have Israeli citizenship under the Law of Return but are not recognized as Jewish by the rabbinate. The State recognizes civil marriages performed outside the country, which has led some Israelis to marry abroad specifically to bypass the internal religious system.

The Law of Return allows anyone with one Jewish grandparent to immigrate to Israel. That person may have Israeli citizenship but not be considered Jewish for marriage in Israel according to religious law. These are two systems with different criteria coexisting in tension.
