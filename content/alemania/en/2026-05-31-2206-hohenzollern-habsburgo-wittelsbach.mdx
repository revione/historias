# Hohenzollern, Habsburg and Wittelsbach — The Three Crowns of Germany

---

When the German Empire collapsed in November 1918, it was not one throne that fell: it was twenty-two. Kings, grand dukes, dukes and princes abdicated in a chain — most in less than a week — and the densest and most fragmented aristocracy in Europe vanished. But three houses dominated above all: Hohenzollern, which held the imperial crown and the kingdom of Prussia; Habsburg, which ruled the Austro-Hungarian Empire; and Wittelsbach, which reigned over Bavaria, the second-largest state of the German Empire.

What follows is not a genealogy of three families. It is the story of how they exercised power for centuries, how they lost it in a matter of days, and how their heirs continue to dispute it — no longer on the battlefield, but in courts, foundations and negotiations with the state — more than a century later.

---

## 1415 — The Hohenzollerns receive Brandenburg

The origin of Hohenzollern power was not in Berlin but in Swabia, at the castle of Zollern — today Burg Hohenzollern, near Hechingen. They were a family of the lower nobility from southern Germany, without significant territories or wealth comparable to the great prince-electors. The turning point came in 1415, when Emperor Sigismund of Luxembourg rewarded Frederick VI of Hohenzollern — burgrave of Nuremberg — with the Margraviate of Brandenburg and the title of Prince-Elector. The reward was no gift: Brandenburg was a poor, sandy, sparsely populated territory, nicknamed "the sandbox of the Holy Roman Empire." But it brought with it a vote in the election of the emperor — the family's true asset for the next two and a half centuries.

Frederick I, as he came to be called, moved the court to Brandenburg. His successors spent generations consolidating the territory, buying neighboring fiefs, inheriting rights and disputing land with the local nobility. It was slow, bureaucratic work, without military glory, but relentless. What set the Hohenzollerns apart from other German dynasties was not their wealth or their antiquity: it was their administrative capacity. They built a state from nothing, estate by estate, right by right.

---

## 1525 — Duchy of Prussia: the second pillar

In 1525, Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach — the last Grand Master of the Teutonic Order and cousin of the main Hohenzollern line — secularized the monastic state of the Teutonic Order and turned it into the Duchy of Prussia, a secular fief under Polish sovereignty. Albert had converted to Lutheranism and established the first Protestant state church in Europe. Teutonic Prussia vanished and in its place arose a Protestant duchy ruled by a Hohenzollern.

In 1618, when the ducal Prussian line died out without heirs, the Elector of Brandenburg — John Sigismund — inherited the duchy. Thus was born Brandenburg-Prussia: two territories separated by hundreds of kilometers of Polish territory, without geographical continuity, but united under a single crown. The map looked like an accident, but the union changed the nature of the family. They were no longer just electors of the Holy Roman Empire: they were sovereigns of a duchy outside the Empire, which gave them an independence no other German prince possessed.

---

## 1640–1688 — The Great Elector: the state before the dynasty

Frederick William, known as the Great Elector (1640–1688), inherited a state devastated by the Thirty Years' War. Brandenburg had lost a third of its population; Prussia was isolated and impoverished. In the following forty-eight years, Frederick William rebuilt the territory with a strategy that would define the Hohenzollerns: a standing army financed by a centralized tax administration.

He was not a conqueror — though he did expand the territory. He was a builder of institutions. He created Brandenburg's first standing army, reorganized the tax system without consulting the noble estates, and broke the power of the Junkers — the Prussian landed nobility — by forcing them to serve the state in exchange for keeping their local privileges. The pact was explicit: the nobles retained absolute control over their peasants and their land, but paid taxes and served as army officers. Thus was born the "Junker state" that would survive until 1918.

When he died in 1688, Brandenburg-Prussia was the second military power of the Holy Roman Empire — behind only Austria. But it was still not a kingdom.

---

## 1701 — Frederick III crowns himself King in Prussia

On 18 January 1701, Frederick III — son of the Great Elector — crowned himself as Frederick I, King *in* Prussia, in a lavish ceremony in Königsberg. The preposition was crucial: he could not call himself King *of* Prussia because western Prussia — with the city of Danzig — was still Polish territory. He was king *in* the eastern part of the duchy, which he did control. The title was a diplomatic artifice, but it worked.

The coronation was the foundational act of what Christopher Clark — in *Iron Kingdom: The Rise and Downfall of Prussia, 1600–1947* (Harvard University Press, 2006) — calls "the most improbable monarchy in Europe." Brandenburg-Prussia had no natural resources, no advantageous geographical position, no large population. Its only asset was an efficient state and an army that consumed 70% of the budget. The phrase that summed up Prussia in the 18th century was coined by the Marquis de Mirabeau: "Prussia is not a state with an army, but an army with a state."

---

## 1713–1740 — Frederick William I: the Soldier King

Frederick William I (1713–1740), grandson of the Great Elector and son of Frederick I, is the figure who turned Prussia into a military machine. He canceled the construction of the royal palace his father had commissioned, dismissed the court, reduced court spending by 75%, and redirected the entire fiscal surplus to the army. He doubled the size of the armed forces — from 40,000 to 80,000 men — and created the most professional officer corps in Europe, recruited exclusively from the Junkers.

He ruled personally, without favorites, without ministers making decisions for him. He reviewed the state accounts every morning and punished corruption with immediate imprisonment. His obsession with discipline gave rise to famous anecdotes — such as the Potsdam Giants regiment, a unit of exceptionally tall soldiers recruited from across Europe —, but the reality was more serious: he built the most solid state treasury on the continent and a recruitment system (the *Kantonreglement*) that linked military service to the territory.

When he died in 1740, he left an army of 83,000 men, a treasury of eight million Taler in cash — with no public debt —, and a son who in less than a year would take Prussia to war against Austria. That son was Frederick II, the Great.

---

## 1740–1786 — Frederick II the Great

Frederick II (1740–1786) leveraged every asset his ancestors had built. When Emperor Charles VI of Habsburg died without a male heir in October 1740, Frederick did not wait for the succession dispute to be resolved: in December he invaded Silesia, an Austrian province rich in agriculture, mining and textile manufacture. It was the first time in a century that a Hohenzollern had launched an offensive war against the Habsburgs, and it shattered the dynastic order of the Holy Roman Empire.

The three Silesian Wars (1740–1742, 1744–1745, 1756–1763) consolidated the annexation. The last of them — the Seven Years' War, in which Prussia survived against a coalition of Austria, France, Russia, Sweden and Saxony — turned Frederick into a European legend. The "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg" — the death of Tsarina Elizabeth in 1762 and the Russian withdrawal from the war — saved him from total defeat. When peace was signed in 1763, Prussia was indisputably the second German power and one of the five great European powers.

But Frederick was not just a commander. He abolished judicial torture, established religious tolerance — "everyone may seek salvation in his own way" —, promoted potato cultivation to combat famines, and drafted legal codes that limited the arbitrary power of landowners over peasants. He was an enlightened despot in the most literal sense: he concentrated all power, but exercised it on rational principles. He played the flute, wrote philosophical treatises, corresponded with Voltaire, and governed Prussia like a precision clock. He died in 1786 without children — his marriage was never consummated and it is speculated that he was homosexual —, and the throne passed to his nephew Frederick William II.

---

## 1797–1840 — Napoleonic reforms and the Holy Alliance

The 19th century began badly for the Hohenzollerns. In 1806, Napoleon crushed the Prussian army in the twin battles of Jena and Auerstedt — in a single day. Prussia lost half its territory, its army was reduced to 42,000 men, and Frederick William III had to flee to Memel, on the far eastern edge of the kingdom.

The response was not military but administrative. The Prussian reformers — Baron vom Stein and Prince von Hardenberg — abolished serfdom (1807), reorganized the government with specialized ministries, and, under the military leadership of Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, created an army based on universal military service with merit-based promotion. The agrarian reform freed the peasants but consolidated the Junkers as capitalist landowners — a pattern that would repeat until 1918.

In 1813, Prussia joined the Sixth Coalition against Napoleon. The Battle of Leipzig — the Battle of the Nations — in October of that year was the largest battle in European history until the First World War: 600,000 soldiers on the field. Napoleon was defeated and Prussia recovered its territories. At the Congress of Vienna (1815), Prussia received the Rhineland, Westphalia and part of Saxony — territories that turned it into an industrial power as well as a military one. Frederick William III, together with Tsar Alexander I of Russia and Emperor Francis I of Austria, formed the Holy Alliance, a pact of Christian monarchs to suppress revolutions and preserve the absolutist order.

---

## 1861–1888 — Wilhelm I and Bismarck: German unification

In 1861, Wilhelm I — younger brother of Frederick William IV, who had died without children — ascended the throne at age sixty-three. In 1862 he appointed Otto von Bismarck as chancellor, a Junker of the old school, absolute monarchist, who governed Prussia for twenty-eight years with a combination of political calculation, diplomatic manipulation, and three perfectly orchestrated wars.

The Second Schleswig War (1864) against Denmark gave Prussia control of Schleswig-Holstein. The Austro-Prussian War (1866) eliminated Austria from German politics and created the North German Confederation under Prussian hegemony: twenty-two states north of the Main River, with Wilhelm I as federal president and Bismarck as federal chancellor. The southern states — Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and Hesse — were left out, but Bismarck tied them with secret military alliance treaties.

The Franco-Prussian War (1870–71) was the masterstroke. Bismarck manipulated a diplomatic telegram — the famous Ems Dispatch — to provoke France, which declared war on 19 July 1870. The southern German states, bound by the alliance treaties, fought on the Prussian side. In less than a year, the German armies defeated France, captured Napoleon III at Sedan, and besieged Paris.

On 18 January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles, Wilhelm I was proclaimed German Emperor (Deutscher Kaiser). The event was deeply symbolic: German unification was proclaimed in the palace of the defeated enemy, on the anniversary of Frederick I's coronation in Königsberg in 1701, and not by popular acclamation but by the German princes gathered at Versailles. Wilhelm I accepted the title reluctantly — he preferred "Emperor of Germany," but Bismarck imposed "German Emperor" so as not to offend the other monarchs.

The German Empire was a federation of twenty-five states — four kingdoms, six grand duchies, five duchies, seven principalities and three free cities — under the hereditary presidency of the King of Prussia. The Hohenzollerns were not just emperors: they remained, above all, kings of Prussia. The army, the bureaucracy, foreign policy — everything was in Prussian hands. The other states kept their monarchs, their parliaments and some autonomy, but real power was in Berlin.

---

## 1888–1918 — Wilhelm II: the last Kaiser

Wilhelm II ascended the throne in June 1888 — the Year of the Three Emperors, in which Wilhelm I (March) and Frederick III (June, after ninety-nine days of reign) died. He was twenty-nine years old, had a left arm atrophied by a difficult birth, and a personality that historians — notably John C. G. Röhl in his monumental trilogy *Wilhelm II* (Cambridge University Press, 1993–2008) — have described as narcissistic, impulsive and deeply insecure.

He dismissed Bismarck in 1890 — "The pilot leaves the ship," was the famous headline in *Punch* magazine — and governed personally for the following twenty-eight years. His reign combined industrial modernization — in 1913 Germany surpassed Great Britain in steel production —, naval expansion — the High Seas Fleet challenged British hegemony in the North Sea —, and erratic diplomacy that isolated Germany in Europe. That same Kaiser had personally arranged in 1906 the marriage of Bertha Krupp to Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach, merging the crown with the cannon — the same Krupp steel that armored his High Seas Fleet.

The Kaiser's power was not absolute — the Reichstag had budgetary control, political parties were strong, and the 1871 constitution established a complex federalism —, but Wilhelm II concentrated the supreme military command, appointed the chancellor, and could dissolve parliament. The Berlin court was the center of political life, and the Kaiser was the final arbiter of all important disputes.

The First World War (1914–1918) destroyed everything. From 1916, real power passed to the military — Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff, who governed Germany as a military dictatorship under the guise of the monarchy. Wilhelm II was marginalized even within his own system. In October 1918, with the army collapsing and revolution spreading through German cities, political leaders asked him to abdicate to save the monarchy. He refused. On 9 November 1918, Chancellor Max von Baden unilaterally announced the Kaiser's abdication — without his consent —, and Friedrich Ebert, Social Democratic leader, proclaimed the republic from a window of the Reichstag. Wilhelm II fled to the Netherlands, where he lived in exile until his death in 1941.

The other German monarchs fell in the same week. King Ludwig III of Bavaria (Wittelsbach) fled Munich on 7 November. The grand dukes, dukes and princes abdicated one after another, without resistance. Five hundred years of monarchical rule in Germany ended in seven days.

## 1273 — The origin of Habsburg power

The Habsburgs took their name from the fortress of Habichtsburg — the "Hawk's Castle" — in the Swiss canton of Aargau, built around 1020. They were minor counts in the Holy Roman Empire until 1273, when Rudolf I was elected King of the Romans — de facto emperor, though he was not formally crowned by the Pope. Rudolf was not the most powerful candidate: he was elected precisely because he was not, at a moment when the prince-electors preferred a weak emperor.

But Rudolf proved surprisingly effective. In 1278 he defeated and killed Ottokar II of Bohemia at the Battle of Marchfeld, and granted the duchies of Austria and Styria to his sons in 1282 as imperial fiefs. That act changed European history: the Habsburgs went from Swiss counts to Austrian dukes, and Austria became their power base for the next six and a half centuries.

---

## 1438–1740 — Three continuous centuries of the imperial crown

From the election of Albert II in 1438 until the death of Charles VI in 1740, the Habsburgs held the crown of the Holy Roman Empire almost uninterruptedly — the only exception was Charles VII of Bavaria (Wittelsbach) between 1742 and 1745. The family motto, *A.E.I.O.U.*, was interpreted as *Austriae est imperare orbi universo* ("It is Austria's destiny to rule the whole world").

Habsburg power was not built by military conquest — with some important exceptions — but by marriage. The phrase *Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube* ("Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry") was not propaganda: it was an accurate description of the dynastic strategy. In 1477, Maximilian I married Mary of Burgundy and acquired the Netherlands. In 1496, his son Philip the Fair married Joanna of Castile, and his grandson, Charles V, inherited Spain, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia, the American possessions and the Netherlands. In 1521, Charles's brother, Ferdinand I, married Anna of Bohemia and Hungary, uniting those kingdoms to the Habsburg domains.

Charles V (1519–1556) ruled an empire where — according to the famous expression — "the sun never set." But the enormity of the territory was ungovernable. In 1556 he abdicated and divided his possessions: the Spanish branch — his son Philip II — received Spain, the Netherlands, Italy and the Americas; the Austrian branch — his brother Ferdinand I — received the hereditary Austrian territories and the imperial crown. The division was permanent. The Spanish line of the Habsburgs died out in 1700 with Charles II, triggering the War of the Spanish Succession.

---

## 1526–1683 — The bastion against the Ottoman Empire

The acquisition of Bohemia and Hungary in 1526 by Ferdinand I turned the Habsburgs into the first line of defense of Christendom against the Ottoman Empire. For a century and a half, Vienna was the capital of an empire that lived under constant threat of Turkish invasion. In 1529, Suleiman the Magnificent besieged Vienna; he failed, but the danger did not disappear.

In 1683, Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa besieged Vienna for the second time with an army of 150,000 men. The city resisted for two months until a coalition of imperial, Polish and German forces — led by the Polish king John III Sobieski and Duke Charles V of Lorraine — broke the siege at the Battle of Kahlenberg on 12 September. It was one of the turning points of European history: from then on began the reconquest of Hungary and Habsburg expansion into the Balkans.

---

## 1740–1780 — Maria Theresa: the first woman to rule Austria

In 1740 Charles VI died without a male heir. To guarantee the succession of his daughter Maria Theresa, he had spent the last years negotiating the Pragmatic Sanction — a treaty that recognized the right of female succession — with all the European powers. The princes signed it. The day Charles VI died, Frederick II of Prussia invaded Silesia.

Maria Theresa was twenty-three years old, had not been educated in affairs of state, and had just given birth to her third child. But in the following forty years she proved to be the most capable ruler of her dynasty. She did not recover Silesia — that loss was permanent —, but she preserved the rest of the empire, reorganized the army and the administration, and founded the Austrian state bureaucracy that would survive into the 20th century.

She ruled as Archduchess of Austria and Queen of Hungary — not as Empress, a title held by her husband Francis I from 1745. She had sixteen children, and married them to the European dynasties with the same strategy as her ancestors. Her youngest daughter, Marie Antoinette, married the Dauphin of France — the future Louis XVI — and died on the guillotine.

---

## 1804–1867 — From the Holy Roman Empire to the Austrian Empire

In 1804, Napoleon crowned himself Emperor of the French. Francis II responded by proclaiming himself Emperor of Austria — Francis I —, creating a hereditary imperial title that did not depend on the Holy Roman Empire. Two years later, in 1806 and after the defeat of the Third Coalition at Austerlitz, Francis formally dissolved the Holy Roman Empire. A thousand years of institution ended with a decree signed by the last emperor.

The Austrian Empire that replaced it was a mosaic of eleven nationalities — Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, Ukrainians, Ruthenians, Romanians, Croats, Serbs, Italians — without a common language, without a unified parliament, and with different legal and administrative systems in each territory. The glue that held the empire together was the dynasty: the Habsburgs were the only ones who ruled over all the nations of the empire, and their legitimacy derived not from popular will but from tradition, the Catholic religion and personal loyalty to the monarch.

The revolution of 1848 almost destroyed it. Ferdinand I — nicknamed "the Benevolent" but mentally incapable of governing — abdicated in favor of his nephew Franz Joseph I, who was eighteen years old and would reign for the next sixty-eight. The imperial army, loyal to the dynasty, crushed the revolutions in Vienna, Prague and Hungary.

---

## 1848–1916 — Franz Joseph I: sixty-eight years on the throne

Franz Joseph I (1848–1916) is the figure who defines Habsburg decline. He was not a tyrant or incompetent — he was a meticulous bureaucrat who worked twelve hours a day processing documents —, but he governed an empire that could no longer be reformed. According to A. J. P. Taylor in *The Habsburg Monarchy 1809–1918* (Hamish Hamilton, 1948), "the empire lasted as long as Franz Joseph lived, and collapsed when he died."

The territorial losses were constant. In 1859, the war against France and Piedmont cost him Lombardy. In 1866, the defeat against Prussia in the Austro-Prussian War expelled Austria definitively from Germany. The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 divided the empire into two halves: Austria and Hungary, each with its own parliament, united only by the person of the emperor-king and three common ministries (Foreign Affairs, War and Finance). The Hungarians obtained equality with the Austrians, but the Czechs, Slovaks, Croats, Romanians and other nationalities remained subordinated to one or the other. The compromise postponed the collapse, but at the cost of petrifying the empire in a structure that no one could reform without provoking Hungary's secession.

Franz Joseph's personal life was a succession of tragedies. His brother Maximilian was executed in Mexico in 1867. His only son, Crown Prince Rudolf, committed suicide together with his lover, Baroness Mary Vetsera, at Mayerling in 1889. His wife, Empress Elisabeth — Sisi —, was assassinated by an Italian anarchist in Geneva in 1898. His nephew and heir, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, was assassinated in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 — the trigger for the First World War. Franz Joseph died in the midst of the war, on 21 November 1916, at age eighty-six.

---

## 1916–1918 — Charles I: the last emperor

Charles I, great-nephew of Franz Joseph, inherited the throne in the middle of the war. He attempted to negotiate a separate peace with the Allies — the "Sixtus Affair" of 1917, through his brother-in-law, Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma —, but the talks failed when Germany found out. In October 1918, with the army defeated and the nationalities proclaiming their independence — Czechoslovakia on 28 October, the State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs on 29 October, Hungary dissolving the union on 31 October —, Charles published a manifesto proposing a federation of autonomous states. It was too late: no one accepted it. On 11 November 1918 he issued a declaration renouncing "all participation in the affairs of state" — without formally abdicating — and withdrew to Schönbrunn Palace. The next day the Republic of German-Austria was proclaimed. Charles and his family went into exile in Switzerland in March 1919.

He attempted to reclaim the Hungarian throne on two occasions — March and October 1921 —, but failed both times due to the opposition of Regent Miklós Horthy and pressure from neighboring states, which threatened to invade Hungary. The Allies exiled him to the island of Madeira, where he died of pneumonia on 1 April 1922, at age thirty-four. His widow, Empress Zita, lived until 1989 and never accepted the republic.

## 1180–1329 — The rise of the Wittelsbachs in Bavaria

The Wittelsbachs took their name from the castle of Wittelsbach in Upper Bavaria, built around 1119. In 1180, Emperor Frederick Barbarossa deposed Duke Henry the Lion and granted the Duchy of Bavaria to Otto I of Wittelsbach, a loyal noble who had fought in the imperial campaigns in Italy. It was the only case in German history in which a tribal duchy — the ancient Guelph Duchy of Bavaria — passed entirely to a new dynasty.

For the following 738 years, Bavaria was governed by the Wittelsbachs without interruption — the longest dynastic reign in German history, far longer than the Hohenzollerns in Brandenburg-Prussia or the Habsburgs in Austria. In 1329, the Treaty of Pavia divided the dynasty into two branches: the senior Palatinate line (which inherited the title of Elector) and the junior Bavarian line. Both branches governed separate territories until the Bavarian line inherited the Palatinate in 1777.

---

## 1623–1806 — Electors of Bavaria

During the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), Maximilian I of Bavaria — leader of the Catholic League — allied with the Habsburgs against the Protestant princes. In 1620 he defeated Frederick V of the Palatinate, the "Winter King," at the Battle of White Mountain. As a reward, Emperor Ferdinand II transferred the electoral dignity from the Palatinate to Bavaria in 1623 and gave him the Upper Palatinate. The Bavarian Wittelsbachs became electors, the highest rank of the imperial nobility after the emperor.

For the following two centuries, Bavaria was the main Catholic counterweight to Prussia within the Holy Roman Empire. Its alliance with France — especially under Maximilian II Emanuel (1679–1726) and Charles Albert (1726–1745) — allowed it to maintain some independence from the Habsburgs. In 1742, Charles Albert was elected emperor of the Holy Roman Empire as Charles VII — the only non-Habsburg between 1437 and the dissolution of the empire in 1806 —, but he died in 1745 and Bavaria, defeated in the War of the Austrian Succession, returned to the Habsburg orbit.

---

## 1806 — Bavaria becomes a kingdom

On 1 January 1806, Napoleon elevated Bavaria from an electorate to a kingdom. Maximilian I Joseph of Wittelsbach — who had been elector since 1799 — became the first king of Bavaria. The transformation was the product of the alliance with Napoleon: Bavaria contributed 30,000 soldiers to the invasion of Russia in 1812 and received in return territories from secularized ecclesiastical states and annexed imperial cities — Würzburg, Augsburg, Nuremberg. Bavaria's population tripled in a decade.

Maximilian I Joseph was one of the most effective reformist monarchs of the Confederation of the Rhine. He promulgated a constitution in 1808 — the first of a German state — that established equality before the law, the abolition of feudal privileges of the nobility, and freedom of conscience. In 1813, ten days before the Battle of Leipzig, he changed sides and joined the coalition against Napoleon — a perfectly calculated move that allowed him to retain all his territorial gains at the Congress of Vienna in 1815. Bavaria entered the 19th century as a sovereign kingdom, with consolidated territory, modern institutions, and a constitution that — though revised in 1818 — made it one of the most liberal German states.

---

## 1825–1886 — Three Ludwigs: the cultural and political splendor of Munich

The three kings who governed Bavaria in the 19th century bore the same name — Ludwig —, but represented radically different trajectories.

### Ludwig I (1825–1848)

Ludwig I, son of Maximilian I, was a deeply contradictory monarch. On one hand, he transformed Munich into a European cultural center: he built the Glyptothek, the Alte Pinakothek, the Ludwigstraße, and transferred the university from Landshut to the capital. He turned Munich into a city of museums, neoclassical squares and boulevards. On the other hand, his relationship with the Irish dancer Lola Montez — whom he made Countess of Landsfeld — triggered a scandal that combined morality, politics and xenophobia. In March 1848, with Munich in revolt, Ludwig I abdicated in favor of his son Maximilian II. He died in Nice in 1868.

### Maximilian II (1848–1864)

Maximilian II, son of Ludwig I, governed for sixteen years without major crises or great deeds. He was an intellectual king: he summoned to Munich a circle of writers and scholars — the *Symphilosophenkreis* — that included Leopold von Ranke, the father of scientific German historiography. He promoted Bavarian historical research and founded the Bayerisches Nationalmuseum. His reign was one of consolidation, not transformation. He died suddenly in 1864, at age fifty-two, leaving the throne to his eldest son, Ludwig, eighteen years old.

### Ludwig II (1864–1886)

Ludwig II is the Wittelsbach the world remembers. He ascended the throne at eighteen and during the following twenty-two governed ever less and built ever more. His castles — Neuschwanstein, Linderhof, Herrenchiemsee — are today Germany's most visited monuments. His patronage enabled Richard Wagner to compose *Tristan and Isolde*, *The Mastersingers of Nuremberg*, *The Ring of the Nibelung* and *Parsifal* — works that without Ludwig's financing would probably never have been completed.

But Ludwig II did not govern Bavaria — or not in the administrative sense. In 1866, after the Austrian defeat, Bavaria signed the alliance treaties with Prussia that tied it militarily to Berlin. In 1870, Ludwig sent Bavarian troops to fight on the Prussian side against France. In November 1870, pressed by Bismarck, he signed the letter — drafted by Bismarck — "inviting" the King of Prussia to accept the German imperial crown. It was the Kaiserbrief, the document that formalized the offer of the imperial crown to Wilhelm I. In return, Ludwig received secret payments from the Welf Fund — the estate of the deposed royal house of Hanover, confiscated by Prussia — that financed his castles.

In June 1886, the Bavarian government declared him mentally incapacitated — the diagnosis of "paranoia" was issued by psychiatrist Bernhard von Gudden without examining the patient — and interned him at Berg Castle, on Lake Starnberg. On 13 June, Ludwig went for a walk with von Gudden. Hours later the bodies of both were found floating in the lake. The official cause was suicide by drowning, but Ludwig was an excellent swimmer and the lake was shallow. The circumstances of his death were never clarified. The version most accepted among historians is that he tried to escape, von Gudden tried to stop him, and in the struggle both died — possibly by a gunshot, then thrown into the water.

---

## 1886–1913 — The regency and the last king

On Ludwig II's death, the throne passed to his brother Otto I, who had been diagnosed for years with a severe mental illness and never governed. The uncle of both, Luitpold, assumed the regency from 1886 until his death in 1912. His twenty-six-year reign as Prince Regent was an era of stability for Bavaria: the *Prinzregentenzeit* is remembered as a period of cultural splendor, with Munich competing with Berlin as a European artistic center.

In 1913, the Bavarian parliament approved a constitutional amendment that allowed them to depose Otto I and proclaim as king Luitpold's son, Ludwig III, who became the last Wittelsbach monarch. He governed for five years.

---

## September–November 1918 — How they lost power in seven days

The German military collapse in the autumn of 1918 was total. On 29 September, Ludendorff informed the Kaiser that the war was lost and demanded a parliamentary government — not out of democratic conviction, but so that the civilians would sign the armistice and bear the blame. On 3 October, Prince Max von Baden was appointed chancellor with a cabinet that included Social Democrats. On 28 October, the constitution was reformed to turn Germany into a parliamentary monarchy: the chancellor required the confidence of the Reichstag, not only of the Kaiser.

It was too late. On 3 November the Kiel sailors' mutiny broke out, when sailors refused to set sail for a final suicidal battle against the British fleet. In four days, the workers' and soldiers' councils — following the model of the Russian Soviets of 1917 — spread through all German cities. There was no central committee directing the revolution: it was a spontaneous, disorganized, and almost bloodless movement.

**7 November 1918 — Munich.** Kurt Eisner, an independent socialist journalist, led a demonstration of workers and soldiers through the center of Munich. The army — without orders and unwilling to fire on the crowd — did not intervene. King Ludwig III, who had gone out for his evening walk in the Englischer Garten, found the palace surrounded on his return. That night he fled Munich without resistance. At ten in the evening, Eisner proclaimed the Free State of Bavaria — a republic — from the balcony of the Landtag. Ludwig III was the first German monarch to fall. He died in exile in Hungary in 1921.

**9 November 1918 — Berlin.** The general strike paralyzed the capital. Thousands of workers marched toward the center, and the troops — except for some elite battalions — refused to fire. Chancellor Max von Baden, desperate to save the monarchy as an institution, unilaterally announced Wilhelm II's abdication and appointed Friedrich Ebert, SPD leader, as chancellor. Wilhelm II, at the headquarters in Spa (Belgium), refused to abdicate for hours. Only when Hindenburg, the Chief of the General Staff and a convinced monarchist, told him that the army no longer supported him, did he agree to cross the Dutch border on 10 November.

That same afternoon, Philipp Scheidemann — Social Democrat and State Secretary — proclaimed the republic from a balcony of the Reichstag with the words: "The German people has triumphed across the board! The old, rotten regime has collapsed! Long live the German Republic!" Two hours later, Karl Liebknecht — leader of the Spartacus League, the embryo of the Communist Party — proclaimed the "Free Socialist Republic of Germany" from a balcony of the Berlin Palace. On the same day, from the same city, two rival republics were proclaimed. The German revolution, unlike the Russian one, was not settled.

**11 November 1918 — Vienna.** Emperor Charles I of Austria issued his declaration renouncing "all participation in the affairs of state." Like Wilhelm II in Germany, he did not use the word "abdication." Like Wilhelm II, he refused to sign a formal renunciation of the throne. Like Wilhelm II, he went into exile convinced that the monarchy would return. On 12 November, the Provisional National Assembly proclaimed the Republic of German-Austria. The Habsburgs had governed Austria for 640 years.

## 1919 — The dispossession: how the republics treated their former dynasties

The republics that succeeded the German and Austrian monarchies faced the same question: what to do with the assets of the royal families? The answers were radically different, and defined the disputes that remain open more than a century later.

### The Habsburg Law (Habsburgergesetz) of 1919

Austria took the hardest path. On 3 April 1919, the National Assembly passed the Habsburg Law (*Habsburgergesetz*), which confiscated all the family's assets — palaces, castles, lands, art collections, archives — in favor of the state. The law prohibited the Habsburgs from residing in Austria unless they formally renounced their dynastic rights and declared themselves "loyal citizens of the republic." Charles I did not do so; he died in exile in 1922. His widow Zita and their eight children — among them Otto, the heir — lived in exile for decades.

The law did not distinguish between crown assets and private family property: everything was confiscated. Palaces like Schönbrunn or the Hofburg passed to the state and became museums. The agricultural and forestry lands were nationalized. The art collections were integrated into state museums — one of the reasons why Vienna today has one of the most extraordinary concentrations of art in the world.

The *Habsburgergesetz* belongs to the German pattern of the *Lex specialis*: laws written for a single entity. The *Lex Krupp* of 1943 (text 2201) dissolved an AG to transfer it to a single heir; the VW-Gesetz of 1960 (text 2206) governs a single company with special voting rights. All three circumvent general law for political reasons. The descendants of Charles I cannot run for the presidency of Austria — a restriction that Otto von Habsburg unsuccessfully challenged before the Austrian Constitutional Court in 1963. The law was softened in 1996, when Austria lifted the entry ban on Habsburgs who had not renounced their rights, but the confiscatory core was never reversed. The family has litigated individual cases — such as the return of a castle and lands in Styria in 2019 —, but there has been no general restitution.

### The Hohenzollern dispute: the restitution that never ends

In Germany the approach was different. The Weimar Constitution of 1919 did not confiscate noble property in a generalized manner, but allowed each state to negotiate individual agreements with the former ruling houses. The result was a mosaic of agreements, compensations and disputes that continue to this day.

The Hohenzollerns — the Prussian imperial house — signed an agreement with the Free State of Prussia in 1926. The family received important private properties: Cecilienhof Palace in Potsdam — where the Potsdam Conference was held in 1945 —, Burg Hohenzollern in Hechingen, several hunting lodges and thousands of hectares of forest and agricultural land. Wilhelm II, exiled in the Netherlands, received substantial financial compensation. But the question of what was "private" and what was "crown property" was left deliberately ambiguous.

After the Second World War, Hohenzollern properties in the Soviet zone — which would become the GDR — were expropriated without compensation by the communist land reform of 1945. The family lost castles, lands and collections in Brandenburg. The German reunification of 1990 opened the possibility of claiming these properties, and since then the Hohenzollern dispute has been one of the longest-running legal battles in German legal history.

The central case revolves around Georg Friedrich Prince of Prussia — great-great-grandson of Wilhelm II and current head of the House of Hohenzollern —, who since 2014 has claimed the restitution of assets valued at hundreds of millions of euros: thousands of pieces of art, the Cecilienhof residence, and the right to reside in various state properties. The legal basis is the Indemnification and Compensation Act of 1994 (*Entschädigungs- und Ausgleichsleistungsgesetz*, EALG), which allows the restitution of properties expropriated in the Soviet zone if the original owner did not substantially collaborate with the Nazi regime.

The key point is precisely that: did Crown Prince Wilhelm of Prussia — the ex-Kaiser's eldest son and head of the house between 1941 and 1951 — collaborate with the Nazis? Georg Friedrich maintains that he did not. Historians have presented documents showing that Wilhelm of Prussia wrote letters to Hitler in 1932 and 1933 urging him to "restore the monarchy," publicly supported the rise of the NSDAP, and participated in regime events. The case has generated a high-level historiographical dispute: Christopher Clark (*Iron Kingdom*) and Richard J. Evans (*The Coming of the Third Reich*) were initially consulted by the family, but the publication of the reports generated controversy over the historians' independence. The dispute remains open: in 2023, negotiations between the Hohenzollern family, the State of Brandenburg and the federal government continued without resolution, and the case has passed through multiple courts. The *Süddeutsche Zeitung* and *Der Spiegel* have extensively covered every procedural turn.

### Wittelsbach: the Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds

Bavaria resolved its dynastic question in the most pragmatic way. In 1923, after intense negotiations between the Wittelsbach family and the Free State of Bavaria, the *Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds* (Wittelsbach Compensation Fund) was created. The agreement was ingenious: the family transferred all its artistic and cultural heritage to the fund — which is a foundation under public law under the supervision of the Bavarian Ministry of Finance — in exchange for financial compensation and the right of use of several palaces and castles.

The *Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds* of 1923 was the pioneering foundation that inaugurated the legal form later copied by the Fazit-Stiftung (1959, text 2203), the Krupp-Stiftung (1968, text 2201) and the Bertelsmann Stiftung (1977, text 2203): a foundation that preserves dynastic heritage under the facade of public benefit. The fund today manages the most important artistic treasure in Germany in non-state hands: collections of painting, sculpture, porcelain, jewelry, tapestries, furniture and liturgical objects accumulated by the Wittelsbachs over seven centuries. The fund's income — from agricultural and forestry exploitation of lands, property rentals, and financial investments — is distributed among the members of the Wittelsbach family according to a key established in the 1923 statutes.

The current head of the house is Franz von Bayern (Francis of Bavaria), born in 1933, great-grandson of the last king Ludwig III. Unlike the Hohenzollerns, the Wittelsbachs are not embroiled in massive restitution lawsuits with the state. The *Ausgleichsfonds* of 1923 resolved the question consensually, and the family maintains a stable institutional relationship with the Bavarian state. Franz von Bayern lives in Munich, participates in cultural and charitable events, and occasionally speaks out on politics — in 2021, for example, he was one of the signatories of an open letter from German Catholic personalities calling for reforms in the Church. Nymphenburg Palace, the Munich Residenz and the castles of Ludwig II are the property of the Bavarian state and are open to the public. The Wittelsbach family retains the right of use of certain wings of the Residenz, the castles of Berg, Berchtesgaden and Hohenschwangau, and extensive agricultural and forestry lands.

Munich was the Wittelsbach capital for 738 years, is the seat of ProSiebenSat.1 and was the base of the Kirch empire (text 2204), hosted the founding of the DGB in 1949 (text 2206), and is home to Krauss-Maffei Wegmann — manufacturer of the Leopard 2 tank — (text 2207). It was also where Kurt Eisner proclaimed the republic on 7 November 1918, triggering the fall of all German monarchies. No other German city concentrates this density of layers of power across the centuries.

## 1919–1945 — The dynasties under Nazism

The relationship of the three dynasties with National Socialism was complex and varied enormously from one family to another.

The Hohenzollerns, as has been extensively documented in the context of the current restitution disputes, had an ambiguous relationship with the Nazi regime. Wilhelm II, from his Dutch exile in Doorn, initially viewed Hitler with disdain — he considered him a "classless upstart." But when Germany invaded France in 1940, he wrote Hitler a letter of congratulations, and on his death in June 1941 he left evidence of his admiration for the German military victories. His son, Crown Prince Wilhelm of Prussia, was a member of the NSDAP and the SA, participated in regime events, and corresponded with Hitler. August Wilhelm — another son of the Kaiser — was an enthusiastic Nazi, a member of the SA with the rank of Obergruppenführer, and publicly supported the NSDAP from the early 1930s. The *Führerprinzip* that Hitler applied to Krupp in 1943 — dismantling the AG to concentrate ownership in a single man — was the same ideological principle that made the Nazi hierarchy seductive to monarchist circles. The Hohenzollerns who dreamed of restoration and the industrialist who received absolute ownership responded to the same temptation. The ambiguity of these relationships is precisely what fuels the current legal disputes over restitution.

The Habsburgs, by contrast, were explicitly anti-Nazi. Otto von Habsburg — son of Charles I and heir to the dynasty — actively opposed the Anschluss of 1938 and the Nazi regime. Hitler considered him an enemy: the Gestapo had orders to arrest him if captured. Otto, from his exile in the United States during the war, lobbied Roosevelt and Churchill to preserve the independence of Austria and the Central European states. His cousin, Maximilian of Hohenberg — son of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, whose assassination triggered the First World War — was arrested by the Nazis and sent to the Dachau concentration camp, where he survived until the liberation in 1945.

The Wittelsbachs maintained a lower profile. The main branch remained in Bavaria during the Nazi regime. Crown Prince Rupprecht of Bavaria — son of Ludwig III and head of the house since 1921 — went into exile in Italy during the war, and the family was monitored by the Gestapo. Rupprecht was openly hostile to the regime, but his opposition was discreet and diplomatic, not militant. His son, Albrecht of Bavaria, was briefly interned in a concentration camp in 1944 together with other family members for his alleged connection to the anti-Nazi resistance.

## 1945–1961 — Otto von Habsburg and the renunciation

On 31 May 1961, Otto von Habsburg signed the declaration that put an end to seven hundred years of Habsburg dynastic claims. He formally renounced "all claims to sovereignty and all dynastic rights," accepted the Austrian republic as his state, and declared himself a "loyal citizen of Austria." The document, negotiated with the Austrian government of Julius Raab for years, allowed Otto to return to Austria for the first time since 1919.

The decision divided the family. His mother, the widowed Empress Zita — who would die in 1989 at age ninety-six —, never accepted her son's renunciation and refused to set foot on Austrian soil under the republic's terms. For Zita, the Habsburgs remained the only legitimate authority over their former territories. For Otto, 20th-century politics demanded something else.

Otto von Habsburg became a citizen of the Federal Republic of Germany and was a Member of the European Parliament for the Bavarian CSU for twenty years (1979–1999). He was an active advocate of European integration, EU enlargement to the east, and reconciliation between Catholics and Orthodox. He organized the "Pan-European Picnic" in August 1989 on the Austro-Hungarian border — the event in which hundreds of East Germans crossed the border into Austria, three months before the fall of the Berlin Wall. He died in 2011 and was buried in the Imperial Crypt of the Capuchins in Vienna — with the traditional Habsburg ritual, where the master of ceremonies knocks on the crypt door and is turned away for bearing imperial titles, until he enters as "a poor sinner." His widow, Regina, was buried there in 2010.

## Today — What remains of the power of the three crowns

The power of the three dynasties did not disappear in 1918: it transformed.

**Hohenzollern.** Georg Friedrich Prince of Prussia, great-great-grandson of Wilhelm II, continues to litigate against the German state for the restitution of assets valued at hundreds of millions of euros. Burg Hohenzollern — the ancestral castle in Hechingen — remains private property of the family and attracts hundreds of thousands of visitors a year. Cecilienhof in Potsdam is a state museum, but the family claims usage rights. The legal dispute has generated a fracture within the family itself: some members, notably the historian Georg Friedrich von Bismarck (great-grandson of the chancellor), have publicly criticized the claims, arguing that the family should not benefit from a heritage built with the power of the Prussian state.

**Habsburg.** Karl von Habsburg — Otto's eldest son and current head of the house — is an Austrian businessman and politician who was a Member of the European Parliament (1996–1999) and leads the *Paneuropa-Bewegung Österreich*. He does not seek monarchical restoration. He lives in Austria and manages the family heritage, which includes Eckartsau Castle — where Charles I spent his last days in Austria — and other properties. He has noted in interviews with *Die Presse* and *Der Standard* that "the monarchy is not a political issue in Austria, and it should not be." The *Habsburgergesetz* remains in force and the Habsburgs cannot run for the presidency of Austria — a restriction that Karl von Habsburg has described as "a legal curiosity of the republic." In 2018, an Austrian court overturned a previous ruling and returned to the family a castle and lands in Styria — a minor case compared to the volume of properties confiscated in 1919, but symbolically important.

**Wittelsbach.** The *Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds* manages assets valued at hundreds of millions of euros — art collections that rival national museums. The Wittelsbach family, headed by Franz von Bayern, is probably the German dynasty that best resolved its transition to the republican era: without massive lawsuits, without confrontation with the state, with a clear and stable institutional structure. Franz von Bayern, ninety-two years old in 2025, is single and has no children; his heir is his younger brother, Max Emanuel of Bavaria, Duke in Bavaria — the title of "Duke in Bavaria" corresponds to the junior branch of the family. The Wittelsbach succession is assured, but the main Bavarian line will become extinct in the next generation.

---

A century and some years after November 1918, the three dynasties remain present in German and Austrian life. They do not govern, but they possess. They do not legislate, but they litigate. They do not command armies, but they manage foundations, palaces and art collections that define the cultural landscape of their countries. Monarchical power as an institution ended in seven days of the autumn of 1918. Monarchical power as heritage — and as dispute — survives.
