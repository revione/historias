# The Rundfunkrat — The Illusion of Independence in German Public Broadcasting

---

## 1945–1949 — The Allies design a Goebbels-proof system

When the Allied powers occupied Germany in 1945, one of their priorities was to dismantle the Nazi propaganda apparatus. Joseph Goebbels had turned German radio — the _Reichs-Rundfunk-Gesellschaft_ — into the most effective weapon of mass manipulation of its time: 16 million Volksempfänger receivers in homes, content controlled by the Ministry of Propaganda, zero space for dissenting voices.

The Allied solution — inspired by the model of the British BBC — was to design a public broadcasting system that was **structurally independent of the state**. The specific architect was Hugh Carleton Greene, the British officer who, as the first director of the NWDR, imposed precisely this architecture to prevent any future German government from re-monopolising the airwaves as Goebbels had done. The centrepiece of that design was not the director-general (_Intendant_) but the **collegiate supervisory councils** (_Rundfunkräte_, _Fernsehrat_) composed of representatives of relevant social groups: churches, trade unions, universities, business associations, cultural organisations. The theory was elegant: if the council represented all of society, no government could control programming. The _Intendant_ was accountable to a plural body, not to a minister.

Article 5 of the Basic Law of 1949 enshrined broadcasting freedom. But the institutional architecture was left in the hands of the Länder through interstate treaties (_Staatsverträge_). It was in these treaties — and in the concrete composition of the councils — that the theory began to crack.

---

## 1950 — The ARD is born: federalism as a shield

The _Arbeitsgemeinschaft der öffentlich-rechtlichen Rundfunkanstalten der Bundesrepublik Deutschland_ (ARD) was established in 1950 as a federation of independent regional broadcasters — NDR, WDR, BR, SWR, HR, MDR, RB, RBB, SR — each with its own _Rundfunkrat_, its own _Verwaltungsrat_ and its own _Intendant_. The federal logic was deliberate: if no single Land controlled all broadcasters, no government could control national programming.

But federalism had a blind spot. The _Rundfunkräte_ of each regional broadcaster were appointed by the parliaments of each Land. Parliaments were controlled by parties. And the parties — essentially the SPD and CDU — quickly learned that whoever appoints the councillors controls the Intendanten, and whoever controls the Intendanten controls the editorial line.

---

## 1961 — The First Broadcasting Judgment of the Constitutional Court

On 28 February 1961, the Federal Constitutional Court (_Bundesverfassungsgericht_) issued its first broadcasting judgment (BVerfGE 12, 205). The case concerned the Adenauer government's attempt to create a second television channel controlled by the federal state — the _Deutschland-Fernsehen GmbH_. The Court declared it unconstitutional.

The same Adenauer who attempted to control television in 1960 had been forced ten years earlier by the trade unions to accept the *Montanmitbestimmung* of 1951 — parity co-determination in the coal and steel industries — following a DGB threat of a general strike. He tried to control television and lost; he tried to resist co-determination and also lost. In both cases, the institutional architecture of the Federal Republic — the Constitutional Court in one case, trade union pressure in the other — blocked the Chancellor's path.

The judgment established two principles that would define the German system for decades. First: broadcasting freedom is a "serving freedom" (_dienende Freiheit_) in the service of free public opinion formation. Second: the state must organise broadcasting but remain **at a distance** from content (_Staatsferne_). Broadcasting is not a matter for the government; it is a matter for society.

But the Court did not specify _how_ to guarantee that distance. And the parties were already occupying the ground.

---

## 1963 — The ZDF and its _Fernsehrat_ are born

As a direct consequence of the 1961 ruling, the Länder created the _Zweites Deutsches Fernsehen_ (ZDF) in 1963 through an interstate treaty — the ZDF-Staatsvertrag. Unlike the ARD broadcasters, the ZDF was a single national corporation, not a federal one.

Its oversight body was called the _Fernsehrat_ (Television Council). The composition reflected the Allied theory: 66 members (expanded to 77 in 1991) representing churches, trade unions, employers' associations, universities, sports federations, women's organisations, youth, the environment, etc. Plus representatives of the Länder and the federal government.

The problem was not in the list. It was in who selected the representatives of each group and how they coordinated once inside.

---

## 1971–1986 — The system consolidates; the parties colonise it

Three further rulings by the Constitutional Court — 1971 (BVerfGE 31, 314), 1981 (BVerfGE 57, 295), 1986 (BVerfGE 73, 118) — reinforced the principles of independence and pluralism. But while the judges were writing, the parties were operating.

The capture mechanism was simple and did not require breaking any law. The members of the _Rundfunkräte_ and the _Fernsehrat_ formally represented social groups — the housewives' association, the sports federation, the youth council — but in practice many of these groups were penetrated by the parties or directly controlled by them. A DGB trade unionist voted like the SPD. A representative of the _Mittelstandsvereinigung_ voted like the CDU. The churches — Catholic and Protestant — had predictable party affinities.

Moreover, the party-affiliated members coordinated informally in the so-called **"Freundeskreise"** (circles of friends): the _roter Kreis_ (SPD) and the _schwarzer Kreis_ (CDU/CSU). Before each council session, each circle met separately and decided its vote. The formal decision in the plenary was a ceremony: the result had already been agreed in the preliminary meetings.

The historical coordinator of the _roter Kreis_ has been Frank Werneke, president of ver.di since 2019 — the unified service sector union with 1.88 million members — which illustrates the porosity between the union leadership and the informal control of public television. The Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung, the SPD foundation financed by the trade unions, acts as a transmission belt between the trade union world and these Freundeskreise: it trains the cadres who, decades later, sit on the Fernsehrat.

The system had a perverse advantage for the parties: it was perfectly opaque. The minutes of the _Fernsehrat_ were not published. The meetings of the _Freundeskreise_ did not officially exist. The citizen could not know who voted for what or why. This same CDU/SPD duopoly operates with different mechanisms in other sectors: Freundeskreise in broadcasting, trade union and employer blocks in corporate Mitbestimmung (text 2206), appointments in the Stiftungsrat of the FAZ (text 2203) and self-recruitment in the Kuratorium of the Krupp-Stiftung (text 2201). Same parties, different legal forms, identical result.

---

## The _Intendant_: the position everyone wants to control

To understand the intensity of the party-political struggle, one must understand what an _Intendant_ is in the German system.

The _Intendant_ is the director-general of the broadcaster. He appoints the programme director, the editor-in-chief, the administrative director. He controls the schedule. He decides which news is broadcast, which documentaries are produced, which topics receive coverage and which do not. His mandate lasts between five and nine years. And he is elected by the supervisory council — the _Rundfunkrat_ or the _Fernsehrat_ — by a qualified majority.

In theory, the _Intendant_ is independent: once elected, he receives instructions from no one. In practice, he owes his position to the arithmetic of the _Freundeskreise_. If the SPD and CDU agree on a candidate, he is elected. If not, they block each other until a compromise name is found.

The _Verwaltungsrat_ (Administrative Council), a smaller body — 14 members in the ZDF — oversees the economic management and approves the appointment of senior editorial positions. Its composition replicated the same party-political logic as the _Fernsehrat_.

The result was predictable: the _Intendanten_ and senior editorial positions were, almost without exception, people acceptable to both major parties. No government order was needed to condition the editorial line. It was enough that anyone aspiring to be _Intendant_ knew which profiles and which coverage would not be vetoed by the wrong _Freundeskreis_.

---

## 2000 — Nikolaus Brender arrives at the ZDF central editorial office

Nikolaus Brender took up the position of _Chefredakteur_ (editor-in-chief) of ZDF in April 2000, appointed by the _Intendant_ Dieter Stolte. Brender came from the WDR, where he had directed the political programme _Monitor_. He was known for incisive, independent journalism without deference to political power — including that of the parties that controlled his own supervisory council.

During his first years, Brender modernised ZDF's news, gave it a distinct profile vis-à-vis ARD competition, and maintained a tense but functional relationship with the parties. In 2002, Markus Schächter took over as _Intendant_ of ZDF and renewed confidence in Brender.

The problem began when Brender started covering issues intensely that made specific CDU sectors uncomfortable: irregular party financing in Hessen, connections between politicians and energy lobbies, the management of the financial crisis. It was not a campaign. It was journalism. But in the _Freundeskreise_ ecosystem, uncomfortable journalism is read as political hostility.

---

## 2009 — The Brender case: anatomy of a partisan veto

In November 2009, Nikolaus Brender's contract as editor-in-chief of ZDF was coming to an end. The _Intendant_ Markus Schächter proposed renewing it for another five years. The _Verwaltungsrat_ — the body that had to approve the renewal — voted on 24 November 2009.

The vote was 7 to 5 in favour of Brender. But in the ZDF _Verwaltungsrat_, decisions on senior editorial positions required a majority of three-fifths of the statutory members (9 out of 14 votes). Brender did not achieve that majority. The five votes against came from members affiliated with the CDU/CSU, led by Roland Koch, then _Ministerpräsident_ of Hessen and federal deputy chairman of the CDU. Koch operated from the same institutional ecosystem that finances the German establishment's reference newspaper: the FAZ has its headquarters in Frankfurt, the heart of Hessen, and the Fazit-Stiftung that sustains it was co-founded by the Frankfurt Chamber of Commerce. The politician who vetoed ZDF's editor-in-chief governed the state that hosts the newspaper that sets the establishment's editorial line.

Koch did not hide his motives. He publicly declared that ZDF needed an editor-in-chief who would "guarantee balanced coverage". Translation: someone who would not make the CDU uncomfortable. The federal party leadership — with Angela Merkel as Chancellor and CDU chairwoman — did not intervene publicly to stop Koch. The veto held. The Chancellor who remained silent while her party crushed ZDF's editorial independence was the same leader whose strategic ambivalence on defence spending left the Bundeswehr "in a lamentable state" by 2017.

The decision provoked a wave of criticism. The German Journalists' Association (DJV) and the ver.di trade union denounced a violation of press freedom. Intellectuals, print media and former ZDF executives — including former _Intendant_ Dieter Stolte — called the veto a "scandal". The _Intendant_ Schächter declared that the decision damaged ZDF's credibility as an independent medium.

But the structure held. Brender had to leave his post in March 2010. The system had functioned exactly as it was designed to function — only it was not designed to guarantee independence, but to manage the distribution of power between the two major parties.

---

## 2011–2014 — The complaint before the Constitutional Court

The Brender case was the catalyst, but the problem was systemic. In 2010, the Länder approved the Fifteenth Broadcasting Amendment Treaty (_15. Rundfunkänderungsstaatsvertrag_), which partially reformed the composition of the _Fernsehrat_ and the _Verwaltungsrat_. The reform limited direct government representatives but left the logic of the _Freundeskreise_ intact and did not introduce real transparency requirements.

The governments of Rhineland-Palatinate (SPD) and Hamburg (SPD) filed abstract constitutional review complaints before the Federal Constitutional Court. The case files — 1 BvF 1/11 and 1 BvF 4/11 — asked the court to declare the composition of ZDF's oversight bodies unconstitutional for allowing excessive state and partisan influence.

On 5 November 2013, the First Senate of the Constitutional Court held the oral hearing. On 25 March 2014, it delivered its judgment.

---

## 25 March 2014 — The ruling that mandated reform

The judgment (1 BvF 1/11, 1 BvF 4/11) declared several provisions of the ZDF-Staatsvertrag incompatible with Article 5.1.2 of the Basic Law (_Rundfunkfreiheit_). The court's requirements were concrete and forceful.

**First: the one-third limit.** The proportion of members linked to or close to the state (_staatliche und staatsnahe Mitglieder_) must not exceed one-third of the total statutory members of each body. This included representatives of governments, parliamentarians, elected political officeholders and **leaders of political parties with prominent responsibility**. For every state member, there must be at least two non-state members.

**Second: incompatibilities.** Members considered "non-state" must not be members of governments, parliamentarians, elected political officeholders or high-level party leaders. It is not enough that they do not hold public office: they must be structurally independent of party politics.

**Third: freedom from instructions.** All council members must exercise their function without being subject to instructions (_Weisungsfreiheit_) and can only be removed for serious cause.

**Fourth: transparency.** The legislator must guarantee a minimum of transparency in the functioning of the councils. At minimum: accessible information on the composition of the bodies and their committees, and publication of session minutes or, at least, substantial information on the topics dealt with and the results of the deliberations.

**Fifth: the underlying problem.** The court explicitly identified the _Freundeskreise_ mechanism as a channel of informal state influence that circumvented formal representation limits. It stated that "the influence of communication structures created by the state and organised along party-political lines must be taken into account; this influence currently manifests itself in the so-called circles of friends" (_Freundeskreise_). The solution was not to prohibit informal meetings — impossible to regulate — but to limit state presence so strictly that, even in coordination, the political members could not impose their will.

**Sixth: the dissenting opinion.** Judge Monika Paulus issued a more radical dissenting opinion (_Sondervotum_). She held that no member of the executive should sit on supervisory councils, as was already the case in most ARD broadcasters managed by the Länder. Allowing their presence, she wrote, "redefines state authority from a threat to an element of diversity". The majority judgment, in her view, only partially applied its own reasoning.

---

## Until 30 June 2015 — The mandated reform

The court gave the Länder a deadline: new regulation in conformity with the Constitution before 30 June 2015. The broadcasting treaties were reformed in 2016.

---

## The real architecture: what the ruling changed and what it did not

The 2014 ruling was celebrated as a victory for the independence of German public media. And in several respects it was: it established clear numerical limits, demanded transparency, and identified the _Freundeskreise_ for the first time as a constitutional problem.

But the ruling did not remove the parties from the system. It merely reduced their formal presence and required that members holding party posts not be counted as "non-state".

The structural problem persists because it does not depend on a law. It depends on an ecosystem of incentives. Political parties continue to control the regional parliaments that approve the broadcasting treaties and set the amount of the licence fee (_Rundfunkbeitrag_). The social groups that appoint the councillors — churches, trade unions, employers' associations — continue to have predictable party affinities. And the _Freundeskreise_ did not disappear: they simply became more discreet.

A member of the _Fernsehrat_ appointed by a cultural association may perfectly well be CDU-affiliated without holding any party post. A DGB trade unionist can vote in line with the SPD without this being recorded in any statute. Formal incompatibilities do not reach as far as regulating informal affinities. And the transparency of minutes — which now exists — allows the public to know what was voted on, but not what was negotiated in the preliminary meetings.

The 2014 ruling forced the parties to be more subtle. It did not force them to withdraw.

## The four pillars of the system: a functional guide

The oversight system of German public broadcasting rests on four figures that it is worth understanding in their mutual relationship.

### The _Rundfunkrat_ and the _Fernsehrat_

These are the broad supervisory councils. The _Rundfunkrat_ exists in each ARD broadcaster (between 30 and 74 members depending on the Land). The _Fernsehrat_ is the equivalent for ZDF (60 members after the 2016 reform). Their formal function: to represent society, approve programming guidelines, supervise their compliance, approve the budget and **elect the _Intendant_** by qualified majority.

Their real function: it is the terrain where the parties compete for editorial control. The members do not receive a salary — they receive attendance fees — but the position confers status, access and influence. The plenary sessions are the visible facade; the real coordination occurs in the _Freundeskreise_.

### The _Verwaltungsrat_

This is the administrative council, smaller (between 7 and 14 members). It oversees the economic management of the _Intendant_, approves his contract, the budget and financial regulations. And — this is key — it must give its approval (_Einvernehmen_) for the appointment of the programme director, the editor-in-chief and the administrative director.

It was precisely this power that enabled the veto of Brender in 2009. The _Fernsehrat_ elects the _Intendant_; the _Verwaltungsrat_ controls editorial appointments. Two locks, both in the hands of the same party-political majority.

### The _Intendant_

This is the director-general. He holds ultimate responsibility for programming, manages the budget — which in the case of ARD and ZDF combined exceeds €9 billion annually (2023) — and represents the broadcaster externally. Formally independent, in practice he must navigate between the demands of the councils, the sensitivities of the parties and the pressure of public opinion.

The _Intendant_ is elected by the _Rundfunkrat_ or the _Fernsehrat_. To be elected, he needs the support of a qualified majority that, before 2014, usually required the approval of both the _roter Kreis_ and the _schwarzer Kreis_. This produced a predictable profile: competent, moderate managers, acceptable to both blocs. Rarely journalists with an uncomfortable profile.

---

## Conclusion: the state within the state

The German system of public media oversight is a masterpiece of institutional engineering. It solves several problems simultaneously: it maintains a flawless facade of independence, channels the distribution of influence between the major parties, and generates the appearance that "society" — not the state — controls public broadcasting.

But the facade cracks under scrutiny. The councils do not represent German society — they represent the organised groups with sufficient power to obtain a seat. The associations that appoint councillors are, in many cases, extensions of the parties or their structural allies. The _Freundeskreise_ coordinated — and to a large extent continue to coordinate — votes that should be individual and independent. And the transparency introduced in 2016 reveals the results but not the negotiations.

German public broadcasting produces high-quality journalism — far superior to that of most European public broadcasters. But it produces it _despite_ its governance structure, not because of it. Real independence does not come from the councils: it comes from the pressure of public opinion, from competition between media, and from the fact that, in a federal system with multiple broadcasters, no party can control all of them simultaneously.

The Rundfunkrat is, in essence, a parliament without voters. Its members are not elected by citizens but appointed by interest groups. Its decisions — including the election of the _Intendant_ and the approval of senior editorial positions — affect what 83 million people watch on television every day. And yet, the average citizen does not know who sits on their _Rundfunkrat_ or how they vote.

The architecture is not unique to broadcasting. The Kuratorium of the Krupp-Stiftung (text 2201), the Stiftungsrat of the FAZ (text 2203), the Fernsehrat of ZDF and the Aufsichtsrat under Mitbestimmung (text 2206) are structurally identical: appointed, not elected, councils; "representative" of social forces; informally coordinated through party-political channels; without electoral accountability. The German power elite operates with the same governance architecture across all sectors.

The 2014 ruling set limits. But it did not change the nature of the system. Because the nature of the system is not a design flaw: it is the design itself.
