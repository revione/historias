# KKR in German Media — The Fund That Played on Two Boards

---

## 1976 — Kohlberg Kravis Roberts: Three Bear Stearns Bankers Go Independent

Jerome Kohlberg, Henry Kravis, and George Roberts worked together in Bear Stearns's corporate finance department. There they developed an idea that would transform global finance: the *leveraged buyout* (LBO). In 1976 they founded KKR. They were not the inventors of the LBO — the technique had existed since the 1950s — but they were the first to systematize it and apply it on an industrial scale.

---

## 1988 — RJR Nabisco: The LBO That Defined an Era

On October 28, 1988, KKR offered $109 per share for RJR Nabisco: $31.1 billion in total, including assumed debt. It was the largest LBO in history up to that point. KKR and its partners put up just $1.5 billion of their own equity. The rest was debt. The operation inspired the book *Barbarians at the Gate: The Fall of RJR Nabisco* (1990).

---

## 2002 — The Collapse of the Kirch Empire Leaves ProSiebenSat.1 as a Banking Inheritance

Leo Kirch had built the largest audiovisual rights conglomerate in Europe. On April 8, 2002, KirchMedia filed for insolvency with debts exceeding €6.5 billion. The creditor banks became forced owners of ProSiebenSat.1 Media AG.

---

## 2003 — Haim Saban Buys ProSiebenSat.1

In 2003, the creditor banks sold ProSiebenSat.1 to a consortium led by Haim Saban for approximately €525 million.

---

## 2007 — KKR and Permira Buy ProSiebenSat.1

On April 3, 2007, KKR and Permira announced the joint acquisition of ProSiebenSat.1 for approximately €3.1 billion. The KEK did not intervene. It could not. The media concentration law evaluates who controls what *at the same time*. KKR did not control any other German media in 2007. Formally, concentration had not increased.

---

## 2008–2010 — The Financial Crisis Delays the Exit

The 2008 financial crisis delayed KKR and Permira's exit plans. KKR responded with more restructuring: headcount reduction, consolidation of production studios, renegotiation of supplier contracts.

---

## 2013–2015 — KKR Exits ProSiebenSat.1

Starting in 2013, KKR and Permira began their exit through accelerated bookbuildings. By 2015, both funds had reduced their stakes to residual levels. The operation was extraordinarily profitable.

---

## 2019 — KKR Negotiates with Friede Springer and Mathias Döpfner

In May 2019, the *Financial Times* revealed that KKR was in advanced talks with Axel Springer SE. Friede Springer and Döpfner were seeking a financial partner who would bring capital, accept a minority position, and not interfere in the editorial line.

---

## 2020 — The Deal: KKR Takes 44.9% of Axel Springer

The deal closed in March 2020. KKR took 44.9% of the capital for approximately €3 billion. 44.9% is not a random figure. Under German takeover law (*WpÜG*), crossing the 30% threshold triggers a mandatory public offer for 100%. KKR did not want to buy 100%.

---

## The Trick: Why the KEK Could Do Nothing

Here is the regulatory loophole: the KEK evaluates media concentration in each specific operation at the time it occurs. If an investor buys a television group, the KEK looks at what other media they own *at that moment*. What they owned *before* — and what they might own *after* — does not enter the equation.

**Phase 1 (2007–2015):** KKR controls ProSiebenSat.1. No press media. KEK sees no concentration problems.
**Phase 2 (2015–2019):** KKR sells ProSiebenSat.1. KEK has nothing to evaluate.
**Phase 3 (2020–present):** KKR acquires 44.9% of Axel Springer. KEK sees no horizontal concentration.

The net result: a single fund has exercised control over Germany's second-largest television group and its largest newspaper group. Not at the same time, but within a span of thirteen years. Economists call this "sequential concentration." The KEK has no tools to address it.

---

## The KEK: A Watchdog with One Eye

The KEK is a collegial body of six independent experts. But its powers are those granted by the *Medienstaatsvertrag* (Interstate Media Treaty), and that treaty was negotiated by the Länder in a context — the 1990s — where the problem to solve was simultaneous horizontal concentration. The 1997 legislator did not imagine — or did not want to imagine — the problem of sequential concentration by financial investors.

---

## What This Pattern Means for German Media Concentration

The German system of media concentration control was built for a world of stable owner-publishers. But 21st-century capital does not work that way. Private equity funds do not want to own media forever. They want to buy, restructure, extract value, and sell. In that cycle, they can pass through several sectors of the information ecosystem without ever triggering simultaneous concentration thresholds.

---

## What Will Happen When KKR Wants to Exit Springer?

The typical investment horizon of a private equity fund is five to seven years. KKR entered Springer in 2020. We are in 2026. The options: IPO, sale to another financial investor, or sale to a strategic buyer. In any of the three scenarios, the period of KKR in the German press will have lasted between six and ten years. And German law will not have registered any anomaly. Because for German law, an investor who is no longer there is an investor who was never there.
