# German Television — ARD, ZDF, RTL and ProSieben

---

## 1945 — Allied occupation and the broadcasting vacuum

When the Third Reich collapsed in May 1945, its media system also collapsed. The Reichs-Rundfunk-Gesellschaft (RRG), which since 1926 had centralized all German stations under state and later Nazi control, fell silent. The Allies —the United States, the United Kingdom, France, and the Soviet Union— found a country without independent media infrastructure, with a population that had spent twelve years receiving exclusively Goebbels propaganda.

The occupying powers applied different models in each zone:

- **British zone**: created the Nordwestdeutscher Rundfunk (NWDR) in Hamburg in 1945, following the BBC model —public corporation, license-fee funded, independent of government—. They appointed Hugh Carleton Greene, a British journalist who had been a correspondent in Berlin before the war, as its first director. Greene imposed a federal structure with broadcasting councils that included representatives from political parties, churches, unions, and civic associations, precisely to prevent the state from recapturing the medium. This model of broadcasting councils, imposed by Greene to prevent state propaganda, was the direct mold from which the ZDF Fernsehrat emerged — the same body that decades later the SPD and CDU would colonize through the Freundeskreise.

- **American zone**: founded Radio München (predecessor of Bayerischer Rundfunk), Radio Stuttgart (SDR), Radio Frankfurt (HR), and Radio Bremen. The American model also favored decentralization, but with greater emphasis on commercial independence —though they never managed to implement a private system in their zone—.

- **French zone**: established the Südwestfunk (SWF) in Baden-Baden, smaller and with fewer resources, following the centralized French model but adapted to the German context.

- **Soviet zone**: created stations under the control of the Socialist Unity Party (SED), which after 1949 would become the Deutscher Fernsehfunk (DFF), the GDR's television service.

The decision to decentralize was deliberate: fragmenting the broadcasting spectrum among multiple *Länder* prevented a future federal government from rebuilding a propaganda monopoly like that of the RRG. The same Allied occupation that designed decentralized broadcasting to prevent state propaganda determined, zone by zone, which aristocratic properties were confiscated and which were preserved: in the Soviet zone, both radio frequencies and Hohenzollern properties were expropriated without compensation; in the Western zones, both broadcasting and noble restitution were negotiated at the Land level (texts 2202, 2205). The shared origin in occupation policy is never traced.

## 1948–1950 — Foundation of the ARD

On June 9, 1950, six regional broadcasters founded in Bremen the **Arbeitsgemeinschaft der öffentlich-rechtlichen Rundfunkanstalten der Bundesrepublik Deutschland** (ARD): Bayerischer Rundfunk (BR), Hessischer Rundfunk (HR), Radio Bremen (RB), Süddeutscher Rundfunk (SDR), Südwestfunk (SWF), and Nordwestdeutscher Rundfunk (NWDR). The Sender Freies Berlin (SFB) would join in 1954. None of them depended on the federal government: each was an *Anstalt des öffentlichen Rechts* (public-law corporation) created by the law of the corresponding Land. The federal government had —and has— no jurisdiction over broadcasting.

This architecture was not accidental. It arose from the Basic Law of 1949 (Grundgesetz), which in Articles 5 and 30 reserved *Kulturhoheit* (cultural sovereignty) to the Länder. The Allies, especially the British, had insisted that broadcasting should be the responsibility of the states and not the central government. Konrad Adenauer tried to reverse this structure almost immediately.

On December 25, 1952, the NWDR broadcast the first post-war German television transmission from Hamburg.

---

## 1953–1954 — The Rundfunkgebühr is born

In 1953, the obligation to pay a fee for each radio set was introduced. In 1954 it was extended to television sets. The fee was not a tax —it was collected directly by the ARD's contribution service (the Gebühreneinzugszentrale, GEZ)— and was justified by the need to finance programming independent of the state and advertising.

The principle was simple: every household with a receiver had to pay. The GEZ, created in 1976, would be responsible for collection and enforcement. The monthly amount in 1954 was 5 German marks per television set (approximately €13 today adjusted for inflation).

---

## 1961 — The first Constitutional ruling (BVerfGE 12, 205)

In 1960, Chancellor Adenauer founded **Deutschland-Fernsehen GmbH**, a private-law company controlled by the federal government, with the intention of creating a second television channel. It was the same chancellor who a decade earlier had personally pressed for the release of Alfried Krupp from Landsberg prison. The SPD-governed Länder —Hamburg, Bremen, Hesse, and Lower Saxony— sued before the Federal Constitutional Court.

On February 28, 1961, the Bundesverfassungsgericht issued the so-called *1. Rundfunk-Urteil* (First Broadcasting Ruling), declaring Deutschland-Fernsehen GmbH unconstitutional. The ruling established the principles that govern the system to this day:

1. **Broadcasting is the competence of the Länder**, not the federal government.
2. **Broadcasting must be independent of the state**. Neither the federal government nor the regional governments may control content.
3. **The medium must be organized so that all relevant social forces have a voice** in the supervisory councils (*Binnenpluralismus* or internal pluralism).
4. **The state has the obligation to guarantee a *Grundversorgung*** (basic provision) of information, education, culture, and entertainment to the entire population.

This ruling forced the creation of a second public channel, but managed by the Länder, not by Bonn. That channel would be ZDF. The same chancellor who in 1951 had been forced by the DGB and IG Metall to accept the *Montanmitbestimmung* under threat of a general strike, attempted nine years later to create a state television channel — and was blocked by the Constitutional Court. Two defeats in one decade, the same man, opposite fronts: the unions forced him to share power, the court forced him to let it go.

---

## 1963 — ZDF, the second channel

On April 1, 1963, **Zweites Deutsches Fernsehen** (ZDF) began broadcasting, with its main headquarters in Mainz, Rhineland-Palatinate —deliberately far from the political capital in Bonn and the economic center in Frankfurt—.

Unlike the ARD —a federation of independent regional broadcasters—, ZDF is a single national corporation created by an interstate treaty (*Staatsvertrag*) between all Länder. Its funding was and remains mixed: license fee and advertising (the latter limited to before 8:00 pm and excluded on Sundays and holidays).

The ZDF was structured with a *Fernsehrat* (Television Council) of 77 members representing political parties, churches, unions, business associations, environmental organizations, and other social groups. The idea was exactly the same as in the ARD: that no interest group could dominate programming.

In practice, the two major parties —CDU/CSU and SPD— colonized the councils of both institutions through the *Proporz* (proportional allocation of posts). The directors of the ARD regional broadcasters and ZDF were elected —and are elected— through complex political negotiations among the Länder, whose regional governments indirectly control the appointments.

---

## 1973–1980 — Consolidation of the fee and first commercial pressures

In 1973, the first *Rundfunkgebührenstaatsvertrag* (Interstate Treaty on the Broadcasting Fee) was signed, unifying the collection system for all Länder.

During the 1970s, German press publishers —especially Axel Springer, Bertelsmann, and the group of regional publishers— began to pressure for access to the television spectrum. Axel Springer was the most aggressive lobbyist among them — the same man whose *Bild Zeitung* had become Europe's largest tabloid. They argued that the ARD-ZDF duopoly violated freedom of expression and that technological progress —satellites, cable— made technically obsolete the spectrum scarcity that justified the public monopoly.

In 1980, Helmut Schmidt's Social Democratic government authorized the first cable pilot projects in Ludwigshafen, Munich, Dortmund, and Berlin —but without yet allowing private stations with commercial purposes—.

---

## 1981 — The third ruling: the FRAG-Urteil (BVerfGE 57, 295)

On June 16, 1981, the Federal Constitutional Court issued its third broadcasting ruling, known as the *FRAG-Urteil* (after the Saarland law that had been challenged). The court declared that **private broadcasting is constitutionally admissible**, provided that:

1. The legislator establishes a regulatory framework that guarantees pluralism.
2. There are safeguards against excessive concentration of media power.
3. The public service maintains its *Grundversorgung* function.

The ruling opened the legal door to private television but imposed strict conditions: it was not enough to eliminate the public monopoly; the Länder had to create regulatory authorities (*Landesmedienanstalten*) to oversee licenses, content, and business concentration.

---

## 1984 — German private television is born

On January 1, 1984, the **Kabelpilotprojekt Ludwigshafen** began, the first cable television project in West Germany. That same day, **PKS** (Programmgesellschaft für Kabel- und Satellitenrundfunk) started broadcasting, which in 1985 would be renamed **Sat.1**. Its main investor was Leo Kirch.

On January 2, 1984, **RTL plus** began broadcasting from Luxembourg. The choice of the Grand Duchy was not trivial: by broadcasting from abroad, RTL circumvented German regulation. The Luxembourg license allowed it to program without the content restrictions that the *Landesmedienanstalten* imposed on Sat.1. The signal reached Germany via cable. RTL's main partner was the Luxembourg group CLT (Compagnie Luxembourgeoise de Télédiffusion), but soon **Bertelsmann** —the publishing giant from Gütersloh— acquired a substantial stake.

The architecture of the new dual system was thus outlined:

- **Public sector**: ARD (federation of nine —later more— regional broadcasters) and ZDF (national channel), funded by license fee and limited advertising.
- **Private sector**: commercial channels funded exclusively by advertising, regulated by each Land's media authorities.
- **Regulation**: each Land has its own *Landesmedienanstalt*; decisions on national licenses are coordinated through the conference of directors (Direktorenkonferenz der Landesmedienanstalten, later ZAK).

---

## 1986–1987 — The fourth and fifth Constitutional rulings (BVerfGE 73, 118 and BVerfGE 74, 297)

On November 4, 1986, the fourth *Rundfunk-Urteil* (Lower Saxony case) confirmed the constitutionality of the dual system —public and private— provided that the public service guaranteed *Grundversorgung* for the entire population. The ruling introduced a crucial distinction: public service must offer a full program (*Vollprogramm*) covering information, education, culture, and entertainment, while private stations may specialize.

In 1987, the fifth ruling (Baden-Württemberg case) specified the pluralism requirements for private stations: limits on ownership concentration, the obligation to include *Fensterprogramme* (regional programming windows), and a ban on a single company controlling more than 30% of the television advertising market.

---

## 1989 — ProSieben and the rise of the Kirch empire

On January 1, 1989, **ProSieben** began broadcasting from Unterföhring, near Munich. It was the third major private channel, after RTL and Sat.1. Its founder, **Thomas Kirch**, was the son of **Leo Kirch**, a Bavarian businessman who since the 1960s had been accumulating distribution rights for international films and series.

Leo Kirch (1926–2011) had studied under Theodor W. Adorno and worked as an academic assistant before founding his own content distributor, Beta Film, in 1959. His business model was simple: buy massive packages of broadcasting rights for Hollywood films and European studios, and then resell them to German television stations. By the 1980s, Kirch controlled the largest rights catalog in Europe —some 15,000 films and 50,000 hours of series—.

When private television was legalized in 1984, Kirch was already a nearly unavoidable content supplier for any new channel. He used that position to become a shareholder of Sat.1 (controlling 43% through PKS) and to found ProSieben with his son. By the early 1990s, the **Kirch-Gruppe** was Germany's largest media conglomerate, with interests in free-to-air television, pay television, sports rights, and film production.

---

## 1990 — Reunification and the end of the DFF

On October 3, 1990, German reunification incorporated five new Länder and East Berlin into the Western broadcasting system. The **Deutscher Fernsehfunk** (DFF), the GDR's state television, ceased broadcasting on December 31, 1991.

The ARD expanded its structure: the ORB (Ostdeutscher Rundfunk Brandenburg) was created in 1991, and the MDR (Mitteldeutscher Rundfunk) —covering Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia— in 1992. The NDR absorbed Mecklenburg-Vorpommern. West Berlin's SFB expanded to the entire city.

The ZDF opened regional studios in each new Land. The license fee was extended to citizens of the former GDR, who had to pay the same rate as those in the West from 1991 —causing considerable unease among a population with significantly lower incomes—.

Reunification also meant an enormous audience injection for private channels: RTL and Sat.1, which were already broadcasting via satellite, instantly reached millions of new Eastern households, where there was no tradition of independent public service broadcasting.

---

## 1996–2001 — RTL, Bertelsmann, and the era of entertainment

By the mid-1990s, RTL had consolidated its position as Germany's most-watched channel. Its formula —tabloid-format news (*RTL aktuell*), daytime talk shows (*Hans Meiser*, *Ilona Christen*), German fiction (*Gute Zeiten, schlechte Zeiten*, *Alarm für Cobra 11*), and sports rights— consistently attracted more viewers than ARD and ZDF among 14- to 49-year-olds, the demographic most interesting to advertisers.

RTL's ownership reflected the transnational structure of the European audiovisual sector:

- **Bertelsmann AG**, based in Gütersloh, controlled the group through RTL Group, a Luxembourg-listed company that also grouped channels in France (M6), the Netherlands (RTL Nederland), Belgium (RTL-TVI), Hungary (RTL Klub), and other countries. The family controlling RTL continues to operate from Gütersloh, the same town where Carl Bertelsmann founded his hymnal printing shop in 1835.
- Bertelsmann held approximately 76% of RTL Group; the rest was listed on the Luxembourg and Brussels stock exchanges.
- The Mohn family, ultimate owner of Bertelsmann through the Bertelsmann Stiftung (which controls 77.6% of the group's shares), exercised indirect control over Europe's most commercial television channel. Through the Bertelsmann Stiftung, the Mohn family operates in the same social democratic policy ecosystem as the DGB unions represented on the ZDF Fernsehrat —a fabric of foundations, think tanks, and Freundeskreise where the German media, union, and political elites overlap—.

RTL had been profitable since 1986. In 2001, RTL Group posted revenues of over 4 billion euros.

---

## 1996–2002 — Leo Kirch, from king of rights to bankruptcy

While RTL prospered, Leo Kirch made the mistake that would destroy his empire.

In 1996, Kirch launched **DF1** (Digitales Fernsehen 1), Germany's first digital pay television service, using the Astra satellite. The investment was massive —billions of marks in infrastructure, decoders, and premium content rights—. To secure exclusive content, Kirch paid record sums for the Bundesliga and Formula 1 rights.

In 1999, DF1 merged with Premiere, the other pay-TV operator, creating **Premiere World**. Kirch controlled the new platform. The plan was to create a "German Sky": mass pay television with football, films, and series as the locomotive. But Germans, accustomed to an abundant free offering (over 30 free-to-air channels), massively resisted paying. Premiere World accumulated annual losses exceeding 1 billion euros.

In parallel, Kirch had acquired in 1996 the broadcasting rights for the 2002 and 2006 World Cups for approximately 1.7 billion euros. Payments were guaranteed through a complex network of bank loans.

On April 8, 2002, **KirchMedia GmbH & Co. KGaA** —the holding company that grouped the free-to-air television businesses (Sat.1, ProSieben, Kabel 1, N24), the film rights, and the sports rights— filed for insolvency. It was the largest corporate insolvency in the history of the Federal Republic since 1945. The accumulated debt exceeded 6.5 billion euros.

The creditor banks —led by Bayerische Landesbank, HypoVereinsbank, Deutsche Bank, and Commerzbank— suddenly found themselves as forced owners of a media conglomerate they did not know how to manage. The mechanism was identical to the rescue of Krupp by the Preußische Seehandlung in 1873 (text 2201): banks with state ties absorbing a strategically vital company that would later be re-privatized.

---

## 2002–2007 — ProSiebenSat.1 changes hands

Following KirchMedia's bankruptcy, the creditor banks took control of ProSiebenSat.1 Media AG —the company that grouped the free-to-air channels ProSieben, Sat.1, Kabel 1, and N24—.

In 2003, a consortium led by **Haim Saban** (the Israeli-American producer and creator of the Power Rangers) acquired ProSiebenSat.1 for approximately 525 million euros. Saban bet on repositioning the channels with new formats and recovering advertising share. The result was mixed: audience numbers stabilized but never regained the levels of the late 1990s.

In 2007, Saban sold his stake to the private equity funds **KKR** (Kohlberg Kravis Roberts) and **Permira** for approximately 3.1 billion euros. The funds cut costs, outsourced production, and squeezed advertising profitability. KKR maintained control of ProSiebenSat.1 until 2015; four years later, in 2019, the same KKR would enter as co-owner of Axel Springer, closing a circuit between Germany's largest press group and its second-largest private television group. The operation was heavily criticized: the new owners loaded the company with debt to finance dividends, leaving it financially weakened.

---

## 2013 — The Rundfunkbeitrag replaces the Rundfunkgebühr

On January 1, 2013, the most profound reform of the public funding system since 1954 came into effect. The **Rundfunkbeitrag** (broadcasting contribution) replaced the old *Rundfunkgebühr* (device-based fee).

The difference was substantial:

- **Before** (Rundfunkgebühr): each household paid for each radio or television receiver it owned. The GEZ sent inspectors to verify whether citizens had correctly declared their devices.
- **Now** (Rundfunkbeitrag): each dwelling pays a fixed amount, regardless of how many devices it has, or even if it has none at all. The justification is that public service broadcasting is available to all and benefits the whole of society, not just those who consume it.

The amount in 2025 was 18.36 euros per month per dwelling. Households with disabled persons, welfare recipients, and certain other groups may apply for exemption. Businesses and self-employed persons pay additional graduated rates based on the number of employees and vehicles.

The reform was driven by the Federal Constitutional Court in its 2007 ruling (BVerfGE 119, 181), which demanded a fairer and less invasive system. It also responded to the technological reality: when the device-based fee was designed in the 1950s, television was watched on a living-room set; by 2013 it was watched on phones, tablets, computers, and any screen-equipped device. It was impossible to maintain device-based enforcement.

The revenue of the Beitragsservice (successor to the GEZ) amounted in 2024 to approximately 8.5 billion euros annually, distributed among ARD, ZDF, Deutschlandradio, and the Landesmedienanstalten.

---

## 2013–2018 — Ownership reorganization at ProSiebenSat.1 and RTL

Between 2013 and 2015, KKR and Permira progressively reduced their stake in ProSiebenSat.1 through stock market placements. The company went into free float, without a clear controlling shareholder. This dispersion left it vulnerable precisely to the type of investor that would appear years later.

Meanwhile, RTL Group —controlled by Bertelsmann— continued expanding its digital and content business. In 2015, Bertelsmann announced its intention to increase its stake in RTL Group from 75.1% to over 77%, consolidating the Mohn family's control over Europe's largest commercial television group.

---

## 2018 — Comcast buys Sky; Sky Deutschland born under US ownership

In October 2018, **Comcast Corporation**, the US cable and entertainment giant (owner of NBCUniversal), acquired **Sky plc** for approximately 39 billion dollars, outbidding a competing offer from 21st Century Fox / Disney.

Sky Deutschland, the Bavarian subsidiary based in Unterföhring, thus passed into Comcast's hands. Sky Deutschland operated pay television via satellite and cable, with exclusive rights to the Bundesliga (Friday, Saturday, and Sunday), Formula 1, the Premier League, and a broad offering of films and series. It had approximately 5 million direct subscribers.

The acquisition marked the entry of a US technology giant into the German television market, a market that had historically been dominated by local and European players.

---

## 2019–2024 — Mediaset-MFE escalates its position in ProSiebenSat.1

Starting in 2019, the Italian group **Mediaset** (renamed **MFE — MediaForEurope N.V.** in 2021, controlled by the Berlusconi family through Fininvest) began acquiring stakes in ProSiebenSat.1 Media SE progressively and without making much noise.

The chronology of the escalation:

- **2019**: Mediaset announces a first stake of 9.6% in ProSiebenSat.1, valued at around 340 million euros. The operation is presented as a "strategic investment" to create a pan-European alliance of commercial television companies.
- **2020**: MFE exceeds 20% of the capital. ProSiebenSat.1's management —then led by CEO Rainer Beaujean— publicly rejects MFE's ambitions, arguing that a forced merger would destroy value for minority shareholders.
- **2022**: MFE reaches 25% of voting rights. German securities law (*Wertpapierhandelsgesetz*) requires the notification of significant holdings but imposes no automatic limit on share accumulation.
- **2023**: MFE hovers around 29.9% of ProSiebenSat.1. Under German takeover law (*Wertpapiererwerbs- und Übernahmegesetz*), crossing the 30% threshold triggers a mandatory public takeover offer for 100% of the company. MFE deliberately stops just below that threshold.

In parallel, MFE pressed for ProSiebenSat.1 to divest its non-media businesses —e-commerce (flaconi), dating platforms (Parship, ElitePartner), price comparison sites (Verivox)— and focus exclusively on television and streaming. In 2023, ProSiebenSat.1 partially yielded and announced the sale or spin-off of these businesses. Pressure from MFE and other activist shareholders forced in 2024 the departure of the supervisory board chairman, Swiss national Andreas Wiele, and CEO Beaujean, replaced by profiles closer to European integration.

MFE did not hide its ultimate goal: to create a "European champion" of commercial television to compete with Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, and YouTube for continental advertising investment.

---

## 2023 — RTL Group: Bertelsmann and the German unit

At the end of 2023, RTL Deutschland —the German subsidiary of RTL Group— operated the channels RTL Television, VOX, RTL II (minority stake), ntv, Super RTL, Nitro, and RTLup, as well as the streaming platform **RTL+**. RTL Deutschland accounted for approximately 35% of RTL Group's total revenues.

Bertelsmann, controlled by the Mohn family through the Bertelsmann Stiftung and the family foundation Bertelsmann Verwaltungsgesellschaft (BVG), held 76.28% of RTL Group's shares. The remaining 23.72% was in free float.

RTL Deutschland's strategy in 2023–2025 focused on the streaming transition: RTL+ already offered exclusive content, live sports (NFL, UEFA Europa League), and a library of series and films competing directly with Netflix and Prime Video in the German market. Linear television —RTL, VOX— continued to generate the bulk of advertising revenues, but linear viewership was declining at a rate of 3–5% annually among viewers under 50.

---

## 2024 — ARD and ZDF at the digital crossroads

In 2024, the two public corporations faced several fronts:

**Funding**: Although the 18.36 euros per month per household seemed stable, the number of paying households decreases each year for demographic reasons and due to the increase in exemptions. The KEF (Kommission zur Ermittlung des Finanzbedarfs der Rundfunkanstalten) —the independent commission that calculates the financial needs of the broadcasters— recommended in its 23rd report (2024) an increase to 18.94 euros monthly for 2025. Several Länder —especially Saxony-Anhalt, governed by the CDU and AfD— blocked or threatened to block the increase, in an unprecedented challenge to the system established by the interstate treaties.

**Legitimacy**: Parties such as the AfD (Alternative for Germany) campaigned openly against the Rundfunkbeitrag, calling it a "compulsory tax." Infratest dimap polls from 2023 showed that nearly 40% of Germans considered the fee "too high" or "unfair."

**Digitalization**: The ARD launched its own platform, **ARD Mediathek**, with on-demand content and live streams. The ZDF did the same with the **ZDFmediathek**. Both collaborate on **funk**, a platform for digital content for young audiences (14–29 years) distributed exclusively on YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, and other social networks. But the digital transition is slow: the production budget for digital platforms represents less than 5% of the total spending of both corporations, which continue to devote the bulk of their resources to linear television.

**Governance**: In 2024–2025, several scandals erupted at the ARD. The rbb (Rundfunk Berlin-Brandenburg) was investigated for embezzlement and nepotism; its director, Patricia Schlesinger, resigned in 2022. The NDR and WDR were also criticized for excessive spending, disproportionate allowances, and irregular contracts with star presenters. These scandals provided ammunition to those advocating for reducing or abolishing the fee.

## 2025 — Sky Deutschland abandons linear television

In April 2025, Sky Deutschland announced one of the most significant turns in recent German audiovisual history: **the cessation of its linear pay-TV channels** (Sky Atlantic, Sky Cinema, Sky Sport) to concentrate exclusively on streaming through its platform **WOW** (formerly Sky Ticket) and access via satellite and cable through IP.

Comcast's decision responded to an inescapable reality: Sky Deutschland's subscribers —mostly satellite customers with decoders— were migrating to streaming platforms such as Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, and Disney+. While streaming was growing at double-digit annual rates in Germany, linear pay television was losing subscribers by the hundreds of thousands each year.

Sky Deutschland's move confirmed what audience data had already been showing: linear television —both public and private— is in structural decline among generations under 50. In 2024, according to the annual report of the Landesmedienanstalten, viewers aged 14 to 29 were already spending more time on YouTube, TikTok, and streaming platforms than on conventional television combined.

---

## 2026 — The present of the dual system

In mid-2026, the German television system finds itself at a crossroads:

- **ARD and ZDF** defend their constitutional mission of *Grundversorgung* and their funding through the Rundfunkbeitrag, but the political pressure to limit or abolish the fee grows with each legislative term. The criticisms focus on governance (*Staatsferne* or distance from the state, which in practice is not fulfilled because the parties control the councils), the salaries of executives, and the slowness of the digital transformation.

- **RTL Deutschland** remains by audience the strongest group in the commercial market, but its linear-television-based model is threatened by the migration to streaming. Bertelsmann must decide whether to invest billions in competing with Netflix —a battle no European television company has won— or retreat to a niche of local German-language content.

- **ProSiebenSat.1** is in an *impasse*: MFE holds 29.9% and wants full control to merge the group with MediaForEurope, but minority shareholders and German regulators may resist. The KEK (Kommission zur Ermittlung der Konzentration im Medienbereich) must assess whether an Italian-German merger would breach concentration limits. German law prohibits a single operator from simultaneously controlling a dominant position in television and the press. MFE has no interests in the German press, but the concentration in the television advertising market would be significant.

- **Sky Deutschland**, now under Comcast's leadership, has bet everything on streaming with WOW, abandoning linear television. It is a risky bet: WOW competes with Netflix, Prime Video, Disney+, RTL+, and Joyn (from ProSiebenSat.1 and Discovery) in an increasingly saturated subscription market.

- **Netflix, Amazon, and YouTube**: According to the Digitalisierungsbericht 2025 of the Landesmedienanstalten, global streaming platforms already captured approximately 40% of the total audiovisual consumption time of Germans under 40. YouTube's advertising revenues in Germany were estimated at over 1.5 billion euros annually, directly competing with RTL and ProSiebenSat.1 for the same advertising pie.

## The funding model in detail

### How does the Rundfunkbeitrag work?

The system is governed by the *Rundfunkbeitragsstaatsvertrag* of 2011 (in force since 2013), a treaty among the 16 Länder that has the force of law throughout the federal territory. The key points:

- **Subject liable**: each dwelling (not each person, not each device). One dwelling = one payment.
- **Current amount** (2025): €18.36/month per dwelling.
- **Exemptions**: persons receiving welfare benefits (Bürgergeld, Sozialhilfe, Grundsicherung, BAföG, etc.), persons with severe visual or hearing disabilities, and certain other cases.
- **Collecting body**: the Beitragsservice von ARD, ZDF und Deutschlandradio, based in Cologne. It is not a state agency but a common service of the public corporations.
- **Distribution**: approximately 70% to the ARD, 25% to ZDF, and 5% to Deutschlandradio. The Landesmedienanstalten receive a small additional fraction (around 2% of the total) for their operation.
- **Periodic adjustment**: every four years, the KEF (Kommission zur Ermittlung des Finanzbedarfs der Rundfunkanstalten), an independent body of 16 experts, assesses the broadcasters' financial needs and recommends the necessary adjustment to parliament. The Länder must approve it unanimously in the Rundfunkkommission.

Total revenue in 2024 was 8.511 billion euros, according to the Beitragsservice's Jahresbericht.
