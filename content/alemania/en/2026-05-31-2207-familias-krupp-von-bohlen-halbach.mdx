# The Krupp and von Bohlen und Halbach Families — Steel, Blood and Power

---

## 1587 — Arndt Krupp arrives in Essen

The Krupp family first appeared in the historical record when Arndt Krupp joined the merchants' guild in Essen. He arrived just before an outbreak of the Black Death and became one of the wealthiest men in the city with a strategy as cold as it was effective: buying the properties of fleeing families at bargain prices.

Arndt ran a business in wine, groceries, real estate and loans. He married his children into the wealthiest families of Essen. The marriage of his eldest son, Anton, to the daughter of a gunsmith involved a Krupp in weapons manufacturing for the first time, during the Thirty Years' War.

---

## 17th–18th Centuries — Between trade and municipal politics

After the Thirty Years' War, the Krupp heirs withdrew into public offices as municipal secretaries of Essen. Others continued as small merchants. Nearly two centuries passed without the name being associated again with heavy industry. Until a woman appeared.

### Amalie Krupp: the true founder

Helene Amalie Krupp (1732–1810), born Ascherfeld, married Friedrich Jodocus Krupp, who died in 1757, leaving her a widow at twenty-five with a colonial goods shop. In the following decades, Amalie did not merely keep the business running: she built a diversified commercial empire — trade, manufacturing, real estate — that made her one of the wealthiest women in the Ruhr.

In 1799 she bought the *Hütte zur guten Hoffnung* (Good Hope Foundry), a bankrupt foundry, at a bargain price. In 1807 she gave it to her grandson Friedrich as an engagement gift. But Friedrich proved to be a terrible manager: within a year Amalie reversed the gift and sold the foundry at a substantial profit to the brothers Haniel and Huyssen, who would turn it into the Gutehoffnungshütte, one of the largest employers in the region.

Amalie died in 1810. Her inheritance — cash, properties, the trading business — was the seed capital with which Friedrich Krupp would found the steelworks a year later. Without Amalie, there is no Krupp.

---

## 1810 — Henry Bohlen is born in Bremen

While the industrial branch of the Krupps was just beginning to germinate in Essen, on the other side of the Atlantic the most colorful figure in the family that would decades later join them was being born.

Henry Bohlen was born in Bremen in 1810. He emigrated to the United States and became a brigadier general in the Union Army during the American Civil War — the first foreign-born general in that army. He had previously fought in the Mexican-American War and in the Crimean War on the French side. He died in combat along the Rappahannock River in 1862. His great-nephew would be Charles Bohlen, US diplomat and expert on the Soviet Union between 1929 and 1969.

---

## 1811 — Friedrich Krupp founds the first steelworks

To understand why a merchant from Essen could found an industry in 1811, one must look at what had happened in Europe in the previous twenty years. The French Revolution (1789) and the Napoleonic Wars had demolished the old feudal order. Noble titles no longer guaranteed power — the aristocrats who survived the guillotine and Napoleon's campaigns could no longer sustain themselves on land rents alone. The wars had broken the monarchies. Power no longer emanated exclusively from the king. In that vacuum, a new source of legitimacy emerged: industrial capital.

Napoleon imposed the Continental Blockade in 1806, banning European territories from trading with Great Britain. For the German states, that meant one thing: no English steel was arriving. The metal that sustained the British Industrial Revolution was suddenly inaccessible. If someone could produce steel locally, they had a guaranteed captive market.

Friedrich Krupp (1787–1826) was not an aristocrat. He had no title, no land, no prince's protection. But he had his grandmother Amalie's inheritance. On 20 November 1811, with that capital, he founded the *Firma Friedrich Krupp zur Verfertigung des Englischen Gussstahls und aller daraus resultierenden Fabrikationen*. His two partners were the brothers Georg Karl Gottfried and Wilhelm Georg Ludwig von Kechel, who already had experience in manufacturing cast steel.

He invested 30,000 Reichstaler — all from the family fortune — in building a foundry alongside the Berne stream. The site was a disaster: poorly connected, with insufficient water flow to power the forging hammers. By 1813 the factory was finished but barely generating revenue. The von Kechels proved to be terrible partners and Friedrich spent a fortune on lawyers to separate from them; in 1816 he became sole proprietor. Just when he finally managed to produce quality steel, the war ended, the blockade was lifted, and English steel — cheaper and better — once again flooded the continent.

Friedrich spent the next ten years trying to make the business work. His largest customer was the Prussian Mint in Düsseldorf, which bought dies and rollers for minting coins — although they returned nine out of every fourteen pieces for quality defects. In total he spent more than 200,000 Taler (equivalent to about €4 million today) on solving the metallurgical puzzle of cast steel. He succeeded in 1823, but too late.

In 1824 he lost the family home to debt. He moved with his wife Therese and their four children into the foreman's shack next to the factory. He died on 8 October 1826, at age 39, of pulmonary edema. He left a company with four employees, 10,000 Taler in debt, and no major customers. His widow Therese did not declare bankruptcy. She kept the business going with the help of relatives while her son Alfred, fourteen years old, learned the trade.

---

## 1828 — Alfred Krupp inherits the forge at fourteen

Alfred Krupp (1812–1887) was born in Essen. In 1828, at barely fourteen years old, he had to take charge of his father's forge, which then had seven employees and owed 10,000 Taler. His mother Therese kept the company afloat with the help of relatives while Alfred learned the trade. In 1848 he bought the factory from his mother for 40,000 Taler and became sole proprietor.

---

## 1831 — Gustav Georg Friedrich Bohlen-Halbach is born in Philadelphia

On 27 April 1831, Gustav Georg Friedrich Bohlen-Halbach was born in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Despite being born on American soil, he would become a German diplomat and master of ceremonies at the court of the Grand Duchy of Baden. He married Sophie Bohlen, daughter of General Henry Bohlen. This marriage united the American branch of the family with the German one, and from that union would come the man who decades later became Gustav Krupp.

---

## 1847 — The first steel cannon

Until 1846 the company survived by producing tools, coins, railway rollers and cutlery. But Alfred had been experimenting for years with something more ambitious: manufacturing a cast steel cannon, something that had never been done before. In 1847 he presented the first single-piece steel cannon to the Prussian Ministry of War.

The military rejected it. They had been using bronze cannons for centuries and did not trust a material they considered brittle. The official report basically said: thank you but no thank you, steel is not suitable for cannons. Krupp kept the prototype and continued pushing.

---

## 1851 — The Great London Exhibition

At the first World's Fair in London, Alfred Krupp presented a cast steel block of 43 hundredweight (more than two tonnes) — something no other manufacturer could produce — and a steel cannon that left observers astonished. He won the exhibition medal. The international press began calling him "The Cannon King." But military orders still did not come: the world still preferred bronze.

---

## 1859 — The order that changed everything

Twelve years after the initial rejection, the Prussian Ministry of War finally purchased **more than 300 steel cannons** from Krupp. The technical difference was by then impossible to ignore: Krupp's steel cannons used a breech-loading system far superior to muzzle-loading bronze cannons, and their range and accuracy were unmatched. From this order onwards, Krupp began selling artillery to almost every European state. In a few years, the company went from making train wheels to being the largest weapons manufacturer on the continent.

---

## 1864 — The Second Schleswig War

The first baptism of fire for Krupp cannons came in the war between Prussia and Denmark over the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein. The superiority of the Prussian steel cannons over the Danish bronze ones was so evident that the Prussian victory was attributed in part to Krupp's artillery. For the first time, a European conflict was decided with weapons manufactured in Essen.

---

## 1866 — Selling to both sides

In the Austro-Prussian War, something happened that illustrates Alfred Krupp's character better than any anecdote: **both armies** — the Prussian and the Austrian — entered the battlefield armed with cannons manufactured by the same company. Krupp had sold artillery to Prussia and Austria simultaneously. There was no moral dilemma: there was business. And as long as the cannon worked, the company delivered.

---

## 1870 — The Franco-Prussian War: the decisive moment

In July 1870 war broke out between France and Prussia. General Helmuth von Moltke, chief of the Prussian General Staff, deliberately deployed Krupp artillery as a central tactical advantage. Prussian steel cannons doubled the range of French bronze cannons — they could fire from distances where French artillery simply could not reach. The difference was devastating. At the Battle of Sedan (September 1870), Krupp's artillery was a decisive factor in the capture of Emperor Napoleon III and 100,000 French soldiers. In January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles — where Wilhelm I was proclaimed emperor — the artillery surrounding Paris bore the seal of Krupp's three rings.

---

## 1871 — Supplier to the Empire

With the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles on 18 January 1871, Alfred Krupp went from being a regional weapons manufacturer to becoming the quasi-official supplier of the new European power. There was no formal decree naming him "official supplier" — none was needed. After Sedan, after Krupp artillery had humiliated the French army, the new German Empire and Krupp were, in practice, the same machinery.

During the Gründerzeit — the economic boom that followed unification between 1871 and 1873 — production skyrocketed. The company was already **the largest in Europe**: 16,000 workers in 1873, factories covering square kilometers in Essen, its own coal and iron mines, a fleet of ships to transport ore from Spain, and an internal social security system that included sickness insurance, pension, workers' housing, a company hospital, schools, public baths and cooperative stores. But the price of that protection was absolute.

---

## 1872 — The *Generalregulativ*: the factory as total state

In 1872, in direct response to a wave of strikes that shook the Ruhr basin, Alfred Krupp published the *Generalregulativ*: the company's internal constitution. The document established a hierarchy of military precision — from foreman to management — but its true purpose was political.

The rules were explicit: workers had to swear loyalty to the company, needed written permission from the foreman even to go to the bathroom, and **all union or social democratic activity was prohibited**. Krupp did not just ban unions: he banned thinking about politics. His proclamations told workers not to worry about national affairs — he would take care of those.

Why?

The first reason was immediate: the 1872 strikes threatened the production of Europe's largest weapons manufacturer. But the second reason was deeper and darker.

The Social Democratic Party of Germany (SPD), founded in 1863, was growing exponentially among industrial workers. Krupp and Bismarck shared the same terror: that workers would organize politically and challenge the established order. Krupp's response was twofold: on one hand, repression — prohibition of unions, dismissal of agitators, internal surveillance —; on the other, a social welfare system so generous for the time that workers would have no *reason* to turn to socialism.

It was a Faustian pact: protection from cradle to grave in exchange for absolute submission. Loyalty to Krupp, not to the state, not to a party, not to a union. The company became the sole source of the worker's identity and security.

Bismarck studied this model and copied it. His social laws of the 1880s — sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), old-age insurance (1889) — were designed with the explicit goal of taking votes away from the SPD: giving workers enough security so they would not need socialism. The formula "social protection in exchange for political demobilization" was born in Essen before it arrived in Berlin.

The German trade union movement described in the text on ver.di and IG Metall grew precisely against this model: against Krupp's paternalism, against the prohibition of unions, against the idea that loyalty to the employer could replace class solidarity.

And there is an even more unsettling detail: later historians — among them William Manchester in *The Arms of Krupp* (1968) — documented that this model of leadership, where an all-powerful boss demands absolute obedience in exchange for protection, was cited as a direct influence on the *Führerprinzip* that National Socialism would adopt half a century later.

In essence, Krupp did not just manufacture cannons. He manufactured a model of social control that survived the Empire, the Weimar Republic, and found its most brutal expression in the Third Reich.

### Paternalism or business?

The uncomfortable question is how much of this was generosity and how much was economic calculation. The answer: both, but with the numbers very clearly on one side.

Krupp's wages were, on average, **higher** than those in the rest of the Ruhr. Paying better was part of the strategy: attract the best workers and retain them. The workers' housing, the hospital, the schools, the cooperative stores (Konsumanstalt) — all of this existed and was real. But the system was self-financing. Housing was deducted from wages. Sickness insurance and pension contributions were mandatory. The company store sold at cost, yes, but also guaranteed that wages returned to the company. The Krupp worker earned more than the average, but also spent more within the Krupp system. And if they left, they lost everything: the house, the pension, medical care, the children's school.

It was a skilled labor retention mechanism disguised as charity. The net cost to the company — after deducting what was recovered via deductions and internal consumption — was lower than the cost of facing strikes, staff turnover, or unions. Social peace was cheaper than class struggle. And Krupp had understood this before Bismarck.

---

## 1873 — Villa Hügel

In 1873 the Villa Hügel was completed — 8,100 square meters, 269 rooms, food elevators, central heating —, the residence that would function as the ancestral castle of the dynasty. From there, Alfred Krupp governed an empire that no longer answered to any state: the states answered to his cannons.

When Alfred Krupp died in 1887, 20,000 workers worked in his factories. They called him *der Kanonenkönig*: the Cannon King.

### The money: no banks, until there was no alternative

A crucial detail that differentiates Krupp from other German industrial giants: Alfred Krupp hated banks. His policy was to finance all expansion with reinvested profits — zero debt, zero outside shareholders, zero interference. While Siemens and AEG grew with credit from German universal banks, Krupp grew with the cash flow from selling cannons.

It worked until it stopped working. In 1873, the stock market crash — the *Gründerkrach* — collapsed steel demand. Krupp had invested fortunes in mines in Spain, a private fleet in Rotterdam, and the massive expansion of the Essen factory. Suddenly, orders vanished. Debts exceeded 30 million Reichsmark. Alfred Krupp had to swallow his pride and accept a bailout.

The consortium of banks was led by the **Preußische Seehandlung** — the Prussian state bank —, which coordinated a 30-million-mark loan in exchange for mortgaging the factory. For a decade, Krupp worked for the bankers. The same mechanism reappeared 129 years later: in 2002, Bavarian and German banks became involuntary owners of the KirchMedia empire after its €6.5 billion collapse (text 2204). In both cases, banks with state links absorbed a strategically vital company and eventually reprivatized it. Alfred progressively withdrew from management, embittered. It was the only time in his life that he lost control.

The lesson was etched into the family's DNA. From then on, the Krupps maintained an ambivalent relationship with outside capital: they needed it in crises but rejected it in prosperity. In 1924/25, another crisis nearly broke them — Gustav refused to integrate Krupp into the giant Vereinigte Stahlwerke and had to apply harsh rescue measures. And in 1966/67, credit problems were the final trigger for Alfried to convert the company into a foundation.

---

## 1854 — Friedrich Alfred Krupp is born

Only child of Alfred, Friedrich Alfred Krupp was born in 1854. When his father died in 1887, he inherited an empire of 20,000 workers and expanded it aggressively: he bought the Germaniawerft shipyard in Kiel (where submarines would later be built). That same naval tradition continues today: HDW in Kiel, now part of TKMS — partially controlled by the Krupp Foundation —, builds the Type 212 submarines for Germany and Norway. One hundred and seventy years of submarines in the same city under the same family umbrella. He also acquired the Rheinhausen foundry and brought the workforce to 43,000. Under his direction, Krupp became the main supplier of naval armor for the German Empire — Krupp steel coated the battleships of the new fleet that Kaiser Wilhelm II was building to challenge England.

But Friedrich Alfred did not inherit his father's obsessive discipline. He was a man of refined tastes, a frequent traveler, and spent long periods in Italy. It was precisely in Italy where his life collapsed.

---

## 1870 — Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach is born in The Hague

Gustav Georg Friedrich Maria von Bohlen und Halbach was born in The Hague on 7 August 1870, the fifth of seven children. His father — Gustav Georg Friedrich Bohlen-Halbach, born in Philadelphia in 1831 — was a German diplomat and master of ceremonies at the court of the Grand Duchy of Baden. His mother, Sophie Bohlen, was the daughter of General Henry Bohlen, the German-American who died fighting for the Union in the Civil War.

The young Gustav grew up among European courts. He studied law in Lausanne, Strasbourg and Heidelberg, and at age 23 entered the Prussian diplomatic service. His career was that of a functional aristocrat: posted at the German embassy in Washington D.C., then in Peking during the Boxer Rebellion, and finally as legation counselor to the Holy See in the Vatican. He spoke several languages, knew everyone who mattered, and had no framework of his own.

What made Gustav perfect for the Kaiser was precisely that: a man of the world without his own ambition, with impeccable manners and without the remotest idea of how a steelworks operated. He was moldable. And in 1906, Wilhelm II needed exactly that.

But where did the "von" come from? The Bohlen und Halbach family was not old landed aristocracy. They were Rhenish merchants who had prospered in metal manufacturing in the 18th century. Gustav's great-grandfather emigrated to Pennsylvania and made a fortune in coal and iron. The "von" — the noble title — was not a medieval inheritance: it was service nobility (*Dienstadel*), granted by the Prussian state in exchange for decades of diplomatic career. Gustav's father had received it for his services as a diplomat and master of ceremonies at the Baden court. His maternal grandfather, Henry Bohlen, had been a general. They had titles, connections, and impeccable manners. But they had no money. They were aristocrats without fortune — exactly what a Kaiser needed who wanted to marry the wealthiest woman in Germany to someone who could not compete with the throne.

---

## 1886 — Bertha Krupp is born

Bertha Krupp was born in 1886, eldest daughter of Friedrich Alfred Krupp. She grew up in the Villa Hügel as heiress to Germany's largest industrial empire. In 1902, at age sixteen, her father's death made her the wealthiest woman in the country — and a political problem for the Kaiser.

---

## 1902 — The Capri scandal and the suicide

In November 1902, the Social Democratic newspaper *Vorwärts* published a series of articles accusing Friedrich Alfred Krupp of maintaining homosexual relations with young Italians on the island of Capri, where he spent summers at the Grand Hotel Quisisana. The accusations included explicit details and names.

To understand why this was devastating, one must understand Wilhelmine Germany. Article 175 of the German penal code punished homosexual acts with imprisonment. Homosexuality was not just a crime: it was absolute social ruin. Unlike other scandals of the time — such as the Eulenburg affair, which involved the Kaiser's courtiers —, there was no way to silence this with political influence. *Vorwärts* was not a sensationalist newspaper: it was the official organ of the SPD, and it had a clear political motivation. Exposing the hypocrisy of a member of the industrial elite who banned unions in his factories while leading a life that the penal code condemned was a masterstroke.

The Kaiser, who had protected other members of his circle in similar cases, distanced himself this time. Friedrich Alfred received the news in Capri. On 22 November 1902 he was found dead in his room at the Hotel Quisisana. The official cause was a stroke. The reality accepted by historians is that he committed suicide — probably with cyanide, though the details were suppressed by the family and the German government.

The funeral was a state event. The Kaiser attended personally and gave a speech attacking the Social Democrats, whom he accused of having "murdered" Krupp with their slander. But the damage was already done. Friedrich Alfred left as sole heir his eldest daughter, Bertha, sixteen years old. The largest industrial empire in Europe was in the hands of a teenager.

---


*Note: The Aktiengesellschaft (AG)*

*Before the AG, German capitalism operated with tools from another era. There was the individual merchant — who was liable with his entire fortune if the business failed —, the personal partnerships where a few partners knew and trusted each other, and the medieval guilds that controlled who could produce what. None of this served to finance a railway, a steelworks or a chemical plant. The First Industrial Revolution — steam engines, looms — could still be paid for by a wealthy family. The Second — railways, electricity, heavy chemicals, steel on a continental scale — required sums that no personal fortune could reach.*

*The railway was the catalyst. A single railway line cost more than the combined fortune of the wealthiest Berlin bankers. The first German AGs were railway companies in the 1830s and 1840s. But founding an AG required a state concession — each one was a political favor, and the government could deny it, condition it or revoke it. That changed in 1870. The liberal reform eliminated the concession system: from that year onward, anyone could found an AG by meeting the legal requirements, without asking for permission. The timing was not coincidental: German unification was underway, victory against France was months away, and the new Empire needed a legal architecture that would allow its industry to compete with England.*

*The AG solved four things that the old system could not. One: pooling capital from hundreds or thousands of investors who did not know each other. Two: limited liability — each investor only risked what they put in, not their house or their savings —, which was unthinkable under the old forms where the owner was liable down to his shirt. Three: the company did not die when the founder died — the shares were inherited without touching operations. Four: scale without structural limit. Between 1870 and 1873, 928 AGs were founded. The Gründerzeit speculative bubble burst in 1873, and the 1884 reform tightened the rules to protect investors. From that reform came the dual structure that would define German capitalism: a Vorstand that manages and an Aufsichtsrat that supervises, two separate bodies, no omnipotent director. Siemens, AEG, Deutsche Bank, Thyssen — all adopted this form in the following decades.*

---

## 1903 — Fried. Krupp AG

By Friedrich Alfred's will, Bertha inherited all the shares of the new Fried. Krupp AG — except for the four required by law, distributed among associates. The three governance bodies (Vorstand, Aufsichtsrat, shareholders) answered to a single will.

What this architecture solved was deeper than a will. The shares passed to the heir without touching operations — the company did not fragment among children or depend on the talent of the next Krupp. It shielded against the state — it was a private entity governed by the commercial code, not a fief subject to the Kaiser's will. It allowed growth without depending on the family fortune. And it transformed the nature of power: under the old regime, an industrialist was a wealthy subject; under the AG, they were an institution. The Krupp family went from merchants to rulers of an entity that existed above them.

Final paradox: in 1943 Hitler would issue the *Lex Krupp* to undo exactly this structure — because for the Nazi regime, an AG with a board and committees was too independent; they preferred a sole owner they could call on the phone.

Fried. Krupp AG ceased to exist as such in 1943. After the war, Alfried rebuilt the group as personal property. In 1968 the estate passed to the foundation. In 1992 Krupp absorbed Hoesch AG (→ Fried. Krupp AG Hoesch-Krupp). In 1999 it merged with Thyssen AG to create ThyssenKrupp AG, the name under which it exists today. The foundation remains the largest single shareholder, with around 21%. The AG no longer bears the name Krupp, but the three rings are still there. The trajectory is not unique: Krupp (family firm → Fried. Krupp AG 1903 → foundation 1968 → ThyssenKrupp AG), Bertelsmann (family publisher 1835 → Bertelsmann AG 1971 → Bertelsmann Stiftung as controlling shareholder 1977, text 2203) and TKMS (division → AG spin-off 2024 → IPO with Krupp-Stiftung as anchor 2025, text 2207) traced the same evolution: personal ownership → corporatization → institutionalization through foundation with access to public capital.

---

## 1903 — Four years in limbo

While the new structure settled and Bertha grew up, the Kaiser was looking for a husband. Not just any husband: someone who could run Europe's largest weapons manufacturer without ambitions of their own to rival the throne's, with enough aristocratic blood so the wedding would not look like a purchase, and willing to adopt the Krupp surname — because the name was the brand, and the brand could not disappear.

---

## 1906 — The wedding of the century

The wedding was celebrated in 1906 at the Villa Hügel. Kaiser Wilhelm II attended personally and acted as a witness. It was a state act in everything but name. Gustav, by royal decree, received the right to place the surname Krupp before his own: from that day forward he would sign as Gustav Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach. The Kaiser told him verbatim: "Your mission is to run the Krupp enterprise in the interest of the German Empire."

Gustav was thirty-six years old, had never set foot in a factory, and overnight became the director of Europe's largest industrial complex. He was appointed vice chairman of the supervisory board of Fried. Krupp AG at the end of 1906, and in 1909 assumed the chairmanship. He learned the business on the job. Historians agree that he was a competent though unimaginative manager — exactly what the Kaiser had ordered. No initiatives of his own that would challenge the Empire's policy, no scandals, no deviations. A loyal administrator.

The industrial money — three centuries of Krupp steel — was united with the diplomatic lineage of the von Bohlen und Halbachs. The merger was complete. The Kaiser who had personally orchestrated this dynastic merger was the same Wilhelm II whose erratic leadership and naval arms race — built with Krupp steel — would drag Germany into the First World War and the collapse of the monarchy in 1918.

---

## 1914–1918 — First World War

Under Gustav's leadership, Krupp had an almost absolute monopoly on heavy weapons manufacturing in Germany. From its factories came the "Big Bertha" — named after his wife —, the Paris Gun, and the U-boats. The company was the heart of the German military-industrial complex.

---

## 1934 — Bertha: owner, dispossessed, forgotten

In 1912, the yearbook of German millionaires had recorded Bertha Krupp as the wealthiest woman in Germany: 187 million marks in assets (equivalent to about €1.23 billion today) and an annual income of 17 million marks. She was, at age twenty-six, the legal owner of Europe's largest industrial empire.

In 1934, Adolf Hitler wanted to visit the Villa Hügel. Bertha refused. She did not consider him a legitimate head of state: to her, he was a "boorish upstart" (*rüpelhaften Emporkömmling*). But in 1935 she could refuse no longer. The swastika flag was hoisted for the first time over the hill of the Villa. Bertha said to an employee: "Go down and see how low we have fallen."

In 1943, the Lex Krupp wrested everything from her. The shares passed to Alfried. Bertha, who had been legal owner of the company since the age of sixteen, was displaced without documented compensation. In 1946, the city of Essen — whose cathedral, hospitals and schools had been financed by the Krupps for a century — withdrew her and Gustav's honorary citizenship. She lived her last years discreetly in Essen. She donated land for a church in the Bedingrade neighborhood. She died on 21 September 1957, at age seventy-one.

---

## 1938 — Alfried joins the Nazi Party

Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach, the eldest son, joined the NSDAP at the end of 1938 — a relatively late affiliation — and was a supporting member of the SS. The question of whether it was opportunism or conviction remains open.

---

## 1939–1945 — Second World War

During the war, the Krupp company used forced labor on a massive scale: prisoners of war and concentration camp inmates worked in its armaments factories. The "Berthawerk" plant, near the Markstadt forced labor camp, bore Bertha's name. Two of the family's children — Claus in 1940 and Eckbert in 1945 — died in combat. Harald spent ten years in Soviet captivity.

---

## 1943 — The Lex Krupp: Hitler dissolves the AG

Since 1903, Fried. Krupp AG had legally belonged to Bertha. She held the shares, she was the owner. Gustav had chaired the supervisory board until 1943, but ownership was never his. In December of that year, Hitler signed a special decree — the *Lex Krupp* — that dissolved the corporation, transferred all shares to Alfried, and declared him sole proprietor. Bertha was displaced. Gustav, elderly and senile, was out. The company that had been hers since age sixteen passed to her son by decree of the Führer.

Why? There were several reasons, and none was sentimental.

The first was ideological. The Nazi regime operated under the *Führerprinzip*: one leader, one will, zero checks. An AG with its collegial Vorstand, its supervisory Aufsichtsrat, and its shareholders' assembly was the antithesis of that. Too many people making decisions, too much independence, too much ambiguity about who was in charge. Hitler wanted a sole owner he could call and say "I need so many tanks by yesterday." Alfried, member of the SS and the NSDAP since 1938, was that man. Bertha was not.

The second was practical. In total war, armaments production could not depend on the rhythms of corporate governance. When Speer, the Armaments Minister, needed to increase submarine or cannon production, he could not wait for a supervisory board to deliberate. He needed an interlocutor with absolute power over the company.

The third was dynastic — but in a Nazi key. The regime believed in primogeniture, in the continuity of the great industrial families as pillars of the racial state. A 57-year-old woman did not fit that mold. Her male son, SS, married, with his own heir, did.

The fourth — perhaps the most cynical — was that the regime needed Alfried tied down. If the state handed him the empire on a silver platter — literally gave him absolute ownership of one of the largest conglomerates in Europe —, Alfried's loyalty was sealed. He was not an industrialist collaborating with the regime out of convenience. He was a man who owed everything to the Führer. Including his mother's fortune.

The *Lex Krupp* was, in essence, the return of industrial power to the form that National Socialism best understood: one man, one family, one company, zero accountability. Exactly the opposite of what Friedrich Alfred had designed forty years earlier. The same *Führerprinzip* that dissolved the Krupp AG to concentrate ownership in a single owner also attracted the Hohenzollern princes: Crown Prince Wilhelm wrote to Hitler asking for the restoration of the monarchy, and Prince August Wilhelm was SA-Obergruppenführer. The *Lex Krupp* belongs to a recurring German pattern: laws written for a single entity. The Habsburgergesetz of 1919 (text 2202) confiscated exactly the assets of one family. The VW-Gesetz of 1960 (text 2206) governs a single company with special voting rights. All three circumvent general law to legislate for specific entities for political reasons.

## 1945 — Arrest

American troops arrested Alfried at the end of the war. Gustav, already elderly and senile, was deemed unfit to stand trial.

---

## 1948 — The Krupp Trial at Nuremberg

Alfried was tried in the so-called "Krupp Trial" of the Nuremberg trials. He was sentenced to twelve years in prison for plunder of occupied territories and for charges related to the use of forced labor. He was acquitted of charges of preparing a war of aggression and conspiracy against peace.

---

## 1950 — Gustav dies

Gustav Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach died on 16 January 1950. He had led the empire for four decades, through two world wars.

---

## 1951 — The pardon

On 31 January 1951, John Jay McCloy — US High Commissioner for Germany — signed the pardon of Alfried Krupp. He also commuted the sentences of the other eleven Krupp executives convicted at Nuremberg and lifted the confiscation of their property. Alfried had served three years of a twelve-year sentence. He left Landsberg prison — the same one where Hitler had written *Mein Kampf* — and returned to Essen.

Who was McCloy and why did he free Alfried Krupp?

John J. McCloy (1895–1989) was no ordinary bureaucrat. He was the archetype of the American establishment. Harvard Law graduate, Wall Street lawyer, assistant to Secretary of War Henry Stimson during World War II — where he oversaw the internment of 120,000 Japanese-Americans and opposed the bombing of the railway lines to Auschwitz —, president of the World Bank (1947–1949), and from 1949 High Commissioner in Germany. After his German post he would become president of Chase Manhattan Bank — the Rockefeller family bank —, president of the Ford Foundation, and president of the Council on Foreign Relations for seventeen years. He was an adviser to every president from Roosevelt to Reagan. In 1963 he would serve on the Warren Commission that investigated the Kennedy assassination.

When McCloy arrived in Germany in 1949, the world had changed. The Cold War was the new framework. West Germany was no longer an occupied enemy: it was a potential ally against the Soviet Union. And you could not rearm Germany while keeping the owner of its largest arms manufacturer in prison.

The pressure did not come only from Washington. Chancellor Konrad Adenauer personally applied pressure. The same Adenauer who tried in 1960 to create a state television channel — Deutschland-Fernsehen GmbH — and was blocked by the Federal Constitutional Court: he freed industrial power with one hand while trying to capture media power with the other. German industry — through its contacts with American corporations — lobbied intensively. Krupp's lawyers argued that the trial had been "victors' justice" and that forced labor was a widespread practice in the German war economy, not a crime exclusive to Krupp. The argument was cynical but effective: if everyone did it, why is only Krupp going to prison?

McCloy did not act alone. He appointed an advisory commission — the Advisory Board on Clemency — headed by David W. Peck, a New York judge. The commission recommended massive sentence reductions. Of the 142 Nuremberg prisoners reviewed, 79 saw their sentences commuted. Thirty-three Nuremberg convicts — among them Alfried Krupp, Friedrich Flick (the largest German coal and steel industrialist) and several SS generals — were freed in a matter of months.

The pattern was clear and not limited to Krupp. It was a geopolitical strategy: denazification was over. The reconstruction of Germany as an anti-communist bulwark was beginning, and that required returning power to the very industrialists who had sustained Hitler.

### Bribery, lobby or conviction?

The inevitable question is whether there was something more than politics: dollars under the table, cross-corporate favors, pressure from Wall Street? The answer is more interesting than simple bribery.

There is no evidence that McCloy received money from Krupp. But McCloy did not *need* to be paid: he was the system. Before the war he had been a lawyer for IG Farben — the same company that manufactured Zyklon B — and other German corporations from his Cravath office, one of the most prestigious law firms on Wall Street. He knew the German industrialists personally. He had worked for them. When he arrived in Germany as High Commissioner in 1949, he was not a stranger judging enemies: he was a corporate lawyer reuniting with former clients.

The lobbying operated on three simultaneous levels, none illegal, all effective:

**First, the German government.** Konrad Adenauer, the chancellor, applied direct and repeated pressure. His argument was simple: you cannot build a stable German democracy if the leaders of its industry are in prison. Reconciliation demanded forgetting.

**Second, the German street.** Hundreds of thousands of Germans protested demanding the release of the Nuremberg prisoners. The McCloy family received death threats. Paradoxically, those exerting the most pressure to free the convicts were the Germans themselves. Denazification, which the Allies had imposed as a moral condition, was deeply unpopular among a population that preferred to look forward.

**Third, the Cold War.** In 1950 the Korean War broke out. The United States needed to rearm West Germany as soon as possible. You could not build the new Bundeswehr without German heavy industry. And you could not have German heavy industry with the industrialists in jail. The logic was cynical but impeccable.

McCloy was not bribed. He was convinced — by Adenauer, by the street, by the Pentagon, and by his own conviction that a prosperous Germany was the best dam against communism. On 31 January 1951 he signed the commutations. Judge William J. Wilkins, who had presided over the Krupp trial at Nuremberg, later wrote: "Imagine my surprise when one day in February 1951 I read in the newspaper that John J. McCloy, the High Commissioner for Germany, had restored all the Krupp properties we had ordered confiscated."

As for the 1906 wedding, the mechanism was different but equally revealing. The Kaiser did not need lobbying: he chose Gustav personally. The wedding was a state act, not an influence operation. But the underlying logic was the same as in 1951: political power needed industrial power, and industrial power needed a facade of legitimacy. In 1906 it was an aristocratic surname. In 1951 it was the Cold War. The pattern did not change: only the pretexts changed.

In 1953, the Mehlem Agreement between Krupp and the Allies allowed Alfried to retain control of the empire in exchange for formally selling his coal mines and steelworks — a sale that never materialized because no buyers appeared. By 1960, the Krupp group was worth more than a billion dollars. Alfried was back. Richer and more powerful than before the war.

---

## 1953 — Return to power

Alfried resumed management of the company. But how did he go from leaving prison to being worth over a billion dollars in seven years? Three factors.

First, the 1953 Mehlem Agreement was a dead letter. The Allies ordered Krupp to sell 75% of its coal mines and steelworks to deconcentrate German industrial power. Krupp put the assets up for sale. Not a single buyer appeared. No one wanted to buy mines and factories requiring massive investments in a Germany that was still in ruins. By the early 1960s, the divestiture order was discreetly shelved. Krupp retained everything.

Second, the *Wirtschaftswunder* — the German economic miracle — created an insatiable demand for steel, machinery and industrial equipment. Krupp was the largest producer. The reconstruction of Germany, NATO rearmament, and the Korean War (1950–53) caused orders to skyrocket. The factories the Allies had not managed to fully dismantle returned to full capacity.

Third, Alfried no longer had to answer to a supervisory board. Hitler's *Lex Krupp*, which had dissolved the AG in 1943, remained in force. He was the sole owner. He could reinvest every mark of profit without anyone's permission, exactly like his great-grandfather Alfred. By 1960, the group was worth more than a billion dollars.

---

## 1957 — Bertha dies

Bertha Krupp died in Essen on 21 September 1957. She was buried in the family crypt. Despite the shadow of the "Berthawerk" and forced labor, in Essen she was remembered as a beloved figure for her charitable work and her visits to sick workers and their families.

## 1966 — Arndt renounces the inheritance

Arndt von Bohlen und Halbach (1938–1986), Alfried's only son, refused to continue with the family business. He renounced all his rights in 1966 in exchange for an annual pension of half a million dollars. The line of succession broke voluntarily.

---

## 1967 — Alfried dies and the foundation is born

Alfried Krupp died on 30 July 1967 in Essen. His only son, Arndt, had already renounced the inheritance the previous year. Without an heir and without a family business to bequeath, Alfried made a decision no Krupp had made before: in his will of September 1966 he decreed that his entire estate — shares, properties, art collections, the Villa Hügel — should pass to a non-profit foundation. The Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach-Stiftung began its activities on 1 January 1968.

### The transition from personal to institutional power

Until 1967, power at Krupp had always been unipersonal. Friedrich, Alfred, Friedrich Alfred, Bertha, Gustav, Alfried — each generation had one owner. Hitler's *Lex Krupp* in 1943 had reinforced exactly that model: one leader, one will, one company. The foundation broke with six generations of unipersonal dictatorship.

But not entirely. Alfried named **Berthold Beitz** (1913–2013) as first executor, the man who had been his personal representative since 1953. Beitz assumed the chairmanship of the Kuratorium in 1968 and held it for **forty-five years**, until his death in 2013. During that period, Beitz was the foundation. The other two executors were Arndt von Bohlen und Halbach (the son who had renounced) and Dedo von Schenck, a Frankfurt lawyer.

Beitz is a paradoxical figure. On one hand, he was the architect of Alfried Krupp's post-war power. On the other, during World War II he and his wife Else had saved hundreds of Jews in Boryslaw, Poland — for which Yad Vashem recognized them as "Righteous Among the Nations." The man who led for half a century the fortune of a Nuremberg convict was also a hero of the Holocaust. That contradiction defines the foundation.

### Who holds power today

Since 2013, the chairwoman of the Kuratorium is **Ursula Gather**, statistician and former rector of the Technical University of Dortmund. The vice chairman is **Christoph M. Schmidt**, economist and president of the RWI institute. The rest of the council — minimum 9 members, maximum 12 according to the statutes — is a who's who of the German establishment:

- **Armin Laschet** — former Minister-President of North Rhine-Westphalia and CDU candidate for chancellor in 2021. As *Ministerpräsident* of the state that houses the Ruhr basin — seat of Funke Mediengruppe, the WDR and the industrial heart of IG Metall —, Laschet embodies the informal link between the Kuratorium and the CDU's control over ZDF through the Freundeskreise (texts 2205, 2206).
- **Helge Braun** — former head of the Chancellery under Angela Merkel
- **Bernd Pischetsrieder** — former CEO of BMW and Volkswagen. In both positions he negotiated for years with the same IG Metall unions that, thanks to *Mitbestimmung*, occupy half the seats on those companies' supervisory boards.
- **Sabine Lautenschläger** — former member of the ECB Executive Board and the Bundesbank. The Fazit-Stiftung, owner of the *Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung*, was co-founded by the Union of German Banks and has historically included former Bundesbank presidents on its board; the same constellation of central bankers — ex-ECB, ex-Bundesbank — is present in the Krupp Foundation's Kuratorium.
- **Rainer Esser** — CEO of the Die Zeit publishing group (Holtzbrinck/DvH Medien), Germany's most influential liberal weekly. His presence on the Kuratorium directly connects the country's leading progressive publication to the foundation that controls 21% of ThyssenKrupp and 10% of TKMS, Europe's largest submarine builder.
- **Isabel Corinna Knauf** — from the Knauf family, multinational construction materials company
- **Ulrike Groos** — director of the Kunsthalle Baden-Baden museum
- **Gabriele Sadowski** — professor of chemistry at TU Dortmund
- **Kersten von Schenck** — lawyer, presumably linked to Dedo von Schenck, the original executor

The Vorstand consists of two people — Volker Troche and Michaela Muylkens — who manage day-to-day operations with a team of fewer than twenty employees.

The power structure changed, but only in part. It is no longer a single man deciding. It is a council of eleven people from the German *establishment* — academics, politicians, business leaders, lawyers. Legitimacy passed from blood to institutional prestige. But power remains concentrated in very few hands, without democratic election, and with a source of income dependent on a single company.

### The Kuratorium: power without voters

The foundation's Kuratorium indirectly controls 20.93% of ThyssenKrupp and 10% of TKMS directly. In ThyssenKrupp's supervisory board, where the foundation holds two seats, IG Metall holds ten of the twenty seats — the rest belong to shareholders —, which means that decisions about the group's future pass through a permanent balance between the Krupp heritage and Europe's most powerful union. No one has elected the Kuratorium. No one from outside can remove it. It has no obligation to account publicly beyond the foundation's annual report. Its eleven members renew themselves among themselves: when someone leaves, the rest vote for their replacement in closed sessions, without press, without external scrutiny, without any parliament, regulator or court being able to veto the decision.

It is perfectly legal. German foundations are governed by civil law and answer to the foundation authority of the federal state — in this case, the Bezirksregierung Düsseldorf —, whose supervision is limited to verifying compliance with the statutes and tax law. It does not audit the council's composition, does not demand transparency in deliberations, cannot remove anyone. As long as the foundation spends its income on science, education, art, culture, health and sport — which it does —, the state has nothing to say about who governs 21% of Germany's largest steel group.

It is, in essence, one of the most opaque power bodies in Germany: perfectly institutional, perfectly invisible to the general public. The Stiftung is the recurring legal vehicle through which German personal or family power becomes institutional power: the Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds (1923, text 2202), the Fazit-Stiftung (1959, text 2203), the Bertelsmann Stiftung (1977, text 2203), the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung (text 2206) and the Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung (text 2206) all use the same legal form for the same purpose: preserving elite control beyond democratic reach. Krupp was not the first and will not be the last. The same model of appointed, not elected, council, "representative" of social forces with no electoral accountability, is repeated in the ZDF Fernsehrat (text 2205) and the Aufsichtsrat under Mitbestimmung (text 2206). A constitutional monarchy without a constitution and without a monarch. Or more precisely: a monarchy with eleven monarchs who elect each other.

### Who controls whom?

The underlying question is whether the foundation controls ThyssenKrupp or the other way around.

With 20.93% of the capital and two seats on the supervisory board, the foundation has influence but not control. The strategic decisions — closing plants, laying off workers, selling divisions, building submarines — are made by ThyssenKrupp's Vorstand. The foundation can vote against, but with 21% it is not enough to block anything. The company has revenues of €37.5 billion a year; the foundation's grants are two orders of magnitude smaller.

The foundation legitimizes the company: it gives it a human face, funds scholarships and museums, keeps the historical memory alive with the archive. But its real capacity to influence what ThyssenKrupp does — beyond voting on a board where it is a minority — is limited. The tail does not wag the dog.

This is especially visible in the armaments division. The foundation holds 10% directly in TKMS in addition to its stake in the parent company. When TKMS sells submarines with cruise missile capability to Israel, when it builds frigates for Egypt, when it competes for military contracts in India and Canada — the foundation receives the dividends and funds scholarships with them. The structure allows the same euro that came out of an arms contract to end up paying for an artist residency or a school laboratory. The compartmentalization is perfect: the Kuratorium approves grants, ThyssenKrupp's Vorstand approves submarines, and they never sit in the same room.

### How it invests

The foundation is the largest shareholder of ThyssenKrupp AG (20.93%). It is not a diversified fund: practically all its assets are in those shares. The book value as of December 2023 was about €800 million. When ThyssenKrupp pays dividends, the foundation receives income. When it doesn't — and between 2013 and 2023 it only paid in 2014, 2015, 2017 and 2018 —, the foundation draws on reserves. Since 1968 it has disbursed €696 million in projects. In 2023 it approved €8.5 million.

The foundation has two seats on ThyssenKrupp's supervisory board and votes at shareholders' meetings. Its decisions affect tens of thousands of workers.

### Are there still weapons?

Yes, and more than just submarines. ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems (TKMS) — where the foundation holds a direct 10% stake in addition to its 20.93% in the parent company — produces:

- **Submarines**: Type 212CD (for Germany and Norway), Type 214 (Turkey, India), Type 218 (Singapore), Dakar class (Israel, with cruise missile capability), Dolphin II class (Israel). It is one of the world's largest manufacturers of non-nuclear submarines.
- **Frigates**: MEKO A-200 class (Egypt), MEKO A-100 / Tamandaré class (Brazil), future F127 for Germany.
- **Support vessels**: *Protecteur* class for Canada.
- **Electronic combat systems**: through its subsidiary Atlas Elektronik, which produces sonars, combat management systems and torpedoes for navies worldwide.

In total, TKMS has 23 submarines and 5 surface vessels in confirmed production, with potential orders for another 50+ in negotiation. It employs 9,100 people. In 2024–2025 the German government intervened to guarantee itself veto power over any sale of the division to foreign capital — they do not want their submarines ending up in the hands of a Saudi or Chinese fund.

Weapons are no longer the main business of ThyssenKrupp — steel, elevators and automotive components move more money —, but the company that for a century and a half manufactured the cannons of every European war continues to produce war machines. Only now the orders are approved in a council where sit representatives of a foundation that also funds scholarships, museums and hospitals.

---

## 1986 — End of the family line

Arndt von Bohlen und Halbach died in 1986. With him the direct male line of the family became extinct. The dynasty that had manufactured cannons for kings, emperors and dictators for more than a century ceased to exist as a family business.

---

## 2022 — Historical research underway

The foundation bearing the name of Alfried Krupp undertook an independent academic project to thoroughly investigate its relationship with National Socialism. What is still under investigation:

- His supporting membership in the SS.
- The reasons for his late entry into the NSDAP at the end of 1938.
- His possible involvement in plans for a production plant in Auschwitz.
- The support he gave in the 1950s to former Nuremberg convicts released from Landsberg.

---

The complete arc of these two families spans four centuries: from a merchant who got rich by buying the houses of plague victims in 1587, to a Prussian-American general who died in the Civil War, to a Kaiser who married a sixteen-year-old heiress to a penniless diplomat, to an empire that armed two world wars with forced labor, to an heir convicted at Nuremberg and pardoned by the United States, to a foundation that — perhaps — is trying to answer the questions the family never answered in life.
