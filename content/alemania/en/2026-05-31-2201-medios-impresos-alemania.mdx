# Print Media in Germany — Who Controls What and How Power Became Concentrated

---

## 1835 — Carl Bertelsmann founds a hymnal publisher in Gütersloh

Carl Bertelsmann (1791–1850), a bookbinder and printer from Gütersloh, Westphalia, founded _C. Bertelsmann Verlag_ in July 1835. His specialty was Protestant hymnals, catechisms, and devotional literature for the Awakening movement (_Erweckungsbewegung_), a Pietist revival sweeping through the Prussian provinces.

The business would not have survived without the Prussian state. The regional Lutheran churches bought hymnals in bulk for their parishes. Carl Bertelsmann was a pious entrepreneur, yes, but also a man who understood that selling books to the ecclesiastical apparatus was a business of guaranteed scale.

When he died in 1850, his son Heinrich Bertelsmann (1827–1887) took the helm. He expanded the catalog to include youth literature and entertainment novels. But the publisher remained provincial: a family business from Gütersloh, respectable but insignificant outside Westphalia.

From this same small Westphalian town, the Mohn family would eventually control RTL —Germany's largest commercial television group— through its Bertelsmann/RTL Group holding. The journey from Protestant hymnals to *RTL aktuell* took 150 years and never left Gütersloh. Nothing in those first hundred years foretold what was to come.

---

## 1875 — Johann Andreas Ludolph Bauer founds a card printing shop in Hamburg

Johann Bauer founded a small lithographic printing shop in Hamburg. The initial product was business cards. In 1903, his son Heinrich Bauer bought a rotary press and began printing advertising flyers. For decades, the Bauers were anonymous printers for third parties.

The leap into publishing came in 1926, when Heinrich Bauer launched _Rundfunkkritik_, a modest radio programming magazine. The true masterstroke would come after the war.

---

## 1893 — Georg von Holtzbrinck founds a bookshop and a reading circle

Georg von Holtzbrinck (1865–1942) founded _Verlagsgruppe Georg von Holtzbrinck_ as a combination of bookshop and reading circle in Stuttgart. The model was simple: sell books by subscription to a loyal reader base, eliminating the intermediary bookseller. It worked: by the 1920s, Holtzbrinck operated a network of reading circles and circulating libraries throughout southern Germany.

Georg died in 1942. His sons Georg Jr. and Dieter inherited the business, but the war destroyed much of the German publishing infrastructure. It had to be rebuilt from scratch.

---

## 1908 — Axel Springer is born in Hamburg-Altona

Axel Cäsar Springer was born on May 2, 1912, in Altona (then an independent city, now a district of Hamburg). His father, Hinrich Springer, was a modest publisher who issued the local newspaper _Altonaer Nachrichten_ along with fishing and nautical magazines. He was not a magnate: he was a small provincial publisher.

Axel grew up among linotype machines and rotary presses. He learned the trade from the ground up, as an apprentice in his father's print shop and later at the Wolff news agency. His great opportunity came not through journalistic talent, but through destruction.

---

## 1945 — The zero hour of the German press

At the end of the Second World War, the Allied powers dissolved the entire German media system. The printing presses that had published the _Völkischer Beobachter_ and _Der Stürmer_ were closed or confiscated. No newspaper published during Nazism could continue. The Allies imposed a licensing system (_Lizenzpresse_): only those who obtained a permit from the occupation authorities, and only if they demonstrated anti-Nazi credentials, could found media outlets.

This created a tabula rasa: for the first time in decades, the German press market was empty. Anyone with a license, paper, and a functioning rotary press could build an empire from scratch. And several did.

---

## 1946 — Axel Springer obtains his first license

At 34, Axel Springer obtained a license from the British authorities to publish the radio magazine _Hörzu_ and the newspaper _Nordwestdeutsche Hefte_. _Hörzu_ —a radio programming guide with entertainment articles— was an immediate success. Post-war Germans were hungry for cheap distraction, and _Hörzu_ gave them exactly that.

In 1948 he launched the _Hamburger Abendblatt_, a local evening daily that combined news with sports and local events. The model was British: a popular but not sensationalist tabloid, with an accessible tone.

But what Axel Springer really wanted was a national daily. In 1952, against the advice of nearly all his advisors, he launched _Bild_.

---

## 1947 — Stern is born

In Hannover, journalist Henri Nannen launched the illustrated magazine _Stern_. The license was granted by the British military government. The first issue —August 1948— sold 130,000 copies. By the 1960s, _Stern_ was Germany's most-read news illustrated, with a formula of spectacular photojournalism, investigative reporting, and a progressive tone.

But Nannen was not the owner. The money came from publisher Gerd Bucerius and printer Richard Gruner. In 1965, Gruner bought the majority of the shares and the magazine passed to the Gruner + Jahr group. In 1969, Bertelsmann bought a 25% stake in Gruner + Jahr. In 1976, Bertelsmann became the majority shareholder. _Stern_, the progressive magazine, became the property of Germany's most conservative empire.

---

## 1948 — Heinrich Bauer launches the first TV magazine

Heinrich Bauer —son of the founder— understood the potential of television before anyone else. In 1948 he launched Germany's first television programming magazine. When public broadcasters began transmitting in the 1950s, Bauer already had the distribution infrastructure, the relationships with newsstands, and the format know-how.

Over the following decades, Bauer became the undisputed king of supermarket-checkout magazines: _TV Movie_, _TV Hören und Sehen_, _Auf einen Blick_, _Fernsehwoche_. To these were added women's magazines (_Bella_, _Laura_), gossip magazines (_Das Neue Blatt_, _Neue Post_), and youth magazines (_Bravo_, _Mädchen_). The model was volume: print runs in the millions, low price, light content, distribution in every newsstand in the country.

---

## 1949 — The Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung is born

The _Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung_ (FAZ) published its first issue on November 1, 1949. It was not a refoundation of anything, though it picked up traditions from the old _Frankfurter Zeitung_ (1856–1943, closed by the Nazis). The FAZ was founded by a group of journalists, businesspeople, and academics who wanted a quality newspaper with a liberal-conservative line.

Initial funding came from a consortium of German companies —including Allianz, Deutsche Bank, Hoechst, Siemens— that contributed capital through the _Fazit-Stiftung_, formally established in 1959. The foundation remains the sole owner of _Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung GmbH_.

The structure is unique in German journalism: a non-profit foundation owns 100% of a national daily. The five editors (_Herausgeber_) make editorial decisions by consensus. There is no owner who can call to change a headline. Nor are there shareholders demanding quarterly profitability.

Independence? Yes, but of a very specific kind: independence from the market, dependence on the establishment that founded it.

---

## 1952 — Bild: the tabloid that conquered Germany

On June 24, 1952, _Bild_ appeared for the first time at German newsstands. Four pages, huge headlines, lots of photos, little text. The price: 10 pfennigs. Everyone in Hamburg —including Springer's own editors— thought it would be a failure.

It was not.

By 1966, _Bild_ was selling more than four million copies a day, the highest circulation in Europe. In the 1980s it exceeded five million. Today it remains Germany's most-read daily newspaper with a circulation of around 1.1 million copies (2024) and massive digital reach.

_Bild_ is more than a newspaper: it is a political institution. Its formula —provocative headlines, extreme simplification, emotional identification with the "common man," clear enemies (communists in the 50s–60s, RAF terrorists in the 70s, refugees and Merkel in the 2010s)— has shaped German public opinion for seventy years. No chancellor —from Adenauer to Scholz— has been able to ignore it.

---

## 1946–1960 — Springer builds the empire

Between 1946 and 1966, Axel Springer built the largest newspaper empire in Europe. The strategy was threefold:

**First: popular dailies for the masses.** _Bild_ was the crown jewel, but he also launched or bought regional dailies and dominated the Sunday market with _Bild am Sonntag_ and _Welt am Sonntag_.

**Second: a quality daily for the elites.** In 1953, Springer bought _Die Welt_, the newspaper founded by the British occupation authorities in 1946. _Die Welt_ was (and is) the intellectual counterweight to _Bild_: a serious, conservative newspaper aimed at readers with university education and high purchasing power. Same owner, two different audiences, a single underlying message.

**Third: vertical integration.** Springer bought printing presses, distribution agencies, stakes in news agencies, and book publishers. By the 1960s, the group controlled everything from the newsroom to the newsstand.

In 1959, Springer moved into a 20-story skyscraper next to the Berlin Wall —right next to the Soviet sector. The building was a statement of principles: Springer was planting his flag on the front line of the Cold War.

---

## 1959 — The Fazit-Stiftung takes legal form

The _Fazit-Stiftung gemeinnützige Verlagsgesellschaft mbH_ was formally established in Frankfurt. Five founding entities: the Society for the Promotion of the FAZ (_Gesellschaft zur Förderung der FAZ_), the Frankfurt Chamber of Commerce, the Union of German Banks, the Federation of the Chemical Industry, and a group of businesspeople.

The model was designed to shield the FAZ from hostile takeovers and commercial pressures. The shares are not listed on the stock exchange. They cannot be sold to third parties. Profits are reinvested in the newspaper or allocated to charitable purposes. There are no dividends to distribute.

The foundation's board of trustees —the _Stiftungsrat_— is composed of figures from the German establishment: bankers, lawyers from major law firms, former politicians, businesspeople. In practice, the FAZ is a newspaper edited by conservative journalists for a conservative audience, financed by a conservative institutional fabric. The editorial line is not dictated by a magnate, but neither does it emerge from a vacuum: it emerges from a consensus among the elites who founded and sustain it. The Stiftung form connects the FAZ to the Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds (1923, text 2202) and the Krupp-Stiftung (1968, text 2201): all variations of the same legal architecture for converting personal power into institutional perpetuity.

---

## 1960 — Gruner + Jahr consolidates

Richard Gruner and John Jahr —the former a printer, the latter a journalist— had joined forces in 1965 to form Gruner + Jahr. Jahr came from the publisher that put out _Constanze_ and _Brigitte_. Gruner contributed printing and distribution capacity.

Gruner + Jahr published _Stern_ (the crown jewel), _Brigitte_ (Germany's most influential women's magazine), _Geo_ (science and travel magazine, launched in 1976), _Capital_ (business magazine), _Schöner Wohnen_ (home décor), and _Eltern_ (parenting).

In 1969, Reinhard Mohn —Bertelsmann's patriarch— bought a minority stake. By 1976, Bertelsmann controlled the majority. Gruner + Jahr became one more division of the Gütersloh conglomerate.

---

## 1947–1971 — Reinhard Mohn transforms Bertelsmann

Reinhard Mohn (1921–2009) was the fifth generation of the Bertelsmann family. When he returned from captivity in the United States in 1946 —he had been a prisoner of war—, the publisher was in ruins. The hymnal business was dead. But Mohn had seen something in the American prison camps: the soldiers read pocket books, cheap books that fit in a uniform pocket.

In 1950, Mohn founded the _Bertelsmann Lesering_ (Bertelsmann Reading Circle), a subscription book club. The model copied the American _Book of the Month Club_: members received a catalog, chose their books, and received them by mail. By 1960, the Lesering had three million members. By 1970, nearly six million.

The book clubs were the money-making machine that financed everything else. Mohn invested the profits in buying publishers: first in Germany, then in Spain (Plaza & Janés, 1976), the United States (Bantam Books, 1977; Doubleday, 1986), the United Kingdom (Transworld, 1984). But the clubs were not the destination: they were the fuel.

In 1971, Mohn transformed the family business into an _Aktiengesellschaft_ (public limited company) and placed the majority of the shares in a foundation: the _Bertelsmann Stiftung_.

---

## 1977 — The Bertelsmann Stiftung: the trick of the century

In 1977, Reinhard Mohn transferred the majority of Bertelsmann AG's shares to the Bertelsmann Stiftung, a foundation that he himself controlled. The public justification was philanthropy: the foundation would finance educational, civic, and cultural projects. The fiscal reality was more interesting.

Non-profit foundations in Germany do not pay inheritance tax. If Mohn had left the shares directly to his children, the German tax authorities would have taken approximately half the value of the empire. By transferring them to a foundation, the tax was zero.

But the foundation was not independent. Reinhard Mohn chaired its board. His wife Liz Mohn would chair it afterwards. His children held key positions. The foundation controlled Bertelsmann, and the Mohn family controlled the foundation.

Bertelsmann is not a listed company. It does not publish quarterly results. Its strategic decisions are made by a board where the foundation has the final word. In practice, it is a family monarchy disguised as philanthropy.

---

## 1983 — The scandal of the fake Hitler diaries

In April 1983, _Stern_ published what it billed as the scoop of the century: the secret diaries of Adolf Hitler, allegedly discovered in an East German barn. The magazine devoted a special issue and sold the publishing rights to media around the world, including London's _Sunday Times_ and _Newsweek_ in the United States.

They were a forgery. The forger was Konrad Kujau, a petty criminal who had carefully imitated Hitler's handwriting. _Stern_ had paid 9.3 million marks for the diaries without subjecting them to proper forensic verification. The magazine relied on a single "expert," the British historian Hugh Trevor-Roper, who gave his hasty approval. The scientific tests —paper, ink, and binding analysis— that would have exposed the fraud within hours were not done until after publication.

When the scandal broke, the consequences were devastating. _Stern_'s two chief editors —Peter Koch and Felix Schmidt— resigned. Henri Nannen, founder and honorary editor, saw his reputation tarnished. The magazine's credibility never fully recovered. Bertelsmann, as majority owner, absorbed the financial and reputational impact.

The episode revealed the structural tension between journalism and corporate ownership: a legendary magazine, pressured by the need for a coup effect to sustain its sales numbers before its Gütersloh owners, bet everything on a scoop it did not verify. And lost.

---

## 1985 — Springer expands beyond print

By the 1980s, Axel Springer AG —which went public in 1985, though the family retained control with a majority of enhanced-voting shares— was much more than newspapers. The group owned magazines (from _Auto Bild_ to _Sport Bild_), stakes in private television (SAT.1), book publishers (Ullstein, Propyläen), and radio interests.

But the crown jewel remained _Bild_. Remained _Welt_. Remained the ability to set the country's political agenda from a Berlin skyscraper. In the 1970s, Springer had personally led the press publishers' campaign that broke the ARD-ZDF public duopoly and opened the door to commercial television — without that lobbying offensive, neither RTL nor Sat.1 would exist.

Axel Springer died on September 22, 1985. He left behind a company with over 12,000 employees, a turnover of billions of marks, and an editorial line that he had personally written into the company statutes: support for German reunification, solidarity with Israel, market economy, and alliance with the West.

---

## 1999 — Bertelsmann buys Springer-Verlag (scientific)

Attention to the name difference: Bertelsmann did not buy Axel Springer AG —the press group— but Springer Science+Business Media, the scientific publisher founded in 1842 by Julius Springer in Berlin. This publisher —specialized in scientific journals, technical books, and medicine— had no relationship with Axel Springer beyond the shared surname. Bertelsmann integrated it into its professional publishing division.

But the name is confusing, and it is worth being clear: there are two "Springer" companies in Germany, with different owners, different business lines, and no corporate link between them.

---

## 2002–2008 — Holtzbrinck restructures

Georg-Dieter von Holtzbrinck (1942–2010), son of Georg Jr., led the group's expansion from the 1970s. Under his leadership, Holtzbrinck bought:
- _Die Zeit_ (Hamburg's liberal weekly, founded in 1946)
- _Handelsblatt_ (Germany's main business newspaper)
- _Der Tagesspiegel_ (Berlin's reference newspaper)
- Rowohlt and S. Fischer (two of Germany's most prestigious literary publishers)
- Macmillan Publishers (the British publisher, bought in 1995)
- Nature Publishing Group (the _Nature_ scientific journals)
- Scientific American

But ambition had a price: debt. In 2002, the family divided the group into two branches. The Dieter von Holtzbrinck branch (DvH Medien) kept the German newspapers and magazines: _Die Zeit_, _Handelsblatt_, _Tagesspiegel_, _Wirtschaftswoche_. The Stefan von Holtzbrinck branch (Verlagsgruppe Georg von Holtzbrinck) kept the book publishers —S. Fischer, Rowohlt, Kiepenheuer & Witsch, Droemer Knaur— and the international business, including Macmillan.

In 2008, Stefan von Holtzbrinck bought his siblings' shares and unified control. Dieter von Holtzbrinck —the elder brother— retained ownership of the newspapers through DvH Medien GmbH. The division persists to this day.

---

## 2009 — Reinhard Mohn dies

Reinhard Mohn died at 88. He left behind an empire of over 100,000 employees, present in 50 countries, with interests in television (RTL Group), publishing (Penguin Random House, the world's largest book publisher), magazines (Gruner + Jahr), services (Arvato), and music (BMG).

Control remained in the hands of his widow, Liz Mohn (b. 1941), and the Bertelsmann Stiftung. Liz Mohn chairs the foundation's board and the group's supervisory board. Her children —Christoph and Brigitte Mohn— hold executive positions. The power structure has not changed since 1977: the foundation owns the shares, the Mohn family controls the foundation.

---

## 2013–2014 — Funke buys Springer's regional dailies

In July 2013, Axel Springer SE announced the sale of its regional dailies and television programming magazines to Funke Mediengruppe for 920 million euros. The package included:
- _Berliner Morgenpost_ (Berlin's morning daily)
- _Hamburger Abendblatt_ (the newspaper Springer had founded in 1948)
- _Bergedorfer Zeitung_
- The magazines _Hörzu_, _Bild der Frau_, _TV Digital_, _Frau von heute_

It was the largest transfer of journalistic assets in German post-war history. Springer —under the leadership of Mathias Döpfner— was shedding local newspapers to concentrate on _Bild_, _Die Welt_, and the digital classifieds business (StepStone, Avrios, etc.). Funke, a family business from Essen, became one of Germany's largest regional press publishers.

---

### Funke Mediengruppe: the discreet giant

Funke Mediengruppe is not a known name outside Germany. It has no national tabloid like _Bild_, no investigative magazine like _Stern_, no intellectual weekly like _Die Zeit_. But in terms of regional newspapers, it is Germany's third-largest group.

Its origins go back to the _Westdeutsche Allgemeine Zeitung_ (WAZ), founded in Essen in 1948 by Erich Brost —a Social Democrat who had fled Nazism— and Jakob Funke —a Catholic journalist. The WAZ became the dominant newspaper of the Ruhr basin, one of Europe's most densely populated regions.

For decades, the Funke family retained control. Jakob Funke died in 1975. His widow, Liselotte Funke, took over management. In 2000, Brost's heirs sold their stake to the Funke family. The WAZ-Gruppe, now entirely in the hands of the Funkes, was renamed Funke Mediengruppe in 2012.

Under the leadership of Julia Becker —daughter of Petra Grotkamp and publisher Günther Grotkamp, granddaughter of Jakob Funke— Funke today owns a portfolio of over 30 regional newspapers, 70 magazines, and numerous digital portals. The Funke family holds 100% of the capital. Not listed on the stock exchange. Does not publish detailed results. It is the archetype of the German provincial publisher who built an empire without making noise.

---

## 2015 — Friede Springer, widow and heiress

Friede Springer (b. 1942) met Axel Springer in the 1960s, when she worked as a nanny in his house. They married in 1978. She was 30 years younger than him. When Axel died in 1985, Friede inherited a majority stake in the group.

For years, Friede Springer was treated condescendingly by the German publishing world: the nanny who married the boss. But she proved to be a shrewd owner. She did not try to run the newspapers —delegated to professionals— but maintained iron control over strategic decisions. In 2002, she appointed Mathias Döpfner as CEO. It was the most important decision of her tenure.

---

### Mathias Döpfner: the power behind the throne

Mathias Döpfner (b. 1963) holds a doctorate in musicology from Goethe University Frankfurt. He started as a journalist at the _Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung_ (1990–1992), then was editor-in-chief of _Wochenpost_ and _Hamburger Morgenpost_. In 1998 he became editor-in-chief of _Die Welt_.

In 2002, Friede Springer appointed him CEO of Axel Springer AG. Döpfner was 39 years old. Since then, he has transformed the group —from a newspaper conglomerate into a digital classifieds technology company— while maintaining the conservative editorial line of _Bild_ and _Die Welt_.

In 2019, Döpfner signed an agreement with KKR, the US investment fund, to delist Axel Springer SE from the stock exchange. KKR bought 44.9% of the capital. Friede Springer and Mathias Döpfner kept the remaining 55.1% — 45.4% through the Friede Springer Foundation and 9.7% for Döpfner as personal owner.

The resulting structure is a hybrid: a German foundation and a US vulture fund share control of Germany's largest press group.

---

## 2018 — Yvonne Bauer, fifth generation

Heinrich Bauer Verlag is today Germany's largest magazine publishing group and one of the largest in the world. The company has reported revenues of over 2.3 billion euros in recent times. It has a presence in 20 countries. Its portfolio includes more than 500 magazines.

Since 2010, the company has been led by Yvonne Bauer (b. 1977), great-granddaughter of the founder and fifth generation of the family. She studied business administration in Germany and France, and worked at the publisher from the age of 22. She is one of Germany's richest and most discreet women: rarely gives interviews, does not frequent the social circles of Munich or Berlin, and keeps the group's headquarters in Hamburg, away from the media spotlight.

The Bauer family owns 100% of the company. No shares on the stock exchange, no external partners, no foundations diluting control. It is the purest and most direct ownership in the entire German media landscape.

---

## 2019 — KKR enters Axel Springer

The agreement between Friede Springer, Mathias Döpfner, and KKR, announced in 2019 and closed in 2020, valued Axel Springer SE at 6.7 billion euros. KKR paid 3 billion for a 44.9% stake. The rest remained in the hands of Friede Springer (through her foundation) and Mathias Döpfner.

KKR —Kohlberg Kravis Roberts— is one of the most aggressive investment funds in the world, famous for the leveraged buyout of RJR Nabisco in 1988 that inspired the book and film _Barbarians at the Gate_. Its entry into Springer set off alarms in German journalism: a US vulture fund controlling _Bild_ and _Die Welt_? In the deal, KKR received shares without majority voting rights: editorial and strategic control remains with Döpfner and Friede Springer. But KKR has financial rights: it wants returns. And returns in a media group are obtained in two ways: cutting costs or increasing digital revenues. Both put pressure on journalism.

The same KKR had been the owner of ProSiebenSat.1 —Germany's second-largest private television group— between 2007 and 2015, meaning that the US fund has controlled, at two different times, both the largest press group and the second-largest commercial television network in the country.

This exposes a gap in German media regulation. The KEK (*Kommission zur Ermittlung der Konzentration im Medienbereich*) exists precisely to prevent a single actor from simultaneously controlling press and television. The problem is that the KEK evaluates **simultaneous** control, not sequential. KKR was not the owner of ProSiebenSat.1 and Axel Springer at the same time: it sold one before buying the other. But from the perspective of structural power over the German information ecosystem, the result is the same: a single US private equity fund has exercised effective control over the largest press group and the second-largest television group in the country, just at different times. It is a regulatory loophole that no German legislator has closed, and that investment funds have learned to exploit: rotate between sectors so as not to trigger concentration thresholds, accumulating influence without accumulating simultaneous ownership.

---

## 2021 — Gruner + Jahr is dissolved within Bertelsmann

After years of losses, Bertelsmann dissolved Gruner + Jahr as an independent entity. The surviving magazines —_Stern_, _Brigitte_, _Geo_, _Capital_, _Schöner Wohnen_, _Eltern_— were integrated into RTL Deutschland, the group's television division. The name Gruner + Jahr disappeared from the organizational chart after 56 years.

_Stern_, the magazine that in 1983 published the fake Hitler diaries and in the 1960s personified progressive German journalism, became one more business line within Bertelsmann's television arm.

## 2024 — The landscape: who controls what

As of 2024, the German print press market is divided among six groups:

### Axel Springer SE (Berlin)
Ownership: Friede Springer and Mathias Döpfner (~55%), KKR (~45%)
- _Bild_ (daily tabloid, Germany's highest circulation)
- _Bild am Sonntag_ (Sunday edition)
- _Die Welt_ (conservative quality daily)
- _Welt am Sonntag_ (Sunday edition)
- _Business Insider_ (digital native, United States and Germany)
- _Politico_ (bought in 2021)
- Digital classifieds platforms (StepStone, Avrios)

Editorial line: Market conservatism, Atlanticism, support for Israel, critique of immigration, frequent climate skepticism.

### Bertelsmann SE & Co. KGaA (Gütersloh)
Control: Bertelsmann Stiftung → Mohn family
- RTL Group (Europe's largest private television group)
- Penguin Random House (world's largest book publisher)
- RTL Deutschland includes the former Gruner + Jahr magazines: _Stern_, _Geo_, _Brigitte_, _Capital_, _Schöner Wohnen_, _Eltern_
- BMG (music)
- Arvato (services)

Editorial line: Variable depending on the medium. _Stern_ maintains a progressive and investigative tone. RTL's media are commercially conservative. The group's general line is pragmatic center, but the foundation —with its network of political influence— exercises a silent institutional conservatism.

### Verlagsgruppe Georg von Holtzbrinck / DvH Medien (Stuttgart)
Control: von Holtzbrinck family
- _Die Zeit_ (leading liberal weekly, 50% stake)
- _Handelsblatt_ (main business newspaper)
- _Wirtschaftswoche_ (business weekly)
- _Der Tagesspiegel_ (Berlin daily, DvH Medien)
- Literary publishers: S. Fischer, Rowohlt, Kiepenheuer & Witsch, Droemer Knaur
- Macmillan Publishers (United Kingdom)
- Nature Portfolio (scientific journals)

The CEO of Die Zeit, Rainer Esser, sits on the Kuratorium of the Krupp Foundation, which controls 20.93% of ThyssenKrupp and 10% of TKMS —Germany's largest submarine builder— thus connecting the country's most influential liberal weekly to the heart of the German military-industrial complex.

Editorial line: _Die Zeit_ is socially left-liberal, economically pro-market. _Handelsblatt_ is liberal-conservative, pro-business. _Der Tagesspiegel_ is center-left. The group does not impose a single line: each medium has editorial independence.

### Funke Mediengruppe (Essen)
Control: Julia Becker (granddaughter of co-founder Jakob Funke)
- _Berliner Morgenpost_
- _Hamburger Abendblatt_
- _Westdeutsche Allgemeine Zeitung_ (WAZ)
- _Neue Ruhr/Rhein Zeitung_
- _Westfalenpost_
- _Thüringer Allgemeine_
- _Ostthüringer Zeitung_
- _Ostsee-Zeitung_
- _Lübecker Nachrichten_
- Numerous magazines (_Hörzu_, _Bild der Frau_, _TV Digital_, etc.)

Editorial line: Pragmatic center, with regional variations. The WAZ maintains a Social Democratic tradition. The newspapers inherited from Springer maintain more conservative lines. Funke does not systematically interfere in each publication's line.

### Heinrich Bauer Verlag (Hamburg)
Control: Yvonne Bauer
- TV magazines: _TV Movie_, _TV Hören und Sehen_, _Auf einen Blick_
- Women's magazines: _Bella_, _Laura_
- Gossip magazines: _Das Neue Blatt_, _Neue Post_
- Youth magazines: _Bravo_, _Mädchen_
- Stakes in radio and television (United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand)

Editorial line: No explicit political line. Bauer manufactures mass entertainment. Its influence is cultural, not direct: it defines what millions of Germans read in the supermarket checkout line.

### Fazit-Stiftung / Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung (Frankfurt)
Control: Fazit-Stiftung (non-profit foundation)
- _Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung_ (FAZ, quality daily)
- _Frankfurter Allgemeine Sonntagszeitung_ (Sunday edition)
- _FAZ Quarterly_
- Digital editions and supplements

Editorial line: Liberal-conservative, pro-market economy, pro-European, Atlanticist. The FAZ is the reference newspaper of the German bourgeoisie: executives, senior civil servants, academics, judges. Its economics section and its _Feuilleton_ (cultural supplement) are national institutions.

## The concentration in figures

According to the most recent report of the KEK (Kommission zur Ermittlung der Konzentration im Medienbereich, 2023), the five largest press groups controlled more than 60% of the German market for general-interest magazines and more than 55% of the market for paid newspapers.

In the television programming magazine segment, Bauer and Funke divide practically everything between them. In women's magazines, Bauer and Bertelsmann (via the former Gruner + Jahr titles) dominate more than 70% of the market. In national quality dailies, the FAZ, _Die Welt_ (Springer), and _Süddeutsche Zeitung_ (independent, owned by Südwestdeutsche Medienholding of Stuttgart) share the elite, but only the FAZ has truly national reach. In popular dailies, Springer's _Bild_ has no competitor.

The local daily press market, on the other hand, is fragmented: over 300 titles published by dozens of regional groups. But even here concentration advances: Funke, Madsack (Hannover), DuMont (Cologne), and Südwestdeutsche Medienholding control the majority of significant titles. The Otto Brenner Stiftung, IG Metall's media research arm, is a key source for the documented monitoring of this concentration.

## The office cartel: hidden interdependencies

A peculiarity of the German system rarely discussed outside academic circles is what researchers call _Redaktionsgemeinschaften_ or the "office cartel." Several newspapers share content, supplements, correspondent bureaus, and even entire sections through cooperation agreements.

The most relevant case is _HA Hessen_ (Redaktionsgemeinschaft Hessen), where the _Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung_ produces content published in dozens of regional newspapers in Hesse and bordering regions. In practice, the FAZ is not only a national daily: it is also a private news agency that feeds its editorial line to titles that are nominally competing with each other.

Another example: _Die Zeit_ and _Handelsblatt_ —both from the Holtzbrinck/DvH group— share content with numerous regional dailies through syndication agreements. The reader of Berlin's _Tagesspiegel_ and the reader of Düsseldorf's _Handelsblatt_ read economic analyses by the same writer, produced by the same company, even though the publications appear independent.

## Foundations and politics: the blurred boundary

Three of the six groups —Bertelsmann, Fazit-Stiftung, and (partially) Springer through the Friede Springer Foundation— use non-profit foundation structures. This has fiscal and political consequences.

The Bertelsmann Stiftung is the most extreme case. With over 400 employees and an annual budget of over 100 million euros, it is not a foundation that simply pays out scholarships: it is a de facto think tank that finances research, organizes conferences, publishes reports, and advises governments. Its influence on German labor market reforms (Schröder's Agenda 2010), educational policies, and healthcare reforms is documented by researchers such as Thomas Schuler (_Die Mohns_, 2004) and Frank Böckelmann (_Bertelsmann: Hinter der Fassade des Medienimperiums_, 2004). The Stiftung's research directly shaped Agenda 2010 — the reform that broke the historic alliance between the SPD and the unions and cost the DGB over a million members in the following decade.

The foundation has privileged access to centers of power: its executives rotate between the foundation's board, Bertelsmann's supervisory board, and the advisory boards of federal ministries. Liz Mohn, Reinhard's widow, received the Commander's Cross of the Order of Merit of the Federal Republic of Germany and is a regular figure at state ceremonies.

The Fazit-Stiftung is more discreet but equally intertwined with the establishment. Its trustees have included former Bundesbank presidents, former finance ministers, presidents of the Federation of German Industry (BDI), and directors of Deutsche Bank. The same central banking elite —ex-Bundesbank, ex-ECB— that populates the FAZ's board of trustees is also found in the Kuratorium of the Krupp Foundation: Sabine Lautenschläger, former member of the ECB executive board, is only the most visible link in a constellation of interests connecting Germany's quality press to the heart of the Ruhr industrial complex. The FAZ's Stiftungsrat, the Krupp-Stiftung's Kuratorium (text 2201), the ZDF's Fernsehrat (text 2205), and the Aufsichtsrat under Mitbestimmung (text 2206) share the same architecture: appointed, not elected, bodies without electoral accountability. The FAZ reflects the interests and worldview of its financiers, not because anyone dictates the editorials, but because the newspaper —its line, its columnists, its priorities— has been immersed in that ecosystem for 75 years.

## The German paradox

Germany has more newspapers per capita than almost any other European country. Its media antitrust laws —administered by the KEK— are stricter than those of France, Italy, or the United Kingdom. And yet, six entities control the majority of what Germans read every day.

The paradox is explained by the ownership structure. German giants are not television chains that buy newspapers, as in Italy (Berlusconi) or France (Bolloré, Bouygues). They are families or foundations that have maintained control for generations, accumulating titles slowly, without drawing attention, shielded by legal structures that do not require transparency.

Bauer is the same family since 1875. Holtzbrinck since 1893. Funke since 1948. Springer (via Friede) since 1946. Bertelsmann (via Mohn foundation) since 1835. The FAZ (via establishment foundation) since 1949.

None of these empires was built through hostile takeovers or aggressive stock market operations. They were built through decades of patient reinvestment, carefully cultivated political relationships, and foundations that shielded the assets from the tax authorities and from wayward heirs.

The result is a media market where competition exists —_Die Zeit_ competes with _Stern_ competes with _FAZ_ competes with _Welt_— but where ownership is so concentrated and so opaque that the reader has no way of knowing, upon opening their newspaper, which family or foundational structure ultimately decides what they are reading.
