# German Defence Industry — Trade Unions, Submarines and the Zeitenwende

---

## 1838 — Howaldtswerke is born in Kiel

On 1 October 1838, the engineer August Howaldt founded the Maschinenbauanstalt und Eisengießerei Schweffel & Howaldt in Kiel. What began as a steam engine workshop on the shores of the Kiel Fjord would, over time, become Germany's most strategic naval shipyard. The other root of that tradition in the same city was Friedrich Alfred Krupp, who bought the Germania Werft shipyards in Kiel in 1896 and consolidated them under full control in 1902 — precisely for submarine construction.

In 1850 it launched its first steamship, the *Vorwärts*. In 1879 it built its first warship for the Kaiserliche Marine. During the First World War it produced submarines. During the Second, its plant was one of the Allied forces' most bombed targets. In 1968 it merged with Deutsche Werft to form Howaldtswerke-Deutsche Werft (HDW). Today HDW is the crown jewel of TKMS: the place where the Type 212 and 214 submarines are built, the quietest in the world, with air-independent propulsion (AIP) technology using hydrogen fuel cells.

---

## 1891 — Founding of the Deutscher Metallarbeiter-Verband (DMV)

On 1 June 1891 the Deutscher Metallarbeiter-Verband was founded in Frankfurt, the direct predecessor organisation of today's IG Metall. It grouped metalworkers — smiths, turners, mechanics, founders — in the midst of Germany's industrial boom. In its early decades under the Kaiserreich, the DMV was systematically excluded from arms factories by decision of the industrialists. Friedrich Krupp had banned all trade union activity in his plants since 1872 with the *Generalregulativ*. Steel and cannon workers laboured under a regime of absolute obedience: social protection in exchange for political submission. Arms production and workers' organisation were incompatible by design.

The DMV grew despite everything. By 1914 it had 550,000 members. It survived the First World War, the Revolution of 1918, the hyperinflation of 1923, the Great Depression. And it was destroyed in 1933, along with the rest of the free trade unions, when the Nazis occupied their headquarters and imprisoned their leaders.

---

## 1949 — IG Metall is born

After the Second World War, German trade unions were rebuilt under a radically new principle: the *Einheitsgewerkschaft*, the unified trade union. There would no longer be social democratic, communist, Christian or liberal trade unions competing with each other — as had happened during the Weimar Republic, with fatal results against Nazism. One single trade union for all metalworkers, without distinction of party or confession.

IG Metall was founded in 1949 and began operating from Frankfurt in 1950. In its founding statutes there was not a word about the defence industry. But the HDW shipyards, the Thyssen factories, the Krupp steelworks — all were within its organising remit. The question was not whether IG Metall would negotiate wages in arms plants, but how it would reconcile its progressive and pacifist identity with the reality that tens of thousands of its members manufactured tanks, cannons and submarines.

The answer, for decades, was a tacit pact: IG Metall organised the sector's workers without publicly championing the defence industry. It negotiated wages, conditions and hours in military shipyards and arms factories as in any other sector, but avoided speaking out in favour of military spending. At its congresses, the pacifist motions always won. In the factories, the trade union delegates defended the jobs. The schizophrenia was stable because no one made it explicit.

---

## 1956 — The Bundeswehr and post-war rearmament

In 1955, the Federal Republic of Germany joined NATO. In 1956 the Bundeswehr was founded. German industry — including the Kiel shipyards — returned to producing armaments, this time for the Western alliance. IG Metall did not frontally oppose rearmament, but maintained an ambiguous position: tacit support for jobs, explicit criticism of the arms race.

During the *Wirtschaftswunder* years, defence production was a smaller fraction of the German metalworking industry. The shipyards built both merchant and military vessels. Krupp's factories produced steel for bridges and for armour. The ambiguity was sustainable because defence did not dominate the order book.

---

## 1979 — NATO's Double-Track Decision

On 12 December 1979, NATO adopted the *Double-Track Decision* (*NATO-Doppelbeschluss*): to deploy 572 intermediate-range Pershing II and Tomahawk missiles in Western Europe — 108 of them on German soil — while simultaneously offering to negotiate their withdrawal with the Soviet Union.

The decision triggered the largest peace movement in German history. Hundreds of thousands took to the streets. The movement cut across churches, universities, political parties and, especially intensely, the trade unions. IG Metall officially positioned itself against the deployment. The trade union argument was twofold: first, the arms race diverted resources that should be allocated to social policies; second, the stationing of nuclear missiles on German soil turned the country into a powder keg.

---

## 1981 — The IG Metall congress and the pacifist turn

At its 1981 congress in Essen, IG Metall passed a historic resolution against the arms race and the deployment of Pershing II missiles. The trade union actively joined the mass demonstrations of the peace movement. Trade union delegates from military shipyards and arms factories voted in favour of the resolution, in what seemed an irreconcilable contradiction between their political identity and their labour reality. Delegates from those same military shipyards — which today build TKMS submarines — voted for the pacifist resolution; it was a contradiction that the 2022 Zeitenwende would definitively break.

The German peace movement of the 1980s was unique in Europe for its scale and breadth. On 10 October 1981, 300,000 people demonstrated in Bonn. On 10 June 1982, during the visit of President Ronald Reagan, half a million. On 22 October 1983, after the Bundestag's decision to approve deployment, 1.3 million people formed human chains and blocked military bases. It was the Germany of the *Friedensbewegung*, of "NATO NO", of the slogan *Frieden schaffen ohne Waffen* ("create peace without weapons").

---

## 1984 — The 35-hour week strike and silence on defence

In 1984, IG Metall waged the toughest strike in its history: seven weeks of walkouts and lockouts for the 35-hour working week. The strike was a partial success — a reduction to 38.5 hours was agreed — and consolidated IG Metall as the most powerful trade union in Europe.

During the strike, the defence sector was not a specific battleground. Military shipyard and arms factory workers participated like the rest. IG Metall was, above all, an industrial trade union: it organised all metalworkers, without distinguishing between civilian and military products. The contradiction remained unresolved because the *Ostpolitik* of détente and the nuclear balance of the Cold War kept it frozen.

---

## 1989–1990 — The fall of the Wall and the "peace dividend"

With the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 and German reunification in 1990, the German defence industry entered a crisis. The "peace dividend" (*Friedensdividende*) translated into massive cuts in military spending. The Bundeswehr was reduced from 585,000 personnel in 1990 to fewer than 250,000 in 2000. Arms orders collapsed. Shipyards like HDW in Kiel fought to survive by diversifying into merchant shipbuilding and luxury yachts.

IG Metall experienced these cuts with ambivalence. On the one hand, its pacifist discourse was validated: fewer weapons, more social spending. On the other hand, every cut meant layoffs in its strongholds. Between 1990 and 2005, employment in the German defence industry fell from 280,000 to fewer than 80,000 jobs. The contradiction between discourse and labour reality worsened, but remained unresolved because German military spending was declining and marginal. It was simply not a priority issue.

---

## 2005 — ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems (TKMS) is born

In 2005, ThyssenKrupp AG reorganised its naval activities and created ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems, grouping under a single entity:

- **Howaldtswerke-Deutsche Werft (HDW)** in Kiel — Germany's most prestigious submarine builder
- **Blohm+Voss** in Hamburg — frigates and surface vessels
- **Nordseewerke** in Emden — civilian and military vessels
- **Kockums** in Sweden (acquired in 1999, sold to Saab in 2014)
- **Hellenic Shipyards** in Greece (sold in 2010)

TKMS brought together more than 170 years of naval tradition, an export submarine portfolio with no real competition in the non-nuclear market — the Type 209, 212, 214 — and a dominant position in the frigate segment with the MEKO family. The company employed around 8,000 workers. The majority, IG Metall members.

---

## 2014 — Crimea: the first wake-up call

In March 2014, Russia annexed Crimea. It was the first forcible border violation in Europe since 1945. The German reaction was ambivalent: economic sanctions, yes; an increase in military spending, no. Chancellor Angela Merkel, in her speech to the Bundestag on 13 March 2014, spoke of a "violation of international law", but did not propose a significant increase in the defence budget.

IG Metall maintained its traditional line. At its 2015 congress, it passed a resolution reaffirming its commitment to disarmament and peace. The trade union publicly supported sanctions against Russia but rejected any rearmament. The Crimean invasion did not break the taboo. It barely cracked it. In the Kiel shipyards, workers continued building submarines. The IG Metall leadership continued talking about peace. The gap between the base and the leadership widened without anyone measuring it.

---

## 2015 — Oliver Burkhard crosses the line

In 2015, Oliver Burkhard (born 1972 in Duisburg) took a step that few in the history of German trade unionism had taken: he left his post as Bezirksleiter (regional director) of IG Metall Nordrhein-Westfalen — the union's largest district, with more than 600,000 members in the Ruhr basin — to join ThyssenKrupp AG as Chief Human Resources Officer (CHRO) and Arbeitsdirektor (labour director).

The step was not unprecedented — the German co-determination system (*Mitbestimmung*) provides for large companies to have an Arbeitsdirektor on their Vorstand, traditionally close to the trade unions — but it was exceptional. Burkhard was not just any trade union delegate. He was a front-line IG Metall cadre, trained at the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung trade union school, seasoned in collective bargaining and labour disputes. His signing by ThyssenKrupp was interpreted as a defensive move by CEO Heinrich Hiesinger to shore up labour relations at a time of profound restructuring of the group.

Burkhard, an SPD member since his youth, represented an increasingly rare profile in 21st-century Germany: the class defector who crosses to the other side of the table without entirely losing their original loyalties. At ThyssenKrupp, his mission was to manage workforce reduction without fracturing social peace. He achieved it with a style that his former trade union colleagues would describe as "correct but distant".

Since his arrival at TKMS as CEO in 2022, Burkhard has maintained his seat on the board of trustees of the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung — the DGB's foundation dedicated to co-determination research. He sits simultaneously at the table of the intellectual arm of organised trade unionism and at the helm of Germany's largest naval defence contractor — and the Krupp Foundation, which controls 10% directly of TKMS in addition to 20.93% of ThyssenKrupp AG, connects both ends of his career. The same foundation finances the co-determination research that empowers trade unions on supervisory boards (text 2206) and, through the Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung — financed by the same trade unions — trains the cadres who decades later occupy the Fernsehrat of ZDF (text 2205). One foundation, three sectors, zero recognition of the circuit. The Krupp Foundation, meanwhile, controls 10% directly of TKMS in addition to 20.93% of ThyssenKrupp AG.

## 2017 — The Bundeswehr, "in a lamentable state"

In 2017, the annual report of the Bundestag Commissioner for the Armed Forces, Hans-Peter Bartels, described the Bundeswehr as a force "in a lamentable state" (*im erbärmlichen Zustand*). Only 30% of Leopard 2 tanks were operational. Fewer than half of the Tiger helicopters could fly. All six Type 212 submarines of the German Navy were in dry dock for maintenance.

The anecdote that symbolised the decade was that of the *G36*, the standard assault rifle of the German infantry: it was proven to lose accuracy when heated, but the Ministry of Defence had been denying the reports for years. Germany spent around 1.2% of GDP on defence — well below NATO's 2% target — and much of that spending went to personnel and pensions, not equipment. Merkel — who in 2009 had remained silent while CDU politician Roland Koch vetoed the ZDF editor-in-chief — maintained the same pattern of non-intervention in defence, allowing the Bundeswehr to atrophy for a decade.

IG Metall did not have an official position on the operational readiness of Leopard tanks. But the degradation of the Bundeswehr was also the degradation of Germany's defence industrial base. Fewer orders meant less work in shipyards and metalworking plants. The trade union delegates at HDW, at Rheinmetall, at Krauss-Maffei Wegmann knew this perfectly. The union leadership preferred not to talk about it.

---

## 24 February 2022 — Russia invades Ukraine

At dawn on 24 February 2022, Russian tanks crossed the Ukrainian border from Belarus, Crimea and the Donbas. Missiles struck Kyiv, Kharkiv, Odessa and Mariupol. It was the first large-scale war of invasion on European soil since 1945.

Chancellor Olaf Scholz, barely two and a half months in office, cancelled his weekend schedule. On Saturday 26 February, the German government announced the shipment of 1,000 Panzerfaust 3 anti-tank rocket launchers and 500 Stinger missiles to Ukraine — the first time in the history of the Federal Republic that Germany sent lethal weapons to an active war zone.

For IG Metall, 24 February was an earthquake. The trade union issued a statement condemning the invasion and expressing solidarity with Ukraine. What it did not say — and what everyone expected it to say — was what position it would now take on German rearmament. The answer came three days later, from the Bundestag.

---

## 27 February 2022 — Scholz's Zeitenwende

On Sunday 27 February 2022, in an extraordinary session of the Bundestag, Olaf Scholz delivered the speech that redefined German defence policy for a generation:

> *"Der 24. Februar 2022 markiert eine Zeitenwende in der Geschichte unseres Kontinents."*
>
> — "24 February 2022 marks a turning point in the history of our continent."

Scholz announced five measures:
1. **Sondervermögen Bundeswehr**: a special fund of €100 billion, to be approved by the Bundestag and enshrined in the Grundgesetz (Constitution), for investments in military equipment.
2. **Permanent commitment of 2% of GDP**: Germany would invest "year after year" more than 2% of its gross domestic product in defence.
3. **Arms shipments to Ukraine**: for the first time, German lethal weapons to a war zone.
4. **Reinforcement of NATO's eastern flank**: more troops in Lithuania, Slovakia and Romania.
5. **Energy independence**: acceleration of renewables and construction of LNG terminals to decouple from Russian gas.

The word *Zeitenwende* — literally "turning of the times", a term that in German evokes both the French Revolution and the end of the Cold War — immediately entered the global political vocabulary. In 2022 it was chosen as "Word of the Year" by the Gesellschaft für deutsche Sprache. Scholz — who as Finance Minister had clashed with ver.di and IG Metall over housing policy — was now guaranteeing the defence sector members of those same trade unions job security for a generation.

---

## March 2022 — IG Metall's dilemma: the grass roots apply pressure

In the weeks following the Zeitenwende, the trade union grass roots in the defence industry began to mobilise. The works councils (*Betriebsräte*) of TKMS in Kiel, of Rheinmetall in Unterlüß, of KMW in Munich, demanded clarity from the IG Metall leadership. The argument was pragmatic and direct: if Germany was going to spend €100 billion on equipping the Bundeswehr, those industrial contracts meant work — and well-paid work, under the metalworking collective agreement, with dual training and with pensions.

The dilemma was real. IG Metall membership in the defence sector was high — at HDW it was around 70%. The trade union delegates had spent years defending jobs in a sector stigmatised by their own union leadership. The Russian invasion had changed the moral frame of reference: it was no longer about exporting weapons to dubious regimes, but about defending an attacked European democracy.

The IG Metall leadership, headed since 2015 by Jörg Hofmann — a moderate trade unionist from Baden-Württemberg, shaped by the pragmatic tradition of southwestern Germany — convened internal consultations. The grass roots could not be ignored. But neither could forty years of pacifist identity be thrown overboard.

---

## April 2022 — "Aufrüstung vs Ausrüstung": the semantic battle

In the spring of 2022, the German public debate crystallised around two words: *Aufrüstung* (rearmament) and *Ausrüstung* (equipment). The difference was semantic but loaded with history. *Aufrüstung* evoked the arms race, the First World War, Nazi rearmament of the 1930s, the Doppelbeschluss. *Ausrüstung* evoked equipping the Bundeswehr with the necessary equipment to fulfil its constitutional defence mandate.

The Scholz government, formed by the SPD, Greens and FDP, carefully adopted the term *Ausrüstung*. Defence Minister Christine Lambrecht (SPD) spoke of "closing capability gaps" (*Fähigkeitslücken schließen*). Economy Minister Robert Habeck (Greens) openly defended arms deliveries to Ukraine.

IG Metall aligned itself with the government on semantics but went further in substance. In an internal document leaked to Handelsblatt in April 2022, the trade union acknowledged that "the defence of democracy may require military industrial capabilities" and that "jobs in the defence industry are qualified industrial jobs like any other". It was a 180-degree turn from previous congress resolutions, but it was not communicated as such: it was presented as an "update" (*Aktualisierung*) of positions.

---

## May 2022 — Oliver Burkhard appointed CEO of TKMS

In May 2022, ThyssenKrupp AG announced the appointment of Oliver Burkhard as CEO of ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems, with immediate effect. The decision was strategic: TKMS needed a credible interlocutor with the trade unions and works councils at a time when the Zeitenwende promised an avalanche of orders that would require increasing production and probably the workforce. No one was better placed to lead that expansion without labour conflicts than a former IG Metall functionary.

Burkhard, aged fifty, became the first former IG Metall trade unionist to run a major German arms manufacturer. The news was received with irony in trade union circles: "The hunter has become the hunted", commented an HDW delegate. But the irony concealed a deeper reality: Burkhard's move was the personal expression of a structural shift. The Zeitenwende had blurred the line between trade unionism and defence industry management. What was once betrayal was now transition.

Burkhard told the Financial Times in June 2022: "We don't build offensive weapons. We build defence systems for democracies. A Type 212 submarine does not invade countries: it protects coastlines." The distinction between offensive and defensive weapons was, at bottom, another semantic battle — a submarine can be used to blockade ports as much as to defend them — but it was a distinction that allowed the German left to reconcile itself with arms production without completely revising its identity.

---

## June 2022 — The Bundestag approves the Sondervermögen

On 3 June 2022, the Bundestag approved the constitutional amendment creating the Sondervermögen of €100 billion for the Bundeswehr. The vote required a two-thirds majority: 567 votes in favour, 96 against, 20 abstentions. The CDU/CSU, by then already in opposition, supported the measure after negotiating that the funds be destined "exclusively for the Bundeswehr" and not for generic defence projects.

On the same day, TKMS received official confirmation from the German government for the construction of two additional Type 212 CD (Common Design) submarines for the German Navy, as part of a joint order with Norway. The contract, valued at €5.5 billion, was the largest submarine order in the company's history.

---

## October 2023 — Christiane Benner, first woman to lead IG Metall

In October 2023, Christiane Benner was elected Erste Vorsitzende (president) of IG Metall, becoming the first woman to lead Europe's largest trade union in its 132-year history. Benner, a sociologist by training, came from the progressive tradition of the union — gender equality, digitalisation, ecological transition — and was expected to maintain the traditional pacifist line.

That was not exactly how it went. In her first speech as president, Benner said: "Peace does not defend itself. Defence industry workers are workers like any other. Our task is to ensure they have good wages, good conditions and a voice in the transformation." The sentence went unnoticed by the general public, but in the German trade union universe it was a bombshell: it was the first time the top IG Metall leader explicitly equated defence workers with the rest of the membership. Weeks earlier, Benner had made history as the first woman to chair IG Metall in 132 years, lending her endorsement of the defence industry the weight of an institutional milestone.

---

## December 2023 — TKMS's order book explodes

At the end of 2023, TKMS reported an order book of €15 billion — more than double that of 2021. The contracts included:

- Type 212 CD for Germany and Norway (€5.5 billion)
- MEKO A-200 frigates for Egypt
- Type 214 submarines for Greece and South Korea
- K130 corvettes for the German Navy
- Modernisation of Type 209 submarines for various customers

The company announced the hiring of 1,200 new employees in Kiel and Hamburg. IG Metall, through the works councils, negotiated that all new hires would be made under the metalworking sector-wide collective agreement — the *Flächentarifvertrag* — with salaries ranging from €45,000 to €72,000 per year for skilled workers.

---

## March 2024 — The Bundestag debates the future of TKMS

In March 2024, the Bundestag debated the future of TKMS in a session dedicated to Germany's defence industrial base. The debate pitted two visions against each other:

- The Greens and part of the SPD argued that TKMS should remain under public control or at least under a German consortium, to prevent strategic submarine technology from falling into foreign hands. They proposed a "European solution" with participation of Rheinmetall, KMW and the German state through KfW (public development bank).
- The CDU/CSU and FDP argued for an IPO as the most transparent option and the only one that would guarantee access to the capital needed to expand production. They argued that a state-controlled "national champion" was an anachronism.

Oliver Burkhard, appearing before the Defence Committee, defended a third way: an IPO in which the Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach-Stiftung would retain a reference stake of 10%, the rest would be placed on the market, and the company would keep its headquarters and production in Germany. The model was Rheinmetall: a listed company, German, profitable, without direct state control.

---

## September 2024 — ThyssenKrupp approves the spin-off of TKMS

On 20 September 2024, the supervisory board of ThyssenKrupp AG formally approved the demerger plan (*Abspaltung*) of TKMS. The operation consisted of:

1. Separating TKMS as an independent legal entity.
2. Placing up to 49% of the capital on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange through an initial public offering (IPO).
3. ThyssenKrupp AG would initially retain a minority stake that would be progressively reduced.
4. The Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach-Stiftung — which already controlled 20.93% of ThyssenKrupp AG — would receive 10% directly of TKMS as part of the reorganisation.

The IG Metall trade union, with representation on the supervisory board of ThyssenKrupp (four of the ten worker seats), voted in favour of the plan. The condition was a job guarantee agreement (*Beschäftigungssicherung*) until 2030 for all TKMS sites in Germany and the commitment to maintain the metalworking collective agreements. Burkhard, as CEO and former trade unionist, was the architect of the deal.

---

## February 2025 — Germany spends 2.12% of GDP on defence

For the first time since the end of the Cold War, Germany exceeded the 2% of GDP threshold for defence spending set by NATO. NATO data showed spending of 2.12% in 2024, compared to 1.57% in 2022 and 1.51% in 2021.

The Sondervermögen had injected approximately €40 billion in contracts by February 2025. Of that, TKMS had captured contracts worth €8.2 billion — submarines, frigates, system modernisation — making it one of the main industrial beneficiaries of the Zeitenwende.

---

## June 2025 — "Aufrüstung vs Ausrüstung": the debate reopens

In June 2025, with the Ukraine war entering its fourth year and no prospects of a ceasefire, the German debate on military spending took a new turn. The CDU/CSU, led by Friedrich Merz, began to speak openly of *Aufrüstung* — no longer just of equipping the Bundeswehr, but of expanding its capabilities beyond current needs. The SPD and the Greens clung to the term *Ausrüstung*.

IG Metall, at its June 2025 congress, passed a resolution that attempted to navigate the tension. The document, titled "Security, Democracy, Good Work", affirmed:

1. Ukraine's right to self-defence is legitimate.
2. The German defence industry produces systems for democracies that respect international law.
3. Workers in the sector deserve the same respect and the same conditions as any other industrial worker.
4. Peace remains the ultimate goal of trade union action.

The resolution was passed with 78% of the votes — a clear but not overwhelming majority by IG Metall standards, where peace resolutions used to be approved unanimously. The remaining 22%, concentrated among young delegates from urban districts (Berlin, Hamburg, Frankfurt), voted against or abstained, arguing that the resolution normalised rearmament.

---

## October 2025 — TKMS goes public in Frankfurt

On 15 October 2025, ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems debuted on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange under the symbol **TMS**. It was the largest IPO of a defence company in Europe since the IPO of Dassault Aviation in 1995 — and the first time in more than a century that a German military shipyard was listed as an independent entity.

The key data of the IPO:

- **Initial valuation**: €12.4 billion
- **Capital placed**: 42% of the total
- **Issue price**: €42 per share
- **First day close**: €48.70 (+16%)
- **Market capitalisation at close**: €14.3 billion
- **Free float**: 38% (the remaining 4% in management hands)
- **Krupp-Stiftung stake**: 10% direct, with veto rights over strategic decisions
- **Residual ThyssenKrupp AG stake**: 8%

Oliver Burkhard rang the opening bell at the Frankfurt Stock Exchange accompanied by Ursula Gather, chair of the Krupp Foundation, and the German Defence Minister. In his speech, Burkhard said: "Today we demonstrate that the German defence industry can be transparent, profitable and responsible at the same time. We are a listed company that pays taxes in Germany, that respects the rights of its workers and that builds systems that protect democracies."

The IG Metall trade union, through its representatives on the new supervisory board, issued a joint statement with management celebrating the "success of a German model of defence industry with co-determination, transparency and social responsibility".

---

## December 2025 — Record order book: €18.7 billion

At the close of fiscal year 2025, TKMS reported a record order book of €18.7 billion. The company employed 9,800 workers — 2,200 more than in 2021 — and had opened a new assembly line in Kiel. Orders included:

- The expansion of the Type 212 CD programme to include the Netherlands as a third partner.
- The contract for the new-generation F127 frigates for the German Navy (€7 billion).
- Submarine modernisation for Israel (Dolphin class).
- Unmanned and electronic warfare systems through ATLAS ELEKTRONIK.

Profitability also improved: the adjusted EBIT margin reached 8.3%, compared to 4.1% in 2021, driven by economies of scale and platform standardisation.

---

## January 2026 — Burkhard, from trade unionist to "defence industrialist"

In an interview with the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung in January 2026, Oliver Burkhard reflected on his career trajectory. Asked whether he still considered himself a trade unionist, he replied:

> "I am still a member of IG Metall. I still pay my dues every month. But my responsibility now is to the 9,800 employees of TKMS and their families. My job is to ensure that this company is competitive, ethical and profitable. If that makes me a 'defence industrialist', I accept the label. But I do not accept the implication that building submarines for democracies is morally inferior to building wind turbines."

The interview generated an intense debate in trade union circles. Christiane Benner, president of IG Metall, came to Burkhard's defence: "Oliver is living proof that one can have a trade union conscience and run an industrial company. Democracy needs defence, and defence needs industry. Denying that reality would be a luxury that German workers cannot afford."

---

## March 2026 — The Sondervermögen surplus and the fiscal debate

In March 2026, the German Finance Ministry published the balance of the Sondervermögen: of the €100 billion approved in 2022, €84 billion had been committed in contracts and €61 billion disbursed. German defence spending reached 2.3% of GDP in 2025, exceeding the NATO target for the second consecutive year.

The debate shifted towards fiscal sustainability. Once the Sondervermögen is exhausted, projected for 2028–2029, Germany would need to finance military spending from the ordinary budget. The CDU/CSU proposed a reform of the debt brake (*Schuldenbremse*) to exclude defence spending from the constitutional limit. The SPD was open but divided. The Greens conditioned their support on a symmetric measure for climate investment.

IG Metall took a pragmatic position: "Security and climate protection are two sides of the same coin. Both require public investment." The trade union that in 1981 demonstrated against Pershing missiles now supported a constitutional reform to facilitate military spending.

---

## April 2026 — TKMS and European strategic autonomy

In April 2026, the European Commission presented the *European Defence Industry Programme* (EDIP), endowed with €1.5 billion to boost joint arms production within the EU. TKMS was identified as one of the "strategic champions" alongside Airbus Defence, Leonardo, Naval Group (France) and Navantia (Spain).

The programme included funding for a next-generation European submarine, the *European Patrol Submarine* (EPS), which would replace the Type 212 and 214 from 2040. TKMS and Naval Group were selected as the two project leaders. Burkhard publicly argued that the consortium should have a "European soul but German management", with headquarters in Kiel and a pan-European supply chain.

---

## May 2026 — The legacy of a decade of transformation

In the spring of 2026, with the Ukraine war having lasted more than four years without a clear end, the political and industrial landscape of Germany was unrecognisable compared to February 2022:

- The German defence industry had grown from employing 98,000 people to 142,000.
- TKMS was worth €16.8 billion on the stock market, 35% more than at its debut.
- IG Metall had gone from avoiding the defence issue to having an articulated position distinguishing between weapons for aggressors and systems for democracies.
- The *Aufrüstung vs Ausrüstung* debate remained active, but the difference had blurred: almost no one in the political mainstream was still discussing whether Germany should have a robust defence industry; the discussion was about how much and with what controls.
- Oliver Burkhard had consolidated himself as the most visible face of the new German consensus: defence yes, but with trade unions, with co-determination, with stock market transparency and with institutional anchoring in the Krupp Foundation.

The deepest transformation, however, was not in the balance sheets or stock prices. It was in the reconciliation between the German trade union left and the reality that freedom, in the 21st century, needed steel, shipyards and submarines just as much as it needed hospitals, schools and solar panels. That reconciliation, forged by the Russian invasion and accelerated by the improbable figure of a former trade unionist turned submarine CEO, was perhaps the most enduring legacy of the Zeitenwende.
