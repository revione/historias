# ver.di and IG Metall — Trade Unions, Power and Mitbestimmung

---

## 1848 — The first spark: Arbeiterbewegung and revolution

The history of German trade unions begins in the same year as the first attempt at German democracy. During the Revolution of 1848, workers were not only demanding press freedom and suffrage: they were demanding labour protection. In April 1848 the *Allgemeine Deutsche Arbeiterverbrüderung* (General Brotherhood of German Workers) was founded in Berlin, the first workers' organisation of national scope, driven by Stephan Born. In September of the same year the first *Arbeiterzeitung* was published in Frankfurt. The Prussian government banned it in 1850 and the labour movement went underground.

German industrialisation — late but explosive — was producing a new class: the factory proletariat. In the 1850s and 1860s, thousands of peasants migrated to the cities to work in coal mines, steelworks, railways and textile factories. In 1849 there were around 600,000 industrial workers in the German states. By 1871 there were already four million. Typical working days were fourteen to sixteen hours. Child labour was widespread. There was no health insurance or pensions. If a worker had an accident, their family ended up on the street.

---

## 1863 — ADAV: Lassalle and the first workers' party

On 23 May 1863, Ferdinand Lassalle founded the *Allgemeiner Deutscher Arbeiterverein* (ADAV) in Leipzig: the first workers' party in Europe. Lassalle, a lawyer and theorist, believed that workers should organise politically and use the state — not just the trade unions — to improve their conditions. His central proposal was to form state-financed production cooperatives.

That same year Lassalle died in a duel over a woman. The ADAV survived its founder and in 1875 would merge with the SDAP of August Bebel and Wilhelm Liebknecht to create the *Sozialistische Arbeiterpartei Deutschlands* (SAP), which in 1890 was renamed the *Sozialdemokratische Partei Deutschlands* (SPD). The connection between trade unions and party was sealed from the outset. No trade union without the SPD and no SPD without the trade unions: they were two arms of the same movement.

The theoretical basis came from three sources. First, French utopian socialism (Fourier, Saint-Simon). Second, English Chartism. Third, and increasingly influential, the Marxism of *Das Kapital* (1867). But German trade unionism was never orthodox Marxist in practice. Since Lassalle, the German labour movement combined three elements that in other countries were separate: the political party (SPD), the mass trade union (the *Gewerkschaften*) and the consumer cooperative. That triad — party, trade union, cooperative — defined the German labour movement until 1933 and explains its institutional strength.

---

## 1878–1890 — The Sozialistengesetze: twelve years underground

On 21 October 1878, Bismarck enacted the *Sozialistengesetze* (Anti-Socialist Laws). The SPD, its newspapers and its associations were banned. But the law did not explicitly mention trade unions, a legal loophole that allowed them to survive as local associations. By 1885 there were already 61 local trade unions grouping some 300,000 members. Bismarckian repression had a paradoxical effect: it radicalised the movement, strengthened internal discipline and, in the long term, increased membership. Trade union identity was forged in resistance.

Bismarck's social model — the laws on health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old age (1889) — was not an original invention of the Chancellor: it was a direct adaptation of the *Generalregulativ* that Alfred Krupp had introduced in his Essen factories in 1872, where social protection and prohibition of trade unions formed a single mechanism of control.

In 1890 Bismarck fell and the laws expired. That same year the *Generalkommission der Gewerkschaften Deutschlands* was founded, the first central body that unified the free trade unions. By 1914, the German free trade unions had 2.5 million members and were the largest trade union movement in the world.

---

## 1891 — The seed of IG Metall: Deutscher Metallarbeiter-Verband

The *Deutscher Metallarbeiter-Verband* (DMV) was founded in 1891 as the metalworkers' trade union. By 1914 it had 500,000 members. During the First World War, the DMV was one of the few trade unions that kept arms production running — which gave it unprecedented access to ministries — while simultaneously pressing for labour rights. This duality between cooperation with the state and worker advocacy defined 20th-century German trade unionism: in 1916, the *Gesetz über den vaterländischen Hilfsdienst* (Auxiliary Service Act) recognised trade unions for the first time and created workers' committees in factories with more than 50 employees. The institutional basis of the future *Mitbestimmung* was born there.

---

## 1918 — Stinnes-Legien: the pact that recognised trade unions

On 15 November 1918, just six days after the abdication of the Kaiser and the proclamation of the republic, trade union leaders sat down with employers. Carl Legien, president of the *Generalkommission der Gewerkschaften*, and Hugo Stinnes, the largest German industrialist, signed the *Stinnes-Legien-Abkommen*.

The agreement contained five points:

1. **Recognition of trade unions** as legitimate representatives of workers.
2. **Prohibition of yellow unions** (*gelbe Gewerkschaften*), employer-controlled organisations that pretended to be trade unions.
3. **Eight-hour day** without wage reduction.
4. **Workers' committees** (*Arbeiterausschüsse*) with the right to co-determination in all factories with more than 50 employees.
5. **Binding collective agreements** (*Tarifverträge*) negotiated between trade unions and employers.

It was the birth of the German *Sozialpartnerschaft* (social partnership). In exchange for legal recognition, the trade unions accepted the capitalist order and renounced the revolution that the Spartacists of Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were attempting on the streets of Berlin.

---

## 1919 — Co-determination in the Weimar Constitution

Article 165 of the Weimar Constitution (11 August 1919) established the principle of co-determination for the first time:

> *"Die Arbeiter und Angestellten sind dazu berufen, gleichberechtigt in Gemeinschaft mit den Unternehmern an der Regelung der Lohn- und Arbeitsbedingungen sowie an der gesamten wirtschaftlichen Entwicklung der produktiven Kräfte mitzuwirken."*

(Workers and employees are called upon to participate, on an equal footing with employers, in the regulation of wages, working conditions and in the entire economic development.) The *Betriebsräte* (works councils) were created through the *Betriebsrätegesetz* of 1920, which gave workers elected representation in every workplace with at least 20 employees.

---

## 1933 — Destruction: the Nazis liquidate the trade unions

On 2 May 1933, SA and SS stormtroopers occupied all trade union headquarters in Germany. The leaders of the ADGB, the SPD and the *Gewerkschaften* were arrested; many ended up in concentration camps. Trade union property — buildings, newspapers, cooperative banks — was confiscated and transferred to the *Deutsche Arbeitsfront* (DAF), a Nazi organisation that included both workers and employers.

The DAF, under the leadership of Robert Ley, was the opposite of a trade union. It banned strikes, froze wages, eliminated the right to negotiate agreements and suppressed works councils. Its purpose was to control the working class, not to represent it. But the DAF also offered *Kraft durch Freude* (Strength through Joy), a leisure and holiday programme that gave German workers access to holidays and entertainment for the first time — something the free trade unions had never achieved on that scale. That combination of repression and material welfare was one of the reasons for working-class resignation under Nazism.

The trade unionists who survived the Third Reich — many in exile, others in the clandestine resistance — would rebuild the movement from scratch in 1945.

---

## 1946 — Reconstruction under Allied occupation

After the war, trade unions were rebuilt immediately. In the British zone, the occupation authorities favoured a model of a single trade union per industrial branch (*Industriegewerkschaft*), rather than the former fragmented craft unions. This principle — one company, one trade union — became the DNA of post-war German trade unionism.

In 1946, the free trade unions in the western zones already had 2.7 million members.

---

## 1949 — Founding of the DGB

On 12 October 1949, in Munich, the *Deutscher Gewerkschaftsbund* (DGB) was founded: the confederation that would unify all West German trade unions. The first president was Hans Böckler, a veteran trade unionist who had been a member of the Reichstag for the SPD during Weimar and had survived Nazism underground.

The DGB was born with the principle of trade union unity (*Einheitsgewerkschaft*): a single trade union per branch, without confessional or party-political divisions. The decision was deliberate: Weimar's fragmentation — there had been social democratic, communist, Catholic and liberal trade unions — had facilitated the Nazi victory. Unity was a vaccine against fascism. Over time, mergers would reduce the 16 founding DGB unions to just 8 by 2025.

---

## 1949 — Founding of IG Metall

In November 1949 *IG Metall* was founded, the trade union of metal, steel and electrical industry workers, as the direct successor of the *Deutscher Metallarbeiter-Verband* of 1891. From day one, IG Metall was the giant of the DGB. By 1951 it already had 1.2 million members. Its first president was Hans Brümmer, but the figure who would define the trade union for two decades was Otto Brenner, president from 1956 to 1972.

Under Brenner, IG Metall became the most influential trade union in Europe. Its strategy combined mass strikes — such as the 1956 strike in Bavaria for the 40-hour week — with centralised collective bargaining. IG Metall's power emanated from two sources: its membership density (almost 90% in large automotive and steel plants) and *Mitbestimmung*, which gave it seats on supervisory boards. The Otto Brenner Stiftung — IG Metall's media research foundation, named after its most legendary president — has produced since 2005 the data monitoring concentration among the six major German media groups, including Bertelsmann and Springer.

---

## 1951 — Montanmitbestimmung: the historic achievement

On 21 May 1951, the Bundestag passed the *Montanmitbestimmungsgesetz*, the co-determination law for the coal and steel industries. It was the first parity co-determination law in the world.

What it established:

- **Parity supervisory board**: 5 worker representatives, 5 shareholder representatives, plus an eleventh neutral member elected by both sides.
- **Labour director** (*Arbeitsdirektor*): a member of the *Vorstand* (management board) appointed with the vote of the worker representatives. He could neither be appointed nor dismissed without their consent.
- **Application**: companies in the coal and steel industries with more than 1,000 employees (coal mining, iron and steel production).

Why it happened in 1951:
Adenauer needed social peace for reconstruction. The coal and steel industry was at the centre of the Schuman Plan (the future ECSC) and NATO rearmament. Without the cooperation of IG Metall and the DGB, Adenauer's plans would collapse. Hans Böckler, DGB leader, used that leverage to extract a historic concession.

The law was the result of an explicit threat of a general strike. In January 1951, more than 95% of miners and steelworkers voted in favour of strike action in a trade union ballot. Adenauer gave in. The *Montanmitbestimmung*, which remains in force in 2025 and applies to around 50 companies, is the most advanced co-determination arrangement in the capitalist world.

The same Adenauer who granted co-determination in 1951 attempted nine years later to create a state-controlled television — the *Deutschland-Fernsehen GmbH* — and was blocked by the Constitutional Court in 1961. Forced by the trade unions to accept co-determination, he tried to control television and lost on both fronts.

---

## 1952 — The Betriebsverfassungsgesetz: works councils everywhere

A year after Montanmitbestimmung, the Adenauer government passed the *Betriebsverfassungsgesetz* (BetrVG). This law required all companies with at least 5 employees to have a *Betriebsrat* (works council) elected by the workers. The councils had rights to information, consultation and — in certain matters — co-determination (working hours, holidays, data protection, performance evaluations).

But in the supervisory boards of companies that were NOT in coal/steel, the 1952 BetrVG gave workers only **one third** of the seats, not parity. For the trade unions it was a partial defeat: they had won the coal and steel war, but lost that of the rest of the economy.

---

## 1956 — The Bavaria strike: IG Metall demonstrates its power

The first major IG Metall strike in the post-war period lasted 114 days and paralysed the Bavarian metalworking industry. The result: the 40-hour week in Bavaria, which later spread to the entire Federal Republic. It was the show of strength that consolidated IG Metall as the most powerful trade union in Europe. Otto Brenner emerged as the continent's most important labour leader.

---

## 1960–1975 — The golden age: trade union expansion and full employment

Between 1960 and 1975, German trade unions experienced their golden age. DGB membership rose from 6.4 million (1960) to 7.4 million (1975). Unemployment was below 3%. Collective agreements covered 85% of workers. Strikes were short, targeted and almost always won.

In those years, IG Metall and the other DGB trade unions operated as a *state within a state*: they set wages for millions of workers, controlled the supervisory boards of the largest companies, and through the SPD — which governed from 1966 to 1969 in coalition with the CDU and from 1969 to 1982 with the FDP — directly influenced labour legislation. Chancellor Willy Brandt (1969–1974) was a social democrat from the trade unions; Labour Minister Walter Arendt was a former trade union leader. The revolving door between the SPD and the trade unions worked in both directions.

---

## 1976 — Mitbestimmungsgesetz: quasi-parity for all

On 1 July 1976, after decades of trade union pressure, the Bundestag passed the *Mitbestimmungsgesetz 1976*, which extended co-determination to all companies with more than 2,000 employees. The 1976 law establishes:

- **Numerically parity-based supervisory board**: half worker representatives, half shareholder representatives.
- **Double vote of the chairman**: the chairman of the board — elected by the shareholders — has a casting vote in the event of a deadlock. This is why it is called "quasi-parity": capital can always prevail in the last instance.
- **Worker representatives**: part must be employees of the company, the rest can be external trade union delegates.
- **Application**: around 700 German companies in 2025 (Daimler, Siemens, BMW, BASF, Volkswagen, Deutsche Bank, ThyssenKrupp, SAP, Bosch...).

The approval was controversial. Employers (BDA) took the law to the Constitutional Court, which declared it constitutional in 1979 (BVerfGE 50, 290). The Karlsruhe judges argued that the right to property (Article 14 of the Basic Law) was not violated because co-determination did not eliminate the entrepreneurial capacity to decide: the Vorstand remained the management body and the chairman could break the tie. The Aufsichtsrat under Mitbestimmung shares architecture with the Kuratorium of the Krupp-Stiftung (text 2201), the Stiftungsrat of the FAZ (text 2203) and the Fernsehrat of ZDF (text 2205): appointed, not elected, councils, informally coordinated through party-political channels, without electoral accountability.

The 1976 law is unique in the world. No other developed capitalist country — not the United States, not the United Kingdom, not Japan, not France — gives workers half the seats on the boards of large companies. It is the backbone of IG Metall's and ver.di's institutional power.

---

## 1984 — The strike for the 35-hour week

In 1984, IG Metall launched the most ambitious strike since the 1950s: the reduction of the working week from 40 to 35 hours, with full wage compensation. The argument was that productivity had grown so much that fewer working hours would distribute employment. Employers (*Gesamtmetall*) opposed it outright.

The strike lasted seven weeks, paralysed plants at Daimler, BMW and Bosch, and cost the economy some 20 billion Deutschmarks. The result was a graduated compromise: the working week was reduced to 38.5 hours, with flexibility and without full wage compensation. But the principle was established. In the following years, IG Metall continued to negotiate gradual reductions until reaching the 35-hour week in most agreements by the end of the 1990s.

---

## 1990 — Reunification and the clash of cultures

The reunification of 1990 presented an unprecedented challenge: absorbing 17 million East Germans who had never known free trade unions. The *Freier Deutscher Gewerkschaftsbund* (FDGB) of the GDR was an appendage of the SED, the ruling communist party. Its members (9.6 million in 1989) were compulsory, not voluntary.

The DGB absorbed the eastern trade unions, but the trade union culture was radically different. In the east, trade unions had organised subsidised holidays and company housing; in the west, they had fought for wages, hours and co-determination against employers. In 1990–1991, eastern membership collapsed: eastern workers abandoned en masse trade unions that seemed irrelevant or directly associated with the fallen regime. Trade union density in the east never recovered. In 2025, less than 20% of eastern workers are unionised, compared to 40–50% in the industrial west.

---

## 1993 — The crisis of the metalworking industry

The recession of 1993 hit the heart of IG Metall: the automotive and steel sectors laid off hundreds of thousands of workers. IG Metall membership, which had peaked at 2.67 million in 1991 (east and west combined), began to fall. The trade union moved from a position of strength to a permanent defensive. Collective bargaining was no longer about how much to raise wages, but about how many layoffs to avoid.

---

## 1995 — IG Metall's first offensive strike in the east

In 1995, IG Metall called the first offensive strike in the new federal states to equalise eastern wages with western ones. The strike failed. Eastern employers — small, undercapitalised companies with minimal margins — could not pay western wages. IG Metall had to accept a gradual equalisation that is still not complete. The east-west wage inequality is one of the great failures of German trade unionism.

---

## 2001 — ver.di is born: the merger of five giants

On 2 July 2001, five service sector trade unions merged to create *ver.di* (*Vereinte Dienstleistungsgewerkschaft*), the unified service sector union:

- **ÖTV** (Gewerkschaft Öffentliche Dienste, Transport und Verkehr) — public services and transport, founded in 1896
- **HBV** (Gewerkschaft Handel, Banken und Versicherungen) — commerce, banking and insurance
- **IG Medien** (Industriegewerkschaft Medien) — media, printing, journalism
- **DAG** (Deutsche Angestellten-Gewerkschaft) — independent white-collar union, founded in 1949
- **DPG** (Deutsche Postgewerkschaft) — postal services

The merger was controversial. Critics pointed out that a trade union of two million members with such disparate sectors — nurses, pilots, journalists, refuse collectors, bank employees, civil servants — was ungovernable. Each sector had opposing interests. The internal structure was organised into *Fachbereiche* (professional divisions) with bargaining autonomy, which in practice reproduced the fragmentation the merger sought to overcome.

Frank Bsirske was the first president of ver.di (2001–2019), succeeded by Frank Werneke (2019–). Werneke is also the historical coordinator of the "red circle" (*roter Kreis*, the SPD Freundeskreis) on the Fernsehrat of ZDF, the body that oversees the second German public television channel.

---

## 2003 — Agenda 2010: the betrayal of the SPD

On 14 March 2003, Chancellor Gerhard Schröder (SPD) presented Agenda 2010 to the Bundestag. The Hartz reforms (I to IV), designed by Peter Hartz — a Volkswagen executive and IG Metall member — cut unemployment benefits, made dismissal more flexible, created *Minijobs* (precarious jobs without full social security) and forced the unemployed to accept any job or lose benefits.

For German trade unions, Agenda 2010 was the greatest shock since 1933. The SPD — their party, their political arm, the organisation they financed and controlled — had passed the biggest labour deregulation since Bismarck. In the streets, thousands of trade unionists returned their SPD membership cards. Trade union membership plummeted: the DGB lost more than a million members between 2002 and 2010. The intellectual framework of these reforms was shaped by the Bertelsmann Stiftung, the *think tank* of the Mohn family's media empire — the same family that controls RTL, Germany's largest commercial television group.

Historically, around 60% of SPD deputies in the Bundestag have been members of DGB trade unions. Agenda 2010 broke that organic bond. From 2003 onwards, the SPD–trade union relationship went from symbiotic to tense and conditional. In 2019, then Finance Minister and candidate for Chancellor Olaf Scholz — also SPD — had to face ver.di and IG Metall in the streets over housing policy. The era in which the SPD and the trade unions were the same thing was over. The same politician who faced trade union protests as Finance Minister declared the *Zeitenwende* as Chancellor in 2022, guaranteeing a decade of record orders for those same trade unions' defence sector members.

---

## 2006 — Equal Treatment Act: ver.di pressures the legislator

The *Allgemeines Gleichbehandlungsgesetz* (AGG) of 2006, which prohibits discrimination at work based on gender, race, ethnic origin, religion, disability, age or sexual orientation, was a direct achievement of ver.di and the SPD's left wing. Employers fiercely opposed it, but trade union pressure in the Bundestag was decisive.

---

## 2015 — Minimum Wage Act: the DGB wins where IG Metall started

On 1 January 2015, the first national minimum wage in German history entered into force: €8.50 per hour. It had been a historic demand of the DGB, but ironically did not affect IG Metall members (their collective agreements were already well above it). The law mainly benefited workers in ver.di sectors — hospitality, commerce, cleaning, care — where collective agreements were weak or non-existent.

The *Mindestlohngesetz* was driven by the SPD (in coalition with Merkel) as a concession to the trade unions after the trauma of Agenda 2010. It rose to €9.35 in 2020, €12 in October 2022, and €12.82 in January 2025.

---

## 2023 — Christiane Benner: the first woman to lead IG Metall

In October 2023, Christiane Benner became the first female president of IG Metall in 132 years of history. A sociologist by training, Benner had joined IG Metall as a secretary in 1997 and risen through organisation and gender policy. Her election was interpreted as a strategic shift: IG Metall needed to modernise its image to attract young workers, women and migrants who had historically ignored the trade union. Shortly after taking office, Benner personally normalised work in the defence industry within the union, publicly declaring that "defence workers are workers like any other" and backing Oliver Burkhard, the former IG Metall functionary turned CEO of TKMS.

## The power structure of trade unions today (2025)

### The two pillars of trade union power in Germany

The power of IG Metall and ver.di rests on two institutional pillars, both unique in the world:

**Pillar 1: Sector-wide collective agreements (*Flächentarifverträge*)**
Trade unions negotiate wages, hours, holidays and conditions for an entire industrial branch with a single employers' association. The agreement is binding for all companies in the sector. This prevents a race to the bottom on wages: Daimler and a small supplier pay the same base wage. Collective agreement coverage has fallen from 85% (1990) to 49% (2024), but is still double that of the US (12%) or UK (26%).

**Pillar 2: Co-determination on supervisory boards**
Through the laws of 1951 and 1976, trade unions have seats — and in some cases parity control — on the supervisory boards of the 700 largest German companies. The works council chair of Volkswagen sits on the *Aufsichtsrat* and decides on investments, plant closures and the appointment of the CEO.

### IG Metall

**Members**: ~2.1 million (2024). Historical peak: 2.67 million (1991). Trend: net loss of ~40,000 members per year.

**Sectors**: automotive (VW, Daimler, BMW, Porsche, Audi, Opel), steel (ThyssenKrupp, Salzgitter), electrical (Siemens, Bosch), mechanical engineering, textiles, wood/plastics, IT. Covers around 3.8 million jobs in 25,000 companies.

**Dues**: 1% of gross salary (the highest in the DGB — the average is 0.6–0.8%).

**Annual budget**: ~€600 million (2024), with accumulated reserves close to €600 million. A substantial portion is invested in a real estate and financial holding controlled by the union.

**Power structure**: Seven *Bezirke* (regions), 154 *Verwaltungsstellen* (local offices), and the *Vorstand* (executive board) in Frankfurt. The congress (*Gewerkschaftstag*) is held every four years and elects the president and the executive committee.

**Main figures**:
- **Christiane Benner**, president since 2023
- **Jürgen Kerner**, vice-president and *Hauptkassierer* (chief treasurer)

The **Hans-Böckler-Stiftung**, the DGB's foundation dedicated to co-determination and labour policy research, counts among its board members Oliver Burkhard, CEO of TKMS and former IG Metall functionary — the same man who today runs Europe's largest submarine manufacturer, partially controlled by the Krupp Foundation.

### ver.di

**Members**: ~1.88 million (2024). Historical peak: ~2.8 million (2001, after the merger). Trend: net loss of ~50,000 members per year.

**Sectors**: public sector, healthcare, transport, commerce, banking, insurance, media, postal services, telecommunications, education, science, churches, energy, cleaning, private security. Covers around 1,000 occupational profiles in tens of thousands of workplaces.

**Dues**: 1% of gross salary.

**Annual budget**: ~€500 million (2024).

**Headquarters**: Berlin.

**Main figures**:
- **Frank Werneke**, president since 2019
- **Christine Behle**, vice-president

**Notable recent strikes**:
- 2023–2024: massive *Warnstreiks* (warning strikes) in public transport (EVG, ver.di), paralysing trains, airports and buses for weeks.
- 2022–2023: Airport workers' strike for wage increases (ver.di).
- 2015: Postal workers' strike — Deutsche Post (ver.di) and nursery workers' strike (ver.di/GEW) for better pay.

## Mitbestimmung: how it works in practice (2025)

### At Volkswagen: the extreme case

Volkswagen AG is subject to the *Montanmitbestimmung* of 1951 (due to steel production) and additionally to a special law, the *VW-Gesetz* of 1960. The *VW-Gesetz* belongs to the German pattern of the *Lex specialis*: laws written for a single entity, like the *Lex Krupp* of 1943 (text 2201) and the *Habsburgergesetz* of 1919 (text 2202). All three circumvent general law to legislate on concrete entities for political reasons. The result is that VW's *Aufsichtsrat* has 20 members: 10 from the shareholders (the Porsche/Piëch family, Qatar, Lower Saxony) and 10 from the workers. But Lower Saxony holds 20% of the shares with a blocking vote (it can veto any decision requiring 80% majority). The state of Lower Saxony, governed by the SPD since 1998, systematically votes with the trade union representatives and has blocked key decisions such as mass redundancies or relocations. In practice, no VW CEO has been able to close a German plant without the consent of the works council and IG Metall.

In 2015, *Dieselgate* forced VW to cut 30,000 jobs globally. But in Germany, the layoffs were agreed with IG Metall through early retirements and voluntary severance. Zero forced redundancies on German soil. The trade union not only protects jobs: it decides how they are eliminated.

The VW works council chair has been, for decades, one of the most powerful men in Germany. Bernd Osterloh (2005–2021) earned over €700,000 a year and had an armoured company car. His successor, Daniela Cavallo, earns around €500,000. The salary of works council leaders is calculated according to executive remuneration — a principle that has generated legal controversy in recent years.

### At Siemens and Daimler: operational quasi-parity

At Siemens AG and Mercedes-Benz Group AG, the supervisory boards each have 20 members: 10 from shareholders and 10 from workers. The worker representatives include IG Metall delegates and works council members. In practice, no strategic decision — merger, sale of a division, plant closure — is taken without the tacit consent of the trade unions. The double vote of the board chairman is rarely used: the system works by consensus. If the trade union says no, the decision is delayed, negotiated or abandoned.

### At ThyssenKrupp: the foundation and the union, face to face

At ThyssenKrupp AG, the 20-member *Aufsichtsrat* has 10 worker representatives (IG Metall) and 10 shareholder representatives. On the shareholder side, two seats are held by the Krupp-Stiftung (20.93% of capital) — the same foundation that controls 10% directly of TKMS, whose CEO Oliver Burkhard is a former IG Metall functionary. On the worker side, IG Metall controls the majority of votes. In votes on redundancies, sale of divisions or restructurings, the union has repeatedly blocked management's plans. In 2020–2023, the dispute over the sale of the elevator division and the restructuring of the steelworks were pitched battles in the supervisory board that IG Metall partially won. When the CEO of ThyssenKrupp wants to close a steel plant in Duisburg or reduce the workforce, he needs the vote of the very workers he wants to dismiss. That is the essence of Mitbestimmung power.

### At ver.di and the banks: Deutsche Bank, Commerzbank

In the financial sector, ver.di controls the workers' seats on the supervisory boards of Deutsche Bank, Commerzbank, Allianz and Münchner Rück. At Deutsche Bank, the board has 20 members: 10 from shareholders and 10 from workers. The chair of the central works council and ver.di delegates sit alongside capital representatives. In 2017–2019, when Deutsche Bank announced 18,000 layoffs, ver.di negotiated the most expensive *Sozialplan* (social plan) in the history of German banking: severance payments of up to €350,000 per employee. Without Mitbestimmung, those layoffs would have been immediate, without compensation, as on Wall Street.

## Trade unions and the defence industry

### The official position vs. economic reality

The DGB, IG Metall and ver.di officially declare themselves pacifist. The DGB congress of 2022 passed motions against military spending and in favour of disarmament. But forty years earlier, the IG Metall congress of 1981 took place in Essen — the city of the Krupp family, the heart of German arms manufacturing. Delegates from military shipyards voted in favour of the pacifist resolution, in a contradiction that would remain unresolved for four decades. IG Metall organises the workers of Germany's largest arms companies and part of its financial and institutional power depends on them.

The main German defence industry companies with IG Metall representation and co-determination:

- **Rheinmetall AG** — ammunition, armoured vehicles, cannons. 28,000 employees in 2024, workforce exploded since Russia's invasion of Ukraine. IG Metall holds 10 of 20 seats on the *Aufsichtsrat*. When Rheinmetall doubled its artillery ammunition production in 2023, IG Metall delegates negotiated extra shift bonuses.
- **ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems (TKMS)** — Type 212CD submarines, MEKO frigates. 9,100 employees. IG Metall on the supervisory board through ThyssenKrupp AG.
- **KNDS (KMW + Nexter)** — Leopard 2 tanks. 9,500 employees in Germany, France and other countries. IG Metall on the German side.
- **Diehl Defence** — missiles, active protection systems for tanks. IG Metall.
- **Hensoldt AG** — sensors, radars, military optics. 7,000 employees. IG Metall.
- **Airbus Defence and Space** — military aircraft, drones, military satellites. IG Metall.
- **MBDA Deutschland** — guided missiles (Taurus, Meteor, Patriot). IG Metall.

These companies represent around 150,000 direct jobs in Germany and jointly generate annual revenues of more than €40 billion. Wages are high (€60,000–€90,000 per year on average), collective agreements are favourable, and IG Metall membership exceeds 70% in most of them.

### The *Zeitenwende* and the transformation of trade union discourse

On 27 February 2022, three days after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Chancellor Olaf Scholz announced the *Zeitenwende* (turning point): a special fund of €100 billion for the German Armed Forces. Overnight, Germany's defence industry went from being a stigmatised sector to a strategic one.

For IG Metall, the dilemma was immediate:
- On the one hand, the trade union has a long pacifist tradition. Its left wing, strong in Stuttgart and Frankfurt, rejects any collaboration with the arms industry.
- On the other hand, the conversion of the automotive industry to electric vehicles threatens to destroy tens of thousands of jobs in the combustion engine supply chain. While Germany's defence industry, with the €100 billion of the *Sondervermögen Bundeswehr*, has guaranteed orders for the next decade.

IG Metall's response has been pragmatic:
- It has not renounced its official pacifist position.
- But it has not blocked the expansion of Rheinmetall or TKMS either.
- In collective bargaining, it has prioritised wages and working conditions, not ethical debates about arms production.
- On supervisory boards, it has voted in favour of investments in new production lines for ammunition and armoured vehicles.

The result: in 2024, Rheinmetall workers received wage increases of 8.5% — well above the metalworking sector average — and productivity bonuses linked to the expansion of military production. IG Metall celebrated the deal as a trade union victory. The fact that the increases were financed by ammunition contracts for Ukraine was not mentioned in the press releases.

### ver.di and NATO

ver.di is also involved — albeit indirectly — in the defence industry through public sector workers who manage military bases, German Army logistics contracts, and NATO civilian employees (NATO headquarters in Europe is in Brussels, but ver.di organises German employees of the alliance, including bases such as Ramstein and NATO headquarters in Oberammergau). In 2023–2024, ver.di called strikes by airport personnel that affected military transport flights, generating an indirect conflict with the Ministry of Defence.

## The SPD and the trade unions: the symbiotic relationship in data

### Financing

German trade unions do not directly finance the SPD with union dues (this has been illegal since 1958). But:
- Trade union officials and employees donate individually to the SPD.
- Trade unions contribute to SPD-affiliated political foundations such as the *Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung*, and finance awareness campaigns that indirectly benefit the party. The Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung, in turn, trains the cadres who decades later populate the Freundeskreise of ZDF — the "red circle" of the Fernsehrat — closing the circuit between the trade union world and the informal control of public television.
- Trade union leaders occupy SPD seats in the Bundestag. In the 2021–2025 legislature, around 27% of SPD deputies were members of DGB trade unions.

### The historical revolving door

The list of trade union leaders who moved into government with the SPD is extensive:
- **Walter Arendt** — from president of IG Bergbau (mining) to Minister of Labour (1969–1976).
- **Hans Matthöfer** — from IG Metall to Minister of Finance (1978–1982).
- **Norbert Blüm** (CDU, but a trade unionist) — from IG Metall to Minister of Labour (1982–1998), demonstrating that trade unionists could also govern with the CDU.
- **Andrea Nahles** — from ver.di to Minister of Labour (2013–2017) and then SPD chairwoman (2018–2019).
- **Hubertus Heil** — from IG Metall to Minister of Labour (2018–2025), current officeholder.

### The trade union blockage in the SPD

Internally, the trade unions operate as a wing of the SPD — the *Arbeitnehmerflügel* — controlling a significant percentage of delegates at the party's congresses. When the SPD tried to reform pensions or make the labour market more flexible, the internal trade union bloc was always the biggest obstacle. Schröder's Agenda 2010 could only be passed because the Chancellor threatened to resign and the SPD feared electoral defeat. Even so, the wound has never fully healed.

## Membership trends: the slow haemorrhage

Membership of DGB trade unions has fallen steadily since reunification:

- **1991**: 11.8 million members (absolute peak after absorbing the GDR's FDGB)
- **2000**: 7.7 million
- **2005**: 6.7 million
- **2010**: 6.1 million
- **2015**: 6.1 million (temporary stabilisation)
- **2020**: 5.67 million
- **2024**: ~5.5 million

IG Metall has gone from 2.67 million (1991) to 2.14 million (2024).
ver.di has gone from 2.8 million (2001) to 1.88 million (2024).

The causes:
1. **Tertiarisation**: Unionised jobs (factory, mine, public administration) lose weight against non-unionised ones (startups, digital platforms, self-employed).
2. **Fragmentation**: Young people change jobs frequently and do not join trade unions.
3. **Ageing**: 60% of ver.di members are over 50 years old.
4. **East**: Membership in the new federal states collapsed after reunification and never recovered. In Saxony, trade union density is below 15%.
5. **Agenda 2010**: The shock of seeing their own party pass labour deregulation alienated many workers from both party and union.

### The counterattack: American-style organising

Since 2010, IG Metall and ver.di have adopted *organising* techniques imported from US trade unions (SEIU, AFL-CIO): aggressive membership campaigns in non-unionised sectors, legal support for works councils in formation, and litigation against companies that violate labour rights. Results have been modest but real: in 2022–2024, IG Metall achieved net membership increases in the IT sector for the first time in decades.

## The future of trade union power: three scenarios

### Scenario 1: controlled decline

The industrial base shrinks, jobs become more precarious, collective agreement coverage continues to fall. Trade unions become organisations of pensioners and civil servants, with power on paper (through Mitbestimmung) but without real mobilising capacity. This is the current trajectory.

### Scenario 2: rebirth through conflict

Inflation, the energy crisis and deindustrialisation generate massive social conflict. Trade unions recover membership as an instrument of defence. The ecological transition — closure of combustion engine factories, conversion of the Ruhr — gives them a mobilising cause. This is what happened in the 1950s and 1970s.

### Scenario 3: institutional irrelevance

Co-determination is maintained as a legal facade but trade unions lose all real capacity to influence corporate decisions. Supervisory boards become bureaucratic formalities. Collective agreements become sectoral exceptions. This is what happened in the United Kingdom after Thatcher.

Germany has not yet chosen which of these three scenarios will be its own. But 4 million members, 700 supervisory boards, a co-determination law unique in the world and 130 years of history do not disappear in a decade. What disappears or survives will depend on whether IG Metall and ver.di manage to do what they always did: organise those who have no voice.
