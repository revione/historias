# Origins of Drug Trafficking in Colombia — The Cattle Ranching Connection

---

## Introduction

In Colombia, the question is not why so many cattle ranchers ended up linked to drug trafficking. The question is: **how can you tell the legitimate rancher from the one who was laundering money, if the system was designed to make it impossible to distinguish them?**

This investigation traces the origins of drug trafficking in Colombia, from the Bonanza Marimbera of the 1970s to the consolidation of the great cartels, and explores the structural connection between cattle ranching, land, and narco money laundering.

---

## 1. The First Crops — Marijuana on the Coast

Drug trafficking in Colombia did not begin with cocaine. It began with **marijuana** on the Atlantic Coast in the 1960s and 70s.

### The discovery

Young Americans traveling to the **Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta** discovered that high-quality marijuana was being grown there. Word spread. Peasants from the **Sierra Nevada, Valledupar, La Guajira and Magdalena** began planting on a large scale. By 1975, Colombia was producing **70% of the cannabis consumed in the U.S.**

### The Bonanza Marimbera (1975-1980)

The first traffickers were not kingpins from novels: they were **local smugglers** who already trafficked whisky, appliances and cigarettes. They simply added marijuana to their routes. They were called **"marimberos"**.

| Capo                                     | Region       | Modus Operandi                           | Outcome       |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| **Alberto "El Caracol" Orlández Gamboa** | Santa Marta  | Controlled shipments to Florida via Bahamas | Captured      |
| **Valdeblánquez Brothers**               | Santa Marta  | Fleet of speedboats                      | Dismantled    |
| **Julio Nasser**                         | Barranquilla | Smuggling + laundering in construction   | Prosecuted    |
| **Manuel María Torres**                  | La Guajira   | Shipments to Aruba and Curaçao           | Killed        |
| **Carlos "El Puma" Guarín**              | Caribbean Coast | Air routes                            | Captured      |

**They operated with**: go-fast boats and small planes bound for Florida. Money was laundered in construction, cattle ranching and commerce in Barranquilla, Santa Marta and Cartagena.

**The figure**: They were exporting **19,000 metric tons per year** by the late 1970s.

### How was marijuana money laundered?

- **Land purchases**: Farms on the Coast, especially Córdoba, Sucre, Bolívar
- **Cattle ranching**: They set up cattle herds on the Coast and the Llanos
- **Construction**: In Barranquilla, Cartagena, Santa Marta — entire buildings financed with marimbero dollars
- **Hospitality**: Hotels on the Caribbean Coast
- **Commerce**: Imports of appliances, liquor, vehicles

### The marimbero-political connection

The Caribbean Coast had a **culture of smuggling** rooted since colonial times. Coastal politicians openly coexisted with smugglers and marimberos. In La Guajira, gasoline and marijuana smuggling was the local economy. Political families like the **Char**, **Name**, **Merheg**, **Cotes**, **Gerlein** built fortunes in that ecosystem. There was no dividing line between "legitimate" smuggling and drug trafficking.

---

## 2. The Shift from Marijuana to Cocaine

The transition occurred in the mid-1970s for three reasons:

1. **Weight/value ratio**: 1 kg of cocaine was worth 20 times more than 1 kg of marijuana and took up much less space
2. **U.S. pressure**: The DEA pressured against marijuana; cocaine was less detectable
3. **Colombia as a natural link**: Peru and Bolivia produced coca leaf. Colombia had the smuggling infrastructure, the geographic location (two oceans, Caribbean close to the U.S.), and the chemical capacity to process coca paste

**When exactly?**: The first cocaine laboratories appeared in the mid-1970s. The **Cartel de Medellín** and the **Cartel de Cali** began buying Peruvian and Bolivian coca paste to process in Colombia. By 1980, cocaine had already surpassed marijuana.

Many marimberos did not survive the transition. Those who did —like the Ochoas— had:

- Contacts in Peru and Bolivia for coca paste
- Chemical processing capacity
- Air connections to move merchandise
- Vision that cocaine was the future

## 3. The First Cartels

### Cartel de Medellín (~1976-1993)

**Origin**: Pablo Escobar started as a smuggler, car thief and hitman. He partnered with the **Ochoa brothers** (Jorge Luis, Juan David, Fabio) —cattle ranchers with contacts and social prestige— and **Carlos Lehder** (who knew the air routes). **Gonzalo Rodríguez Gacha** "El Mexicano" provided military logistics in the Llanos.

**Rapid evolution**:

- 1976: Escobar caught with 39 kilos of cocaine (case dismissed)
- 1982: Escobar elected alternate to the Chamber of Representatives
- 1984: Tranquilandia discovered
- 1989: Assassination of Luis Carlos Galán
- 1993: Escobar killed

**Structure**: Flexible coalition, not hierarchical. Each partner ran their own organization, but they shared routes, laboratories and protection. Forbes listed **Jorge Luis Ochoa** among the 20 richest men in the world in 1987 (estimated fortune: US$3 billion).

**Peak**: They controlled ~80% of global cocaine traffic in the mid-1980s.

### Cartel de Cali (~1977-1995)

**Origin**: **Gilberto Rodríguez Orejuela** and **José Santacruz Londoño** started as kidnappers and smugglers. **Miguel Rodríguez Orejuela** joined later. They organized in Cali, with contacts in the Valle del Cauca business elite.

**Difference from Medellín**: They were "discreet". They infiltrated the state, bribed instead of killing. They controlled cocaine routes to New York and Europe. More corporate and hierarchical structure.

### Cartel de la Costa (~1975-1990s)

Born from the Bonanza Marimbera. **Alberto Orlández Gamboa** was the great coastal kingpin. They controlled marijuana from the Sierra Nevada. They were dismantled by Colombian police and the DEA in the early 1980s. Many marimberos did not make the transition to cocaine.

### North Valle Cartel (~1990s-2000s)

Emerged after the fall of Medellín and Cali. **Wilber Varela** ("Jabón"), **Diego Montoya** ("Don Diego"), **the Orejuela brothers**. They operated in the Valle del Cauca. They were the last great kingpins.

## 4. The Cattle Ranching Connection — Why Cattle Ranching?

### Why did narcos buy land?

- **Money laundering**: Cattle ranching allowed converting dollars into "legitimate" assets without leaving a tax trail. Cattle move, die, are born — it is nearly impossible to audit the real inventory of a farm
- **Social prestige**: In Colombia, being a cattle rancher means being "someone". Land confers status. A narco who arrived at a horse fair with a $500,000 animal was welcomed by the elite
- **Appreciation**: They bought undervalued farms, injected money, improved them and resold them, or used them as collateral for more loans
- **Tax evasion**: Cattle ranching was (and is) one of the least audited sectors by the DIAN. Perfect for justifying income

### How was money laundered?

1. **Purchase of farms with cash**, without mortgage credit
2. **Undervaluation**: They declared a lower price than the real one; the seller received the difference under the table
3. **Front men**: They put farms in the names of employees, relatives or partners (testaferros)
4. **Overvaluation of cattle**: They bought cattle at inflated prices; the difference was laundered
5. **Fictitious exports**: They declared exports of meat or cattle that never left the country
6. **Cattle rotation**: They bought cattle, fattened them, sold them — thus justifying continuous income
7. **Fictitious loans**: A rancher "lent" money to another (who was a narco); the narco "paid" with interest, legitimizing dollars

### The paso fino horse connection

The **Colombian paso fino horse** was central. The equine fairs (Medellín, Sincelejo, Bogotá) were the meeting point for narcos, politicians and legitimate ranchers. A champion horse could be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. **Pablo Escobar, the Ochoas, Rodríguez Gacha** openly competed at these fairs. It was the common language: a narco who paid $500,000 for a horse was welcomed into cattle ranching society.

### Landmark cases

- **Pablo Escobar — Hacienda Nápoles** (Puerto Triunfo, Antioquia): 2,000 hectares, zoo, airstrip, 27 buildings. Façade: bullfighting and paso fino horses. Reality: Cartel headquarters
- **The Ochoas — Haras La Margarita**: Fabio Ochoa Restrepo was a legitimate cattle rancher (pre-dating the narco era). He raised champion horses and fighting bulls. His sons used the cattle ranching façade to launder millions. The **Las Margaritas** restaurant in Medellín was a meeting point
- **Rodríguez Gacha**: Bought extensive farms in the **Llanos Orientales** (Meta, Vichada), where he established laboratories and airstrips

---

## 5. The Llanos Orientales — The Production Center

### Why the Llanos?

- **Vastness**: ~250,000 km² of jungle and savanna without state control
- **Proximity to Peru and Bolivia**: Direct flights from the Amazon with coca paste
- **Navigable rivers**: Meta, Guaviare, Yarí, Apaporis — ideal river routes
- **Low population**: What happened there, no one saw
- **Cheap land**: Colonization incentivized by the state since the 1940s

### The Antioquian colonization

Since the 1940s, people from Antioquia colonized the Llanos, displacing indigenous peoples and establishing cattle ranches. Families like the **Uribes**, **Ochoas**, **Mejías** expanded their herds toward Meta, Casanare, Vichada. **Those same Antioquian ranchers in the Llanos served as a façade for the Cartel de Medellín.** Cattle ranches hid laboratories and airstrips.

### Tranquilandia (March 1984)

Located in the **Llanos del Yarí** (Meta-Caquetá border). The largest cocaine processing complex ever found:

- 19 laboratories
- 8 airstrips
- 13.8 tons of cocaine seized (estimated US$1.2 billion)
- Processing capacity: **30 tons/month**
- Satellite communications
- Housing for 100+ workers

The helicopter **HK-2704-X** from Aerofotos Amórtegui (a company where **Alberto Uribe Sierra** was a partner, father of Álvaro Uribe) appeared there 9 months after Alberto's death.

## 6. Infiltration into Politics

### Presidential campaigns

Escobar financed the campaigns of **Alfonso López Michelsen** and **Belisario Betancur** according to statements by his son Juan Pablo Escobar. The Cartel de Cali financed campaigns of Valle del Cauca congressmen.

### Narco-politics uncovered

The **Nuevo Liberalismo** of **Luis Carlos Galán** and **Rodrigo Lara Bonilla** exposed narco-politics. Lara Bonilla (Minister of Justice) was assassinated on April 30, 1984 by orders of the Cartel, after a debate in Congress where he denounced that the Civil Aeronautics Agency (directed by Álvaro Uribe 1980-82) had a "passive and negligent attitude" toward drug trafficking.

Galán was assassinated on August 18, 1989. His movement was the only one that dared to openly confront narco money in politics.

### Infiltration of the State

- **Civil Aeronautics Agency**: Flight licenses issued without oversight
- **DAS**: Infiltrated by the Cartel and later used to persecute opponents (Uribe-Noguera case)
- **Congress**: ~60 congressmen convicted for parapolitics (alliance with paramilitaries financed by narcos)
- **Attorney General's Office**: Cartel de la Toga protecting politicians allied with narcos

## 7. The Connection with Paramilitarism

### MAS — The embryo

On **November 12, 1981**, **Martha Nieves Ochoa Vásquez** (sister of the Cartel de Medellín leaders) was kidnapped by the M-19. Pablo Escobar, the Ochoas, Rodríguez Gacha and Carlos Lehder created **MAS (Muerte a Secuestradores — Death to Kidnappers)**: 2,230 hitmen searching for the kidnappers. Martha was released after 92 days. MAS was the **embryo of modern paramilitarism in Colombia** and set the precedent for the narco-politician-paramilitary alliance.

### The Convivir

In the 1990s, during the governorship of **Álvaro Uribe** in Antioquia (1995-1997), the **Convivir** (Private Surveillance and Security Cooperatives) were created. 87 were created in Antioquia; at least 13 were linked to paramilitary groups. Cattle ranchers financed them to "protect themselves from the guerrillas." The reality: they were the legal façade of paramilitarism.

### ACCU

The **Autodefensas Campesinas de Córdoba y Urabá** (Peasant Self-Defense Forces of Córdoba and Urabá) were directly financed by cattle ranchers in the region. Carlos Castaño, Vicente Castaño, Salvatore Mancuso — all emerged from this pact between ranchers and narcos. Parapolitics demonstrated that it was not a temporary alliance: it was a political-military project to control territory and land.

## 8. The Thesis of the "Narco-Cattle Ranching Economy"

Historians like **Jorge Orlando Melo**, **Marco Palacios** and **Alejandro Reyes** have documented how drug trafficking inserted itself into the Colombian legal economy not as an anomaly, but as a **logical continuation of the land accumulation model** that dated back to the 19th century:

- **19th century**: Elites accumulated land through civil wars and dispossession
- **20th century**: Land became even more concentrated with the "Green Revolution" and incentives for extensive cattle ranching
- **1970s-80s**: Narcos arrived with dollars and bought what the cattle ranching elite already controlled
- **1990s-2000s**: Paramilitaries dispossessed land for the narcos

**The structural answer to the question "why do all large cattle ranchers end up linked?" is this: there was never a moment when "legitimate" cattle ranching ended and "narco-cattle ranching" began. Because there was never a clear dividing line.**

Structural reasons:

1. **Lack of land registry**: Colombia did not have (and still does not fully have) a reliable land registry. No one knows who the true owner of the land is. Title chains are shaky
2. **Informal economy**: Cattle ranching operates largely in cash, without electronic invoicing, without tax oversight. Perfect for injecting dollars without leaving a trace
3. **Colonization without a State**: The Llanos, the Amazon, the Coast — were colonized without state presence. Land was "acquired" with machete and rifle, not with deeds
4. **Tax exemptions**: The cattle ranching sector has historically enjoyed tax benefits. Land does not pay significant taxes
5. **Social prestige**: Being a cattle rancher in Colombia means being part of the elite. Narcos wanted that status, and the cattle ranching elite accepted the money because it suited them

**State complicity**:

- Politicians received narco financing in exchange for legal protection
- The Civil Aeronautics Agency issued licenses without oversight
- The DIAN did not audit the agricultural sector
- The justice system was weak, corruptible or complicit
- The security forces —in many cases— worked for narcos or paramilitaries

## 9. The Fall of the Cartels

### Pablo Escobar

Killed on **December 2, 1993** in Medellín by the Bloque de Búsqueda, supported by the DEA and **Los Pepes** (Perseguidos por Pablo Escobar — Those Persecuted by Pablo Escobar). Los Pepes were created by Fidel and Carlos Castaño, financed by the Cartel de Cali, and operated with privileged information from the DEA and police.

### The Ochoas

- **Jorge Luis Ochoa** and **Juan David Ochoa**: Surrendered in January 1991, released in 1996. Juan David died in 2013. Jorge Luis lives free.
- **Fabio Ochoa Vásquez**: Extradited to the U.S. in September 2001. Sentenced in 2003 to **30 years in prison** for cocaine trafficking and conspiracy. Released on **December 3, 2024** and deported to Colombia on December 23, 2024.

### Carlos Lehder

Extradited in 1987. Sentenced in the U.S. Released in 2020. Resides in Germany.

### Rodríguez Gacha

Killed on **December 15, 1989**.

### Cartel de Cali

Gilberto Rodríguez Orejuela captured in 1995. Miguel in 1996. Extradited to the U.S. in 2006. Sentenced to 30 years.

## 10. The Legacy

The fortune of the Cartel de Medellín was estimated in the billions of dollars. Part was seized, part was lost, and part —according to multiple investigations— was laundered through cattle ranching, land and politics.

The connections between the Antioquian cattle ranching elite (where the **Uribes** and the **Ochoas** moved) and drug trafficking were not an exception. They were the rule in a Colombia where land was the spoils, cattle ranching the façade, and the State the accomplice.

## 11. How They Played the Dirty Game with the U.S.

### The largest consumer

The U.S. was the world's largest cocaine market. The Colombian cartels would not have existed without American demand. But the relationship was not just supply and demand: **there was a deeper connection between U.S. agencies, Colombian narcos and the anti-communist struggle**.

### International logistics of the cartels

**Cartel de Medellín:** Operated from the mid-1970s until 1993. Led by Pablo Escobar, Gonzalo Rodríguez Gacha ("El Mexicano"), the Ochoa brothers and Carlos Lehder. They controlled production in the jungles of Caquetá and Meta (Tranquilandia). They used small planes from clandestine airstrips to islands in the Bahamas as a launch point to the U.S., with main destinations in New York and Miami.

**Cartel de Cali:** Called "Los Caballeros de Cali" or "Cocaine Inc." for its business-like structure. Led by Gilberto and Miguel Rodríguez Orejuela, José Santacruz Londoño and Pacho Herrera. More sophisticated than Medellín: they operated as a corporation, avoided public violence. They controlled 80% of New York's cocaine by the mid-1990s. Allied with the Italian Camorra for distribution in Europe. They tried to buy a submarine (they were scammed by former Ukrainian military officer Ludwig Fainberg) and bought Russian military helicopters.

### Export routes to the U.S.

- **Medellín**: Small planes from Colombia to the Bahamas, then to Florida. Speedboats and hidden cargo in merchant ships
- **Cali**: Import of heavy machinery with camouflaged banknotes. Santacruz designed global trafficking chains. Alliance with the Juárez Cartel (Amado Carrillo Fuentes) to reach the West Coast, transporting up to 14 tons in a single flight
- **Central American route**: Both cartels used Honduras, Nicaragua and Panama as transit points

### Money laundering in the U.S.

The cartels laundered billions in the U.S.:
- **Real estate in Miami**: Entire buildings, mansions, condos bought with cash through front men
- **Banks**: The Cartel de Cali infiltrated the **Bank of Credit and Commerce International (BCCI)**, the "bank of the narcos". They used Swiss accounts managed by front men. They hired international financial intermediaries
- **Shell companies**: The Cartel de Cali acquired the Grupo Radial Colombiano (GRC), América de Cali, the Hipódromo del Valle, shares in First Interamericas Bank, and the Drogas La Rebaja pharmacy chain
- **Vehicle fleet**: Car rental companies, laundromats, restaurants as fronts

### Landmark cases of the relationship with the U.S.

**Barry Seal (1939-1986)**: TWA pilot, recruited by the CIA (Operation 40, Air America in Vietnam and Laos). Worked for the Cartel de Medellín transporting cocaine in CIA C-123 planes. Arrested in 1984, negotiated with the DEA as an informant. Revealed how the CIA financed the Nicaraguan Contras with drug trafficking resources (Iran-Contra connection). Killed on February 19, 1986 in Baton Rouge by Cartel de Medellín hitmen (MAC-10 shots). His story shows how blurred the line was between "fight against narcos" and "collaboration with narcos."

**Celerino Castillo**: Former DEA agent who denounced that the U.S. government protected the Cartel de Cali because its leaders collaborated with the CIA in anti-communist operations in Central America. He said: "The DEA pursued small-time narcos and protected the big ones who worked for the CIA."

**Michael Levine**: Former DEA agent who denounced that the CIA interfered in anti-drug operations because Colombian narcos financed the Contras in Nicaragua. He wrote about how the war on drugs was sabotaged by CIA geopolitical interests.

**The Iran-Contra connection**: The scandal broke in 1986. The Reagan administration sold arms to Iran (against the embargo) to finance the Nicaraguan Contras. **Oliver North** (National Security Council) handled $47 million in Swiss accounts. The Cartel de Medellín and the Guadalajara Cartel (Caro Quintero, Félix Gallardo) set up infrastructure in Yucatán to bring cocaine into the U.S. with CIA support. The narcos economically supported the Contra in exchange for trafficking facilities.

**Gary Webb and Dark Alliance (1996)**: The San José Mercury News journalist published a series documenting that the CIA sold tons of cocaine in the U.S. to finance the Contras. His investigation showed that the crack that devastated African American communities in Los Angeles was brought by narcos who worked with the CIA. Webb was found dead in 2004 with two gunshot wounds to the head. Officially a "suicide." His case was reopened in 2024.

**Enrique Camarena**: The DEA agent was killed in Mexico in 1985. Former DEA agents claim the CIA was the intellectual author because Camarena discovered the agency's drug trafficking operations.

### The pattern

The pattern has been consistent since the 1980s: **the CIA and other U.S. agencies protected and used Colombian drug traffickers for geopolitical reasons** (anti-communist struggle in Central America). The "war on drugs" was not a real war: it was a **business where everyone played all sides**. And the final irony: Uribe received US$2.8 billion from the U.S. for that same war while his family had ties to the Cartel de Medellín and the CIA had protected Colombian narcos.

**The myth of the "Colombian narco as enemy" collapses when you understand that trafficking was not a supply problem, but a demand and geopolitics problem. Where it is produced is not where it is consumed. But the blame always falls where it is produced, not where it is consumed.**

## 12. How They Got Weapons

### The main routes

The Colombian cartels armed themselves through multiple international routes:

- **Israel**: Weapons manufactured by Israel Military Industries (IMI) and sold through intermediaries. Gray market of Israeli weapons facilitated by private security companies. The most emblematic case is **Yair Klein**
- **Russia/Eastern Europe**: After the fall of the Soviet Union (1991), the black market flooded with Soviet weaponry: AK-47 rifles, PKM machine guns, RPG-7 rocket launchers, C4 explosives, mines. They arrived via Central America or directly to Colombia. The Cartel de Cali bought two Russian military helicopters
- **Central America**: Weapons from the civil conflicts in Guatemala, El Salvador and Nicaragua flowed into Colombia
- **U.S.**: Weapons bought at fairs and legal stores, trafficked via Mexico or the Caribbean
- **Peru and Ecuador**: Weapons from South American military surpluses

### The Yair Klein case

**Yair Klein** (b. 1942, Israel) is the central figure in the training of Colombian paramilitaries and hitmen:

- Former lieutenant colonel in the Israeli Defense Forces, participated in the 1982 Lebanon War
- Founded **Spearhead Ltd.** and **Hod Halanit**, mercenary and security companies
- **Arrival in Colombia**: Contacted by the Cartel de Medellín in the mid-1980s. Taught the cartel how to activate remote-controlled bombs
- Trained paramilitary groups of **Fidel Castaño**, **Carlos Castaño** and the **Magdalena Medio**, financed by landowners and cattle ranchers
- Trained the men of **Gonzalo Rodríguez Gacha** and the paramilitaries of **Puerto Boyacá** (the first organized paramilitary group in the country)
- **Contradictory versions**: Klein has said he traveled to Colombia at the invitation of the U.S., the National Police, and landowners. He accused **Álvaro Uribe Vélez** without naming him directly of having paid for the training. Uribe has denied it and called him a "bandit"
- Sentenced in Colombia in 2001 to **10 years and 8 months** (in absentia) for terrorist instruction
- Captured in Russia in 2007; extradition blocked by the European Court of Human Rights (he feared for his safety in Colombia)
- Also trained the Revolutionary United Front (RUF) in Sierra Leone (sentenced to 16 months)
- In 2012 he testified before a Colombian court that his training was "known and supported by the government of the time"

**The Uribe connection**: Yair Klein claimed that Álvaro Uribe Vélez (then a landowner in Antioquia) was among the financiers of the paramilitary training. Uribe has always denied it. Uribe's private security during and after his presidency has had ties to Israeli instructors.

### The post-Cold War black market

With the dissolution of the USSR (1989-1991), thousands of tons of surplus weapons flooded the black market. The Colombian cartels bought assault rifles, machine guns, explosives and rocket launchers at bargain prices. The weapons arrived through Ukraine, the Balkans and the Baltic states, destined for Panama, Venezuela or directly to Colombia.

### International connections

The Colombian cartels established alliances with:
- **The Italian Camorra**: Cocaine-for-weapons exchange and European distribution
- **The Mexican cartels**: U.S. weapons in exchange for cocaine routes (Cali-Juárez alliance with Amado Carrillo Fuentes)
- **The Russian mafia**: Eastern European weapons in exchange for drugs
- **The FARC and the ELN**: Although they were enemies of the paramilitaries, they bought weapons from the same traffickers
- **British mercenaries**: The Cartel de Cali hired **Peter McAleese** to attack Escobar at Hacienda Nápoles (1989). The operation failed due to a helicopter accident
- **Lebanese Phalanges**: Klein supplied weapons to the Phalanges that participated in the Sabra and Shatila massacres (1982)

## 13. Cattle Ranchers Today — How They Keep Getting Rich

### Fedegán and cattle ranching power

**Fedegán** (Federación Colombiana de Ganaderos — Colombian Federation of Cattle Ranchers) is the most powerful cattle ranching association in Colombia. It manages millions in parafiscal resources from the Fondo Nacional del Ganado.

**José Félix Lafaurie Rivera**: President of Fedegán since 2004. Civil Engineer (U. Javeriana). His trajectory:
- Deputy in Cesar (1981-1985), Vice Minister of Agriculture (1991), Vice Comptroller General (1998-2002)
- Superintendent of Notaries and Registry (2002-2004) — sanctioned by the Attorney General's Office for **influence peddling**
- Married to **María Fernanda Cabal**, senator of the Democratic Center and presidential pre-candidate. Staunch Uribista
- Was part of the Petro government's negotiating team with the ELN (2022-2025)
- **Admitted in 2006** that the cattle ranching association financed paramilitaries as a "self-defense mechanism" against the guerrillas
- His predecessor **Jorge Visbal Martelo** was captured for ties to paramilitaries

Fedegán has:
- Systematically blocked the **comprehensive rural reform** of the Peace Agreement
- Blocked the **land registry update** (which would reveal who owns what)
- Maintained the political power of the cattle ranching sector in Congress
- Pressured against limits on property concentration and the Land Fund

### Yair Klein and the cattle ranchers

The Israeli mercenary mentioned **cattle ranching landowners** as financiers of his paramilitary training, claiming that Uribe was among them (denied by Uribe). The pattern is the same since the 1980s: ranchers finance armed groups to protect their land, those groups become drug traffickers, and the cycle repeats.

### Narco-cattle ranching today

The money laundering mechanisms in the cattle ranching sector have not disappeared. They have evolved:

- **Cryptoassets**: Narco-dollars converted to cryptocurrencies, used to buy farms and cattle without leaving a bank trail
- **Fictitious exports**: Shell companies that declare meat exports to Venezuela, Russia, the Middle East that never happen
- **Loans and microcredits**: The financial system remains permeable to laundering through the agricultural sector
- **Under-invoicing and over-invoicing**: They remain the standard mechanism for justifying income
- **GAO (Organized Armed Groups)**: "Narco-cattle ranching" is no longer just about large cartels. FARC dissidents, the ELN and paramilitary groups use cattle ranching to legalize illicit income in regions like Catatumbo, Cauca, Valle, Córdoba, Meta and Casanare

### Congress and land

The Colombian Congress has historically had a **strong presence of cattle ranchers**. In the 2022-2026 Congress, several congressmen from the Caribbean Coast and the Llanos Orientales are recognized cattle ranchers. Opposition to rural reform and the multipurpose land registry has been led by these sectors. María Fernanda Cabal (Lafaurie's wife) is one of the main voices against the reform.

### Recent cases of investigated cattle ranchers

- **Samuel Santander Lopesierra** "El Hombre Marlboro": Smuggler turned cattle rancher in La Guajira, investigated for money laundering. His money allegedly reached the Petro 2022 campaign through Nicolás Petro
- **Dávila brothers**: Cattle ranchers from Meta, investigated for money laundering and ties to armed groups
- **Jorge Enrique Pizano**: Cattle rancher from Cesar, prosecuted for money laundering
- The Attorney General's Office has detected in recent years cases of cattle ranchers buying land with money from FARC and ELN dissident groups in Valle del Cauca, Córdoba, Meta and Casanare

### The blocked Comprehensive Rural Reform

**Point 1 of the Peace Agreement** with the FARC (2016) included:
- Multipurpose land registry (update land ownership)
- Land bank (redistribute unproductive large estates)
- Property formalization
- Technical assistance and credit for peasants

As of 2026:
- **Less than 30% of the land registry is updated** — it remains a mystery who owns what
- The land bank has redistributed **less than 5% of what was planned**
- Thousands of peasants have been waiting 20+ years for land titling
- Fedegán and the cattle ranching associations' opposition has been the main obstacle
- The Duque government (2018-2022) halted the reform
- The Petro government (2022-2026) has tried to reactivate it, but faces strong opposition in Congress

---

## 14. Conclusion

Drug trafficking in Colombia was not an accident or a deviation from the normal development of the country. It was the **logical continuation of a land accumulation model that dated back to the 19th century**, where cattle ranching served as a front for money laundering, politicians as protectors, the State as accomplice, and the U.S. as the end consumer that —through the CIA— protected narcos for geopolitical reasons.

**The myth of the "Colombian narco as enemy" collapses when you understand that trafficking was not a supply problem, but a demand and geopolitics problem. Where it is produced is not where it is consumed. But the blame always falls where it is produced, not where it is consumed.**

Today, Colombia still has:
- A **land GINI of 0.87** (the highest in the world)
- A **land registry outdated in 95% of municipalities**
- A **largely informal rural economy**
- A **justice system that has never convicted the powerful**
- A **Congress with a strong cattle ranching presence** that blocks any reform

As long as that does not change, the question "why do cattle ranchers end up linked?" will continue to have the same answer: because the system is designed to make it impossible to know who is a legitimate rancher and who is laundering money. And that ambiguity is not an accident: **it is the purpose**.
