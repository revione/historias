# The Israel-Palestine conflict: roots, reasons, and perspectives

> A deep, non-judgmental exploration of the historical, religious, geopolitical, and material reasons behind the conflict.

---

## 1. Why does Israel want territory?

The reasons are multiple and intertwined. There is no single explanation:

- **Security**: Israel needs defensive territorial buffers because of its minimal size.
- **Religion and ideology**: Religious Zionism sees these lands as an eternal divine right.
- **History and refuge**: Israel was founded as a refuge from persecution, especially after the Holocaust.
- **Demography and resources**: Nine million people in 22,000 km² create pressure on land and water.
- **Internal politics**: Territorial expansion is structurally integrated into most Israeli parties.

For Palestinians and neighboring countries, what Israel calls "defense" or "return" is the illegal occupation of someone else's land.

## 2. Religious and ideological reasons: religious Zionism

### The promise in the Torah

The root lies in the Hebrew Bible. Religious Zionism takes the divine promise to Abraham literally:

| Verse | Content |
|---|---|
| **Genesis 12:7** | "To your descendants I will give this land" |
| **Genesis 15:18** | "From the river of Egypt to the great river, the Euphrates" |
| **Genesis 17:8** | "The land of Canaan, as an everlasting possession" |
| **Genesis 28:13** | The promise is renewed with Jacob |
| **Numbers 34 / Ezekiel 47** | More detailed borders: present Israel, West Bank, Gaza, southern Lebanon, southwestern Syria |

The concept of **"Greater Israel" (Eretz Israel)** holds that the modern state should encompass all the lands of biblical Israel, including areas of Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria.

### Three readings of the same text

**Literal reading**: God physically promised that land forever. Settlements fulfill the divine will.

**Conditional reading**: Some rabbis note that the Torah itself says the land is lost if the people fail to keep the commandments. The promise is not unconditional.

**Allegorical reading**: Many modern and secular Jews see "the promised land" as a spiritual metaphor, not as a literal title deed.

### Main interpreters

**Rav Abraham Isaac Kook** reinterpreted secular Zionism as an unconscious instrument of God. Even atheist Jews building Israel were, in his view, unknowingly fulfilling the divine will.

**Zvi Yehuda Kook**, his more radical son, declared after the Six-Day War of 1967 that every meter of conquered territory was sacred and that returning it was religiously forbidden. From that logic, any government negotiating territorial withdrawal commits a grave religious sin.

**Maimonides** read prophecies about the land and the Messiah in political and natural terms, not miraculous ones, leaving less room for territorial fanaticism.

**Martin Buber** supported a Jewish homeland but rejected an exclusively Jewish state. He argued that the promise came with a moral obligation toward the other, citing Leviticus: "You shall love the stranger as yourself, for you were strangers in Egypt."

**Islam's position on the same territory** does not deny that God gave the land to the children of Israel, but adds that the promise was conditional on obedience and that Abraham's covenant continues through Ishmael, ancestor of the Arabs.

### How secular Zionism was ideologically captured

Herzl's Zionism was secular and socialist, not religious. The idea was to create a nation-state for the Jewish people for political reasons, just as France is for the French. Herzl did not argue from the Torah; he argued from nineteenth-century European nationalism. Many Orthodox rabbis rejected him precisely because the project was not religious and seemed to violate the Three Oaths.

Over time, secular Zionism was gradually **theologically legitimized** by the religious sector:

1. **Rav Kook** gave Herzl's secular project a theological justification: even atheist founders were unconscious instruments of God.
2. **The founding of Israel in 1948** created a fait accompli that religious Jews had to relate to through the principle of *bediavad*.
3. **The 1967 war** became the decisive catalyst. The victory was interpreted as a divine sign.
4. **Zvi Yehuda Kook and the settler movement** transformed that religious energy into a concrete territorial strategy.

The result is that religious Zionism, which Herzl himself would likely not have recognized, now dominates Israeli politics in ways secular Zionism did not anticipate. Netanyahu's major governing allies, **Bezalel Smotrich** and **Itamar Ben Gvir**, represent that transformation. The historical irony is striking: the secular movement founded by Herzl became politically dependent on a religious-nationalist reading of the land.

### The descendants of Abraham: an internal theological knot

Islam recognizes Abraham (Ibrahim) as a prophet, and Arabs consider themselves descendants of Ishmael, Abraham's eldest son. Jews consider themselves descendants of Isaac, the younger son. Both groups, from their scriptures, claim descent from Abraham.

The Torah itself is explicit that Ishmael is also Abraham's son. Genesis 17:20 records God's blessing to Ishmael: he will be fruitful and multiply greatly. Jewish tradition distinguishes this from the specific covenant of the land, which Genesis 17:21 renews through Isaac.

This opens questions Jewish theologians do not answer uniformly:

- If Ishmael receives his own divine blessing, do his descendants not also have a legitimate place?
- If the Torah repeatedly commands care for the *ger* — the foreigner living among Israel — how is this reconciled with displacing a population that lived in the territory for centuries?
- Some medieval rabbis saw descent from Abraham as carrying moral obligations beyond territorial claims.

Mainstream religious Zionism resolves the tension by emphasizing the covenant with Isaac and minimizing the rest. A minority of Jewish theologians argues that this is a selective and incomplete reading.

## 3. The Three Oaths of the Talmud: internal religious tension

### What are they?

They appear in the Talmud, in **Ketubot 111a**, as a rabbinic interpretation of a verse from the Song of Songs. They were formulated in the context of exile, after Rome expelled the Jews from the land in 70 CE.

For nearly two thousand years, the dominant rabbinic position was to wait passively for the Messiah and not attempt a mass return by human force. For most of Jewish history, many rabbis considered creating a Jewish state by human initiative to be contrary to religious law. Religious Zionism as a political force is a twentieth-century phenomenon, not the default traditional position.

### The three oaths

| Party | Oath |
|---|---|
| **The Jewish people** (1) | Not to ascend to the Land of Israel "like a wall": no organized mass return by force |
| **The Jewish people** (2) | Not to rebel against the nations among whom they live |
| **The nations of the world** | Not to oppress the Jewish people excessively during exile |

The key word in the third oath is **"excessively"**, which generated later debate.

### The Zionist reinterpretation: the Holocaust broke the contract

Religious Zionists argued that the Holocaust was clearly "excessive oppression." In contractual logic, if one side violates the agreement, the other side is released from its obligations. Therefore, Jews were no longer required to wait passively.

### The anti-Zionist response: Neturei Karta

Neturei Karta maintain that the Jewish oath was unconditional and had no escape clause. They even argue that the Holocaust occurred because Jews had already violated the oaths by beginning the secular Zionist movement. They are a small minority, but intellectually consistent with the classical anti-Zionist reading.

### Bediavad: what already happened

A third path is **bediavad**, meaning "after the fact." Some things may not have been ideal *lechatchilah* — beforehand — but once they exist, they cannot simply be undone. Once Israel survived wars and became a state with millions of inhabitants, many initially anti-Zionist or neutral rabbis accepted it pragmatically as a fact to be dealt with. This position is more common in Orthodox Judaism than public debates often acknowledge.

### Kibbush: conquest as a religious commandment

Religious Zionism also uses **kibbush**, "conquest" or "taking possession" of the Land of Israel, as an active foundation. Medieval rabbis such as Nachmanides argued that inhabiting and conquering the land is not only a Jewish right but an active Torah commandment, based on Numbers 33:53. This shifts the relationship to the land from passive waiting to active obligation. From there, every settlement can be framed as fulfilling a positive commandment.

## 4. The Six-Day War (1967): a turning point

### Context

Since 1948, neighboring Arab states publicly declared that they wanted to destroy Israel. This hostility appeared concretely:

- In 1948, the Arab League secretary called it "a war of extermination"; five armies invaded Israel the day after its founding.
- In 1967, after the war, the Arab League adopted the **Three Noes**: no peace, no recognition, no negotiation with Israel.
- Nasser publicly declared that the objective was Israel's destruction.
- The original PLO charter declared Israel illegal and called for its elimination.
- Arab representatives at the UN described Israel as illegitimate in official records.

### The immediate tension before 1967

In May 1967, Egypt under Nasser:

- Expelled UN peacekeepers from Sinai
- Closed the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping
- Signed military pacts with Syria and Jordan
- Mobilized 100,000 soldiers toward Israel's border

### The war

On June 5, 1967, Israel launched a preventive attack and destroyed almost the entire Egyptian air force on the ground within hours. In six days it conquered:

- **Sinai and Gaza** from Egypt
- **The West Bank and East Jerusalem** from Jordan
- **The Golan Heights** from Syria

Israel tripled its territory in less than a week.

### Why it became religiously decisive

The speed and scale of the victory were interpreted by the religious sector as beyond ordinary military explanation: a divine sign. Gush Emunim saw the conquests of 1948 and 1967 as signs of the coming Messiah. Zvi Yehuda Kook and his followers turned that victory into a concrete religious obligation: populate every conquered area so that returning it would become politically impossible.

## 5. The history of the territory: how we got here

### What existed before Israel was founded?

In 1947, Palestine was not empty:

- **1.2 million** Palestinian Arabs, Muslim and Christian
- **600,000 Jews**, many recently arrived from Europe

Palestinians had been the majority population there for centuries.

### The British and contradictory promises

Palestine had been part of the **Ottoman Empire** since the sixteenth century. Its population was mostly Arab, with Jewish and Christian minorities. There was no formal Palestinian state because the Ottomans did not organize territories as modern nation-states, but the Arab population had lived there for centuries with villages, land, agriculture, and social structures.

During World War I, the Ottoman Empire sided with Germany and Austria-Hungary and lost. Britain and France then divided its territories. The way they did it explains much of Arab distrust toward Western powers.

**Sykes-Picot (1916)** was a secret agreement in which Britain and France divided the Middle East between themselves while Britain was simultaneously promising Arabs independence in exchange for revolt against the Ottomans. France would take Syria and Lebanon; Britain would take Iraq, Jordan, and Palestine. Borders were drawn by ruler on a map, ignoring local populations and realities.

In 1917, the Bolsheviks published the secret treaties of the old Russian regime, including Sykes-Picot. Arabs learned through the press that Britain had been negotiating the partition of their lands while promising them independence. That betrayal became a foundational memory.

Palestine became the **British Mandate**, a form of colonial administration with a vague commitment to future independence. Britain controlled the territory militarily but had no clear moral title over it.

**Promise 1 — to the Arabs (1915):** McMahon promised a large independent Arab state including Palestine if the Arabs revolted against the Ottomans. The Arabs did their part; Britain did not.

**Promise 2 — to the Jews (1917):** The **Balfour Declaration** promised "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people," while also saying that nothing should prejudice the rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. That clause was never effectively enforced.

The same land was effectively promised twice to two different groups.

### The UN partition plan and the Nakba

In 1947, the UN proposed dividing Palestine into two states. Jews accepted. Arabs rejected it, considering it unjust to give territory mostly inhabited by Arabs to recent immigrants.

When Israel declared independence and the 1948 war began, Palestinians experienced the **Nakba** ("catastrophe"):

- Around **750,000 Palestinians** fled or were expelled
- Hundreds of Palestinian villages were destroyed or emptied
- UN **Resolution 194** recognized the right of return, which Israel never implemented
- Today their descendants number around 5 million registered refugees

The **Absentees' Property Law (1950)** confiscated the property of displaced Palestinians. Its definition of "absent" was extremely broad: even Palestinians who never left Palestine but moved from one town to another during the war could be legally treated as absent. These became the **"present absentees"**: physically present in the country but legally absent for property purposes.

The Israeli "new historians," such as Benny Morris, later showed from declassified archives that both flight and expulsion occurred, depending on place and circumstance.

### The Holocaust: the moral foundation of the Israeli narrative

The Holocaust was the systematic genocide of European Jews by Nazi Germany between 1941 and 1945. Around **6 million Jews** were murdered, roughly 67% of European Jewry and 36% of world Jewry.

It was not collateral damage of war. It was a deliberate state policy, with bureaucracy, budget, infrastructure, transport, camps, and chain of command. Jews were first legally excluded, then violently targeted, then confined in ghettos, deported, and finally murdered in extermination camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec, Chelmno, and Majdanek.

The Allied powers had information about extermination camps and chose not to act specifically to stop them. Refugees were often rejected. The MS St. Louis, carrying 937 Jewish refugees, was turned away from Cuba and the United States in 1939; many passengers later died in the Holocaust.

This created two realities:

**The Israeli argument**: no modern people had been targeted for such total extermination while the world largely failed to protect them. Therefore Jews could not rely on others for survival; they needed their own state and army.

**The Palestinian problem**: the guilt for the Holocaust lay in Europe. Palestinians did not commit it. Yet the solution was implemented in Palestine, at the cost of Palestinian land and displacement. The Holocaust explains why Israel exists with such intensity, but it does not answer why Palestinians had to pay the price.

That moral asymmetry is central: Jewish refugees genuinely fled persecution and extermination; Palestinians living there had no responsibility for Europe's crimes.

## 6. Security reasons: Israeli military doctrine

### Foundational mentality

Israel uses the concept **Ein Breira** — "no alternative." The logic is: if Israel loses one total war, it may disappear. A large country can recover from defeat; Israel, because of size and context, fears a single existential defeat could be final.

### Territory as defensive depth

In its original 1948 borders, Israel was at one point only **15 kilometers wide**. Military planners call this lack of strategic depth:

- No time to mobilize reserves
- No space to retreat and reorganize
- No margin for error against simultaneous attacks

**The Golan Heights** gave Syria artillery superiority before 1967; after Israel conquered them, Israel held the high ground.

**The West Bank** is a mountainous area overlooking Israel's coastal plain and Tel Aviv. If hostile forces controlled those heights, they could threaten critical infrastructure, including the airport.

### Pillars of the doctrine

**Preventive attack**: Israel does not wait if it perceives an imminent threat. The Six-Day War is the classic example.

**Take war into enemy territory**: because Israel is so small, wars should not be fought inside Israeli cities.

**Rapid decisive victory**: long wars are economically and strategically dangerous for a reserve-based society.

**Deterrence**: attacking Israel must always appear more costly than any possible benefit.

### Nuclear ambiguity

Israel has never confirmed or denied having nuclear weapons. This policy is called **nuclear ambiguity**, or **amimut**. Independent estimates speak of **80 to 400 nuclear warheads**. The logic is deterrence without triggering regional nuclear proliferation or inspection obligations.

The Dimona reactor was built in the 1950s with French help. In 1986, technician Mordechai Vanunu revealed details of the program to the Sunday Times; Mossad kidnapped him in Rome, brought him to Israel, and he was sentenced to 18 years in prison. Strategists call Israel's last-resort deterrence the **"Samson Option."**

### Intelligence system

**Mossad** operates offensively abroad, including targeted assassinations. Examples include Operation Wrath of God after the 1972 Munich massacre, attacks on Iranian nuclear scientists, and the 2010 assassination of a Hamas commander in Dubai.

**Shin Bet** handles internal security and counterterrorism in Israel and the occupied territories, with an extensive network of informants.

**Unit 8200** handles signals and cyber intelligence. It helped develop **Stuxnet**, which sabotaged Iranian nuclear centrifuges, and is associated with the ecosystem that produced **Pegasus**.

**Pegasus** is surveillance software capable of infiltrating smartphones without user action. NSO Group says it sells only to governments for counterterrorism and crime prevention, but investigations have documented its use against journalists, activists, heads of state, and relatives of Jamal Khashoggi. Israel regulates its export as a weapon, making it a diplomatic tool.

## 7. Resources and demography: the most concrete reasons

### Water: why the West Bank is hard to "let go"

David Ben-Gurion said in the 1950s:

> "Whoever controls the water controls the country."

The main water sources:

**The Jordan River** begins in the Golan Heights. Whoever controls its sources controls how much water flows downstream.

**The Mountain Aquifer** under the West Bank is the most critical. Around **80% of its water flows toward Israel**. If a sovereign Palestinian state controlled it, it could decide how much water to use, sell, contaminate, or let flow to Israel. From the Israeli perspective, that is unacceptable.

**The Coastal Aquifer** under Israel and Gaza is overused and polluted. In Gaza, about **97% of water is not drinkable** by international standards.

After occupying the West Bank, Israel issued **Military Order 92 (1967)**, transferring control of water to the Israeli military. Palestinians need Israeli permission to drill wells; such permits are rarely granted. Israeli settlements have far greater access. Oslo created a joint water committee, but Israel retained effective control, and Palestinian quotas were never adjusted to demographic growth.

### The demographic dilemma

Israel defines itself as **Jewish and democratic**. Those two things function together only while Jews remain a majority. If Israel annexed all occupied territories and gave full citizenship to everyone, Arabs could become a demographic majority. Some Israelis call this the **"demographic bomb."**

| Option | Description | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Two states** | Separate Israeli and Palestinian states | Requires returning territory |
| **Annexation with full citizenship** | Israel keeps everything and grants citizenship | Creates an Arab majority |
| **Annexation without citizenship** | Israel keeps territory but denies Palestinians citizenship | Two classes of people; many jurists call this apartheid |
| **Status quo** | Maintain occupation indefinitely | Solves nothing and creates its own problems |

No option is clean. Each sacrifices something Israel considers fundamental.

## 8. The cycle that has turned for decades

The structure is:

1. Israel takes measures to guarantee its security: territorial, military, resource-based.
2. Those measures generate more hostility.
3. That hostility justifies more security measures.
4. Return to step 1.

The conflict is difficult not only because of religious fanaticism or irrational hatred. There are concrete material interests — water, land, demography — colliding with the equally concrete rights of another population. There are real historical traumas on both sides, making the trust needed for any solution almost impossible to build.

Depending on whom you ask, the same action is called "defense" or "invasion." That is precisely why the conflict has remained unresolved for decades.
