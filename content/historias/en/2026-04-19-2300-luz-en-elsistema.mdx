# The nervous system as a generator of light

_A document in progress — not a closed system, but an open exploration._

---

There is a question running through all of this that none of the reviewed books answers directly: where does the light in the body come from, how does it work, and why is its knowledge not part of what is taught?

The interesting thing is that this question does not need to be metaphysical. It has partial answers in biophysics, neuroscience, and documented history. None is complete on its own. But together they begin to draw something.

---

## The nervous system as starting point

The nervous system is the only system in the body that operates purely through electricity. Electrical impulses travel through neurons, synapses generate electromagnetic fields, and the brain produces measurable frequencies. The spine is the central axis of all of this — the channel through which the central nervous system connects the brain with the rest of the organism.

What is not taught is what happens at a smaller scale, inside the neurons themselves. Each neuron has an internal structure called microtubules — protein filaments that until recently were considered only mechanical scaffolding, the skeleton of the cell. The question some physicists began asking is whether these structures do more than support.

Jack Tuszynski, a physicist at the University of Alberta, found that they do. The mitochondria of neurons — the same organelles that produce cellular energy — emit photons. Real, measurable light. And those photons do not simply scatter: they travel through microtubules and directly affect the electrical activity of the neuronal membrane. There is a light-based communication channel inside each neuron, running parallel to the electrical and chemical channels classical neuroscience already knew. (Rahnama, M., Tuszynski, J. et al., _Emission of Mitochondrial Biophotons and their Effect on Electrical Activity of Membrane via Microtubules_, Journal of Integrative Neuroscience, 2010.)

A later paper went further: myelinated axons — the long cables connecting neurons to one another — may function as photonic waveguides. Myelin, the sheath surrounding them, has optical properties consistent with that function. In other words: the nervous system may be transmitting light throughout its architecture, not only inside each cell. (Kumar, S., Boone, K., Tuszynski, J. et al., _Possible existence of optical communication channels in the brain_, Scientific Reports, 2016.)

Anirban Bandyopadhyay, a physicist at the National Institute for Materials Science in Tsukuba, reached the same territory from another angle. He measured the resonance frequencies of microtubules and found that they oscillate across multiple ranges simultaneously, from kilohertz to terahertz — like an instrument producing not only one note but its whole harmonic series. His hypothesis is that the brain rhythms measured by EEG are beat frequencies of those deeper oscillations — exactly as two nearby musical notes create an audible pulse between them. The brain would not be only a network of neurons switching on and off: it would be a system of nested resonances, with microtubules as fundamental oscillators. (Bandyopadhyay, A. et al., confirmed in multiple publications between 2013 and 2018, including _Transactions on Molecular, Biological, and Multi-Scale Communications_, IEEE.)

What unites Tuszynski and Bandyopadhyay is this: both point toward the nervous system using not only electricity and chemistry to process information, but also light and resonance. And the vehicle of both is the microtubule — the same structure classical neuroscience ignored for decades because it did not know what to ask of it.

This is not consensus. It is active research at the margins of neuroscience, published in peer-reviewed journals, pointing toward something classical neuroscience still does not explain: how the brain synchronizes millions of neurons in real time without a central conductor, at speeds chemistry alone cannot reach.

## What leaves the organism — what Popp measured

If light travels inside neurons, the next question is whether it also exits the organism. The answer is yes, and it was measured before Tuszynski studied microtubules.

In 1922, a Russian biologist named Alexander Gurwitsch observed something strange while studying onion roots: dividing cells in one root accelerated division in cells of another nearby root, even without physical contact. When he placed quartz glass between the roots, the effect persisted; when he used ordinary glass, it disappeared. Quartz transmits ultraviolet light, ordinary glass does not. This led him to propose that cells communicated through light radiation — something he called mitogenetic radiation. (Gurwitsch, A. & Gurwitsch, L., _Mitogenetic Radiation_, 1934.)

With 1920s technology, the hypothesis could not be reliably verified. Fritz-Albert Popp, a German biophysicist, returned to it in the 1970s with modern instruments and confirmed that living organisms constantly emit photons — not as a metabolic byproduct but as organized signal. He called them biophotons. (Popp, F.A. et al., _Recent Advances in Biophoton Research_, World Scientific, 1992.)

What he found was not only that they existed, but that they had a specific property: in healthy organisms, the emission is _coherent_ — organized, synchronized, capable of transmitting information. Like a laser, not like a candle. In organisms under chronic stress or disease, the emission becomes chaotic. The light is still there, but disordered, with no capacity to communicate. Each living cell emits and absorbs up to 100,000 photons per second, with the DNA of the cell nucleus as the main source. (Popp, F.A., _Properties of biophotons and their theoretical implications_, Indian Journal of Experimental Biology, 2003.)

Popp proposed an inversion of the paradigm: light is not a byproduct of metabolism. It could be its regulator. The variable that organizes life from within.

That hypothesis remains debated. But the existence of biophotons and their variation according to the state of the organism is documented and replicated. It is not speculation — it is measurement. And it connects directly with what Tuszynski found inside: the same source — mitochondria — produces the light that travels through microtubules and the light that exits the organism outward.

## The heart that does not receive, but sends

The nervous system does not operate alone. It has a synchronizer that classical anatomy did not anticipate.

The heart has its own nervous system — approximately 40,000 neurons — and generates the most powerful electromagnetic field in the body: its electrical field is about 60 times greater in amplitude than the brain's, and its magnetic field is more than 100 times stronger, measurable several meters from the body with specialized magnetometers. (McCraty, R., _The Energetic Heart_, in Rosch & Markov eds., _Clinical Applications of Bioelectromagnetic Medicine_, Marcel Dekker, 2004.)

The most counterintuitive point is the flow of information. The heart sends more signals to the brain than it receives. It is not a passive organ responding to instructions. It is a synchronizer.

When the cardiac rhythm enters what HeartMath calls coherence — an ordered rhythmic pattern — the entire nervous system synchronizes with it. Breathing, blood pressure, brain activity: everything comes into phase. And what generates that state is not an isolated breathing technique: it is sustained emotional states. Genuine appreciation. Real gratitude. Non-forced calm. The physiological correlate of those states is measurable in the heart-rhythm pattern. (McCraty, R., _Science of the Heart, Volume 2_, HeartMath Institute, 2015.)

The implication matters: emotional state is not a consequence of the body. It is a variable that directly regulates the quality of what the system generates.

## The switch no one teaches how to use

Stephen Porges, a neuroscientist, described in the 1990s what is now known as polyvagal theory: the autonomic nervous system does not have two states, but three, organized in an evolutionary hierarchy. (Porges, S., _The Polyvagal Theory_, W.W. Norton, 2011.)

The newest one — the ventral vagal state — is the state of real safety, social connection, integration. It is where creativity, regeneration, and coherence occur. The sympathetic state is threat, fight or flight — it consumes more energy than it produces. The dorsal vagal state is collapse, freezing.

It should be said honestly: polyvagal theory has legitimate criticism from neuroanatomists and evolutionary biologists who point to inconsistencies with established anatomy. It is not closed scientific consensus. But as a map of the autonomic nervous system's territory, it is widely used in clinical contexts, especially trauma work.

What it describes is useful regardless of technical debates: a nervous system living in chronic sympathetic state cannot generate coherence. Not metaphorically — physiologically. The states are incompatible.

And the modern environment is designed — not necessarily intentionally, but functionally — to keep most people in that state almost permanently. Chronic attention fragmentation briefly activates the sympathetic axis with each interruption; multiplied hundreds of times a day, it keeps the system in permanent micro-alert. Artificial light at night suppresses melatonin, which is not only a sleep hormone but a powerful neurological antioxidant. Gut inflammation introduces direct interference into the vagus nerve — 70% of the autonomic nervous system resides in the gut and the vagus connects it directly with the brain. Chronic economic scarcity keeps the amygdala activated and the prefrontal cortex measurably suppressed, as documented by Mullainathan & Shafir in _Scarcity_ (Times Books, 2013) and clinically by Gabor Maté in _When the Body Says No_ (Vintage Canada, 2019).

None of this requires a conscious conspiracy to function as suppression of coherence. The result is the same regardless of intention.

## What was suppressed

Here the historical line enters. And what appears is not speculation — it is documented persecution.

The traditions that worked with the body as an instrument of generation were precisely the ones that did not survive with their technical content intact.

Gnosticism proposed gnosis: direct, experiential knowledge. Not faith in external doctrines but recognition of something that could be perceived from within. Gnostics described an inner luminous spark — not as abstract theology but as something accessible through practice. That structure was incompatible with any institution that needed intermediaries to survive. Gnostic teachings were declared heretical by orthodox Christianity in the fourth century, their texts forbidden and often burned. For centuries what remained were the criticisms made against them by Church Fathers — until December 1945, when an Egyptian farmer discovered a sealed jar near the cliffs of Jabal al-Tarif containing 13 papyrus codices with 52 Gnostic texts. The manuscripts had been buried for about 1,600 years. They are believed to have been hidden by monks when possession of those writings became a serious crime under Bishop Athanasius of Alexandria. (Robinson, J.M. ed., _The Nag Hammadi Library in English_, HarperOne, 1977. Pagels, E., _The Gnostic Gospels_, Random House, 1979.)

Tantra worked directly with the body as vehicle, not obstacle — the opposite of dominant ascetic traditions. Its kundalini model describes energy at the base of the spine rising through the central nervous system. The anatomical correspondence is interesting: the seven centers Tantra places along the spine correspond to major nerve plexuses in Western anatomy — inferior hypogastric, superior hypogastric, solar/celiac, cardiac, and upward. (Feuerstein, G., _Tantra: The Path of Ecstasy_, Shambhala, 1998. Eliade, M., _Yoga: Immortality and Freedom_, Princeton University Press, 1958.) Recent research on meditation and pranayama shows that they stimulate the vagus nerve and shift the nervous system toward parasympathetic dominance: reduced cortisol, stabilized heart rhythm, improved emotional regulation. In other words, they produce exactly the state Popp associates with greater coherence in biophoton emission.

The tantric practices that survived after being absorbed into majority traditions lost their technical content and preserved only symbolic form.

Sufism preserved practices that induce specific nervous-system states: dhikr — rhythmic repetition — music, dervish movement, controlled breathing. Idries Shah documented that Sufism existed as a tradition of direct knowledge before Islam and Christianity, and that part of Gnostic thought migrated there when its practitioners adopted Islam. (_The Sufis_, Doubleday, 1964.) What Tantra, Gnosticism, and Sufism have in common is exactly this: all three work with the physical body as generator, not with obedience as passive posture.

What survived of all of them in institutional versions is exactly the part that does not teach how to generate anything from within. Contemplation. Obedience. Waiting for external salvation.

## The traditions that arrived intact

Of all the traditions that worked with the body as generator, two reached the twentieth century with their practices more or less intact: Tibetan Vajrayana and Dzogchen Buddhism, and Kashmir Shaivism. It is not accidental that they are related — there is academic documentation of direct influence from Kashmir Shaivism on the development of Dzogchen during the eleventh and twelfth centuries. (Sanderson, A., _The Śaiva Age_, in Genesis and Development of Tantrism, University of Tokyo, 2009.)

The most specifically relevant practice for this investigation is tummo — in Tibetan, "inner fire." It is not a metaphor. It is a technique combining specific breathing with sustained visualization and producing physiological effects that can be measured with a thermometer.

Herbert Benson, a Harvard cardiologist, spent a decade studying its effects in the Himalayas. In 1982 he published in _Nature_ that advanced practitioners could raise the peripheral temperature of their fingers by up to 8.3 degrees Celsius during practice — without muscular movement, without external heat source. (Benson, H. et al., _Body temperature changes during the practice of g Tum-mo yoga_, Nature, 295, 1982.) A later, more controlled study separated the two components of the technique: vase breathing directly produces thermogenesis, while visualization extends and deepens the effect. Most relevantly: researchers taught the breathing to people with no meditative experience, and they also produced temperature increases. The technical content is separable from the religious framework. (Kozhevnikov, M. et al., _Neurocognitive and Somatic Components of Temperature Increases during g-Tummo Meditation_, PLOS ONE, 2013.)

The connection with light is direct. Tummo produces thermogenesis — metabolic heat. Metabolic heat and biophoton emission share the same source: mitochondria. A practice that raises peripheral temperature by 5 to 8 degrees without muscular movement is radically altering mitochondrial activity. And mitochondria are the main source of biophotons in neurons according to Tuszynski's work. The bridge between Dzogchen's "inner fire" and twentieth-century biophysics is not metaphorical — it is mitochondrial.

Dzogchen goes further. Its texts describe three phases of meditative experience: bliss, clarity, and non-conceptuality. The clarity phase — _gsal ba_ in Tibetan — is described specifically as luminosity. Not as metaphor: as direct perception of light. Longchenpa's instructions, the great Dzogchen systematizer of the fourteenth century, include practices working explicitly with breath and visualization of inner light. And the awakened state they describe is not located in the abstract mind — it is in the totality of the body. (Ray, R., _Touching Enlightenment: Finding Realization in the Body_, Sounds True, 2008.)

What makes these traditions unique is that they have uninterrupted transmission lineages up to the present and a documented technical body in texts that survived. They are not reconstructions. They are instructions transmitted from teacher to student for centuries, with the physical body as laboratory.

## Where the two lines touch

Two lines ran in parallel throughout this document. One scientific: the nervous system uses light and resonance to process information, and emits biophotons whose coherence varies with the state of the organism. One historical: the traditions that worked with the body as generator were suppressed or emptied of technical content, and those that survived intact described exactly that process in other terms.

The point where they touch is concrete.

Tummo, tantric pranayama, and Sufi dhikr all produce the same measurable physiological effect: they activate the vagus nerve, shift the nervous system toward parasympathetic dominance, reduce cortisol, stabilize heart rhythm, and alter mitochondrial activity. That is, they bring the organism into precisely the ventral vagal state Porges describes as the only one in which integration and regeneration occur — and which, according to Popp's measurements, corresponds to coherent biophoton emission — and which, according to Tuszynski's work, is the state in which light inside neurons can operate as an information channel.

It is not a metaphor. It is the same variable described from three angles across centuries: Dzogchen's inner fire, Gnosticism's luminous spark, and Popp's coherent biophotons describe the same state of the organism.

## What activates the generator — what is already known

The document described in detail what turns coherence off. But a great deal is also known about how to turn it on.

The most documented work comes from HeartMath. Its research on 1.8 million cardiac biofeedback sessions shows that physiological coherence can be consistently induced. The most effective method is not rhythmic breathing alone, but the combination of slow breathing with the active generation of a specific emotional state — genuine appreciation, not simulated. Breathing slowly toward the heart area while sustaining real gratitude produces the coherent heart-rhythm pattern reproducibly within minutes. (Elbers, J. & McCraty, R., _From Dysregulation to Coherence_, Sage Journals, 2025.)

The vagus nerve has its own entry points. Extended exhalation — inhaling for four counts, exhaling for six or eight — directly activates the parasympathetic branch. Cold water contact on the face, especially forehead and cheekbones, immediately lowers heart rate through the mammalian diving reflex. Singing, humming, gargling — all stimulate the vagus nerve through throat muscles and vocal cords, which are directly innervated by the ventral vagal branch. This is not metaphor: it is why Sufi dhikr and mantra chanting have the physiological effect they have.

What these practices have in common is that they are accessible, require no infrastructure, and produce measurable effects in minutes. They also share something more uncomfortable: they are exactly the kind of knowledge that is not taught in any educational system, does not generate dependence on any product or institution, and works independently of any belief framework.

What is still not precisely known is whether these practices directly and measurably increase biophoton coherence. Popp's research and HeartMath's research run in parallel and point in the same direction, but they have not been experimentally connected in a systematic way. That remains an open question.

## What remains open

Which concrete practices reproducibly and measurably increase biophoton coherence? Is there direct historical continuity between suppressed traditions, or did they independently converge on the same practices because they were describing the same mechanism? Is there evidence that suppression was intentional, or is it enough that the result is the same regardless of intention?

And the most important question: if the source is inside, what concrete conditions of daily life activate it, and how is that distinguished from just another belief system?

Gurwitsch did not need a theoretical framework to discover biophotons. He needed a precise question and a method that could separate variables. The question was simple: can something non-material pass between two roots? The method was quartz glass versus ordinary glass.

The analogous question is: under what concrete conditions does the certainty from which this investigation emerged appear or disappear? Notice what precedes it — sleep, food, movement, type of attention, social contact, absence of certain stimuli. Notice what interrupts it. Over time, those patterns begin to reveal the conditions of the generator from within, without needing mediation through any belief system.

That is exactly what surviving traditions call practice. And it is what biophysics still cannot do by itself: measure the subjective experience accompanying changes in biophoton emission.

The two lines need to meet. And that meeting can only happen in an organism that observes.
