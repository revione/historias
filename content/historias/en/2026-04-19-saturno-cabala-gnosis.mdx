## The geometric chain: cube → hexagon → hexagram → Star of David

The geometric connection is real and documentable. A cube in isometric perspective generates a hexagon. The hexagon inscribes a hexagram (six-pointed star). The hexagram is the Star of David, the central symbol of Zionism and the State of Israel. This visual chain is the starting point of multiple symbolic traditions.

The connections are noted on three levels. Geometric: cube → hexagon → hexagram → Star of David. Symbolic-esoteric: in occultist traditions, Saturn is associated with the hexagram; Saturn's number in kabbalistic numerology is 6 (six points, six triangles, hexagon of six sides); Kabbalah works with sacred geometry. Astronomical: Orion is a constellation with great symbolic weight in ancient cultures of the Middle East, including those that preceded Judaism.

## Saturn in the Jewish texts

The connection to Saturn is not external to Judaism — it is inside its own texts.

In Hebrew, Saturn is called Shabbatai (שַׁבַּתַּאי). It comes from the same root as Shabbat, the sacred Jewish Saturday. The Jewish Sabbath is directly named in honor of Saturn. In Kabbalah, Saturn/Shabbatai corresponds to the sefirah Binah in the Tree of Life. Binah represents supreme understanding, the divine feminine, and is one of the highest sefirot.

The Sefer Yetzirah (Book of Formation) is one of the oldest kabbalistic texts. In it, the universe is structured through 10 Sefirot, 22 Hebrew letters, and planets assigned to letters and cosmic functions. Saturn is assigned the letter Bet (ב), the first letter of the Torah ("Bereshit" = In the beginning), associated with wisdom and creation. Saturn in this cosmology is the slowest planet visible to the naked eye; that is why it is associated with time, old age, permanence, and the Shabbat as the day of cessation and rest. It corresponds to Binah, the third sefirah, which is the upper limit of what is comprehensible to the human mind. Above it are Chokhmah and Keter, considered inaccessible.

The Merkaba (מרכבה) literally means "chariot" or "throne." It is represented as a three-dimensional Star of David that rotates to form a cube. It is a central figure in Jewish mysticism, appears in ancient Jewish texts, and geometrically connects the hexagram with three-dimensional structures like the cube.

## The Tzimtzum

The Tzimtzum (צִמְצוּם) was developed primarily by Isaac Luria (the Ari) in the sixteenth century. It literally means "contraction" or "concentration" in Hebrew.

Before creation, according to this doctrine, God was everything and was in everything. There was no space for anything else because God filled all of existence. The central question is: how can something exist that is not God, if God is infinite? Luria's answer was that God contracted into himself, leaving an empty space — and in that space he created the universe.

The process: Ein Sof (אין סוף), the infinite God without limits who fills everything, withdraws to a central point, leaving a circular void. Into that void enters a line of divine light called Kav. That light organizes the 10 Sefirot and forms the universe.

The philosophical implications: creation was not an act of expansion but of renunciation. God created the world by limiting himself. The void is not the absence of God, but the space God chose not to occupy.

Binah is the sefirah where the infinite light finds its first limit, its first defined form. That is why it is associated with Saturn, the planet of limit and contraction. The real connection of Saturn/Binah is with the form and limit of things, time as restriction, death and transformation, and the Shabbat as restriction of ordinary time.

## The Hakafot: walking in circles

The Hakafot (הקפות) literally means "circumambulations" or "going around." It is practiced at Simchat Torah (dancing in circles with the Torah scrolls), at Hoshana Raba (circling the altar), and at Jewish weddings (the bride circles the groom seven times).

The circle has no beginning and no end; it represents the infinite and divine, connecting to the Ein Sof. Surrounding something is an act of consecration and protection. The deep geometric connection is that the Tzimtzum describes a circular void that God creates, and the Hakafot physically reproduce that act. Walking in a circle is ritually recreating the primordial contraction.

In Islam there is something analogous: the Tawaf consists of walking around the Kaaba in Mecca. The Kaaba is literally a cube.

## Gnostic texts of Nag Hammadi

They are a collection of 52 ancient texts discovered in 1945 in Egypt. Written in Coptic (ancient Egyptian), dated approximately to the third and fourth centuries AD. They were hidden, possibly by monks to protect them from destruction.

The word Demiurge comes from ancient Greek: demi (people) + urgos (worker, craftsman), literally "craftsman of the people" or "maker." The origin of the word is philosophical, not Gnostic: Plato uses it in the dialogue Timaeus to describe a being that did not create the universe out of nothing but took chaotic matter and gave it form. For Plato the Demiurge is neutral. The Gnostics took this concept and developed it: the Demiurge is ignorant or malevolent, organizes chaos but traps souls in matter, and is inferior to the true God of light.

The word Gospel comes from Greek: eu (good) + angelos (message, messenger), literally "good news." In the first and second centuries AD it was not exclusive to official Christianity. Any text that conveyed a revelation or saving teaching could be called that. The canonical Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) were accepted by the official Church and narrate the life and miracles of Jesus, focused on faith and external salvation. The Gnostic Gospels (Thomas, Philip, Mary, Judas) were rejected and destroyed. They transmit secret teachings, gnosis (inner knowledge), and present Jesus as a teacher who teaches you to save yourself.

The Gospel of Thomas narrates no miracles and no crucifixion. It is 114 sayings of Jesus. It begins: "These are the secret words that the living Jesus spoke and that Didymus Judas Thomas wrote down."

The central themes of Gnosticism: the material world was created by the Demiurge, an inferior being, not the true God. The true God is far beyond, pure light and spirit. Gnosis is direct knowledge of the divine, not blind faith, but direct experience. The goal is to remember that you are a divine spark trapped in matter. The Archons are beings who govern the material world, planetary guardians that keep souls trapped in the cycle of matter. Each Archon controls a planet. Saturn/Yaldabaoth is frequently identified with the Demiurge.

The most important texts: The Gospel of Thomas (secret words of Jesus), The Gospel of Philip (sacraments and symbolism), The Apocryphon of John (describes the creation by the Demiurge), The Pistis Sophia (the descent and ascent of the soul), The Gospel of Truth (attributed to Valentinus).

## Jesus in historical context

Most historians agree that Jesus was a real Jew born in Galilee approximately in 4-6 BC. He was a rabbi and preacher within Judaism. He gathered followers. He was crucified by Rome under Pontius Pilate approximately in 30-33 AD. Jesus did not come to found Christianity. He himself was a practicing Jew.

For Judaism, the Messiah must fulfill concrete things: rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem, gather all Jews in Israel, bring world peace, make all of humanity recognize God. Jesus fulfilled none of these requirements. For Judaism, the Messiah is a concrete political and spiritual leader who physically transforms the world, not a spiritual figure who promises a kingdom in the afterlife.

The Gnostic texts present Jesus in a radically different way. He did not come to die for sins. He came to teach that the God of the Old Testament is the Demiurge, and that the Jews were worshiping the Demiurge, not the true God. In Gnosticism he is a messenger of light who comes to awaken trapped souls. This explains why these texts were destroyed by the official Church.

## The Jewish religious elite of the first century

The Pharisees arose approximately in the second century BC. They were not priests, but teachers and interpreters of the law. They had great influence over the common people. They believed in the written Torah and in the oral Torah (transmitted tradition). Their power lay in controlling the interpretation of the law, managing the synagogues, and being the teachers of the people. They would be the modern equivalent of the rabbis.

The Priests were the religious aristocracy of Israel, descendants of the tribe of Levi, specifically of Aaron. Only they could officiate in the Jerusalem Temple. The highest title was the High Priest (Kohen Gadol). Their power lay in total control of the Jerusalem Temple, which was the economic, religious and political center. They collected religious taxes, controlled sacrifices and rituals, and managed the Temple treasury.

The Sadducees were the priestly and aristocratic elite. They collaborated openly with Rome. They did not believe in the resurrection or in angels. They accepted only the written Torah, nothing of oral tradition. They disappeared when Rome destroyed the Temple in 70 AD.

The Sanhedrin was the real power structure. The supreme Jewish court, composed of 71 members. It included Pharisees, Sadducees, and priests. Presided over by the High Priest. It had judicial, legislative, and religious powers. Its competencies: judging religious and civil cases, controlling the religious calendar, overseeing the Temple and its finances, representing the Jewish people before Rome.

Jesus confronted everyone. The Pharisees he called hypocrites publicly, said they followed the letter of the law but not its spirit, and questioned their interpretive authority. To the Priests: he entered the Temple and expelled the merchants (the Temple was their source of wealth; this probably sealed his fate). To the Sanhedrin: they judged him internally before handing him over to Rome, accusing him of blasphemy for saying he was the Son of God.

## Rome, Pontius Pilate, and the power structure

In the first century, Rome controlled the entire Mediterranean, western and central Europe, North Africa, the Middle East including Judea, and Anatolia (modern-day Turkey). It was the largest empire in the Western world at that time.

Rome governed without destroying local structures — it used them. Roman governors controlled militarily. Local elites maintained social order. Rome collected taxes through local elites. Local religion was respected as long as it did not threaten Roman power.

Judea was a small but strategically important province: it connected Egypt with Syria, was a key trade route, and had a population very difficult to control. The Jews were known for being extremely resistant to Romanization.

Pontius Pilate was the Prefect of Judea from 26 to 36 AD. He was not a first-rate governor. Judea was considered a problematic and minor province. Pilate had military power but was politically dependent on the governor of Syria. His personal situation was delicate: he had already had several conflicts with the Jewish population, had used Temple money to build an aqueduct, the people had rebelled, and Rome had already reprimanded him several times.

The Jewish elite knew exactly how to pressure him. The phrase from the Gospel of John: "If you release this man, you are no friend of Caesar" was devastating because Pilate was already under suspicion in Rome, and the accusation of protecting a rival to Caesar could cost him his position or worse.

What the Jewish elite did control: the entire religious system, the Temple and its economy, the religious courts, the interpretation of the law, Jewish public opinion, and trade networks throughout the Mediterranean. What they did not control: the military, foreign policy, executions (they needed Roman permission), and imperial taxes.

In the first century AD there were significant Jewish communities in Alexandria (one of the largest, controlled trade), Rome (influential in the imperial capital), Antioch (key commercial center), Babylon (ancient and important community), and Athens (intellectual and commercial presence). This network gave them transregional influence that few local elites had.

The tacit agreement between Rome and the Jewish elite: Rome implicitly said "keep the people quiet, collect taxes, don't cause military problems." The Jewish elite implicitly said "we control the people, respect our Temple and our religion." Jesus was dangerous to both: for the elite he threatened their religious and economic authority; for Rome a preacher with crowds was a potential rebellion leader. What happened with Pilate was a political negotiation where each side used the other.
