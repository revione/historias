This document defines the principles, safeguards, and pitfalls that guide investigations in this project. It is not an exhaustive methodological manual: it is a compass for keeping research free, honest, and resistant to the distortions introduced by power, money, and ideology — in both science and history.

It is based on concrete investigations already published in this project: the replication crisis in science, the mechanisms of geopolitical control documented across eleven countries and institutions, and the patterns of institutional capture traced from the 16th century to the present.

### TL;DR

1. **Separate the layers.** Do not mix facts (Layer 1) with what a source claims (Layer 2) with third-party interpretations (Layer 3). What power says about itself is always Layer 2.
2. **Evidence over authority.** A Nobel laureate can be wrong. Someone without credentials can be right. The argument is in the data, not in the résumé.
3. **"Not replicable" does not mean "false."** Between 40% and 89% of published papers also fail to replicate. The question is not "did it replicate?" but "is there positive evidence that it is false?"
4. **Follow the money and the legal architecture.** Ask who funds, who owns the outlet, what law protects the entity, what is not being said.
5. **If you cannot conclude, say so.** Research does not fail by not reaching certainty. It fails by faking it.
6. **Ask: if this were false, how would I know?** If there is no answer, you are not investigating — you are confirming.
7. **Acknowledge your own biases.** The project has a structural bias: it investigates hidden power, publishes when it finds patterns, has no editorial counterbalance. This does not invalidate the findings, but it requires declaring it (§10.9).

## 1. Fundamental Principles

### 1.1 Free Inquiry

Every question is legitimate. Every hypothesis deserves examination. The only prior filter is intellectual honesty: a line of investigation is not discarded due to political discomfort, institutional pressure, or the consensus of the era.

This does not mean every claim is valid. It means no claim is discarded without examination. **Legitimate to examine does not mean scientifically valid.** A non-falsifiable hypothesis can be the subject of historical, philosophical, or journalistic investigation, but it cannot be presented as science. The distinction lies in the method, not in the topic (see §6).

### 1.2 Primacy of Evidence over Authority

A scientist does not have more credibility for being called a scientist. A paper is not true because it is published in a high-impact journal. A consensus is not correct because it is majoritarian.

Each claim is evaluated by its foundations, not by who makes it. Institutional authority can be a useful informational shortcut — a peer-reviewed journal filters better than an anonymous forum — but the shortcut does not replace examination. Authority points, it does not certify.

### 1.3 Epistemic Honesty

Saying "I don't know" is a legitimate answer. Saying "the public data do not allow distinguishing between these hypotheses" is a valid conclusion. Research does not fail when it does not reach certainty; it fails when it fakes a certainty it does not have.

### 1.4 The Researcher's Biases

Power is not the only source of distortion. The researcher also introduces biases — and they are more dangerous because they are invisible to those who hold them.

- **Confirmation bias.** Seeking and remembering only the evidence that confirms what we already believe. The antidote is to actively seek the best evidence against one's own hypothesis.
- **Survivorship bias.** The cases of rejected scientists who later turned out to be right (Semmelweis, Marshall) are memorable. The cases of rejected scientists who were indeed wrong are countless and forgotten. That some outsiders were right does not mean all of them are.
- **Proportionality bias.** Not every criticism of a heterodox position is institutional censorship. Some are simply correct. Distinguishing one from the other requires examining the argument, not assuming conspiracy.
- **Narrative bias.** The brain prefers clean stories to messy data. An explanation that connects eleven patterns into a single narrative is satisfying — and for that very reason, suspicious. Real complexity does not always form patterns.

**The rule:** Before publishing, ask: what evidence would make me change my mind? If there is no answer, you are not investigating — you are confirming.

### 1.5 Transparency of Sources and Method

Every reader must be able to trace each claim back to its origin. If a source is weak, it is said to be weak. If a connection is speculative, it is said to be speculative. The reader should not need to trust the writer: they should be able to verify.

## 2. The Layers of Reading

Adapted from the principle already applied in the analyses of religious traditions, this criterion extends to all investigations:

| Layer | What it contains | How it is treated |
|---|---|---|
| **Layer 1** | Verifiable facts: archaeological data, official documents, public records, replicated experiments. | Presented as evidence. The source is cited. The degree of certainty is indicated. |
| **Layer 2** | Internal content of a source: what a text, a person, or an institution claims. | Presented as the source's claim, not as established truth. Quoted verbatim or paraphrased faithfully. |
| **Layer 3** | What one source says about another: interpretations, critiques, connections drawn by third parties. | Presented as external interpretation. It is identified who makes it and from what position. |

**Golden rule:** Mixing layers without signaling it produces confusion. Confusing the reader is the first sin of research.

### 2.1 Beyond the Three Layers: What Power Produces

In geopolitical and institutional investigations, the three layers are not enough. Three additional distinctions that power systematically exploits must be added:

**Legal truth ≠ material truth.** An operation can be perfectly legal and deeply unjust. The Absentees' Property Law (Israel, 1950) declared "absentees" Palestinians who never left Palestine. The 99-year leases in East Jerusalem are technically rentals, practically sales. The Lex Krupp was written for a single company. In all three cases, legal truth conceals material truth. **Research must look at the legal form, not only the visible content.**

**Media truth ≠ factual truth.** The media do not necessarily lie. But they select, emphasize, omit, and frame. Five groups control all of the German press. Netanyahu traded $500 million in regulatory favors for positive coverage from Walla! The question is not only "what does the news say?" but "who owns the outlet, what interests do they have, and what is not being published?"

**Historical truth ≠ taught truth.** The Nakba is not taught in Israeli schools. The Allies did not bomb the railway lines to Auschwitz in 1944 even though they had the coordinates. Sykes-Picot was secret until the Bolsheviks published it. Official history does not lie: it omits. And omission is the most effective form of lying because it leaves no evidence of itself.

**Additional rule:** When an investigation touches power — state, corporate, religious, media — the official version must be treated as Layer 2 (what power says about itself), not as Layer 1 (verifiable fact). Power produces its own narrative. The researcher's job is to distinguish it from the facts.

**Warning:** This criterion introduces a structural bias. The framework is designed to detect hidden power. It has no equivalent machinery for detecting when power is legitimate, when a consensus is correct, or when silence on a topic is irrelevant rather than conspiratorial. The researcher must remember that the fact that a mechanism *could* explain something does not mean it *does* explain it. The presence of a pattern is not proof of its operation.

## 3. Criteria for Evaluating Claims

### 3.1 Sources

- **Primary source > secondary source > tertiary source.** An original paper weighs more than a popular science article. A court document weighs more than a press note.
- **Multiple and independent sources > single source.** A claim confirmed by three sources that do not cite each other has more weight than a claim repeated by three sources that copy the same one.
- **Source with direct interest > red flag.** A source that benefits economically, politically, or institutionally from what it claims is not discarded, but is examined with greater rigor.

### 3.2 Method

- **Replicability.** An experiment replicated by independent groups weighs more than a single experiment. But non-replication is not synonymous with falsehood. The replication crisis has shown that between 40% and 89% of published scientific papers — in peer-reviewed journals, by prestigious researchers, at elite institutions — also cannot be replicated. Non-replication is the normal state of published science, not an anomaly that distinguishes the legitimate from the illegitimate. Demanding robust replication as a standard for accepting a claim is reasonable; rejecting a claim solely because it has not been replicated is applying a yardstick that institutional science itself does not pass.
- **Falsifiability.** A hypothesis that cannot be tested cannot be treated as scientific. It may be a legitimate subject of historical, philosophical, or speculative investigation (see §1.1), but it must not be presented with the language or authority of science. Falsifiability distinguishes the scientific from the non-scientific, not the true from the false.
- **Explanatory power.** A hypothesis that explains more with fewer assumptions is preferable — but not automatically correct.

### 3.3 Certainty

Every claim must be accompanied by an indication of certainty:

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Very high** | Replicated by multiple independent groups with rigorous methodology, or documented by multiple consistent primary sources. |
| **High** | Well documented with some limitation (single but reliable source, broad but not unanimous consensus). |
| **Medium** | Partial evidence, sources with potential bias, or contested consensus. |
| **Low** | Scarce evidence, indirect sources, or reliance on uncorroborated testimonies. |
| **Very low / speculative** | No direct evidence; inference, rumor, or untested hypothesis. |

## 4. Things We Want to Avoid

### 4.1 Reasoning Fallacies

- **Appeal to authority (ad verecundiam).** "It's true because a Nobel laureate said so." No. The argument is worth what its structure and evidence are worth, not the résumé of whoever makes it.
- **Appeal to consensus (ad populum).** "It's true because most scientists accept it." Consensus is a data point — it indicates where the center of gravity of a discipline lies — but it is not an argument. The history of science is full of consensuses that were incorrect.
- **Appeal to tradition (ad antiquitatem).** "It's true because it has always been believed." The age of a belief does not make it correct. The novelty of a hypothesis does not make it incorrect.
- **Appeal to consequences (ad consequentiam).** "If this were true, the consequences would be terrible, therefore it cannot be true." Reality has no obligation to be pleasant.
- **Straw man.** Refuting a caricatured version of a position is not refuting the real position.
- **False equivalence.** Two contested positions do not carry the same weight just because both exist. Evidence decides the weight.

### 4.2 Institutional Fallacies

- **The label as argument.** Calling something "pseudoscience," "conspiracy," or "denialism" is not an argument: it is a rhetorical maneuver. If a claim is false, it must be shown why it is false, not disqualified with a label.
- **Prestige as shield.** A prestigious institution can be wrong. A marginalized researcher can be right. The history of science is full of both cases. Institutional prestige does not immunize against error.
- **Peer review as certificate of truth.** Peer review filters gross errors and improves methodological quality. It does not guarantee that a result is correct. Many peer-reviewed papers have been refuted. Many important discoveries were initially rejected by reviewers.
- **Funding as invisible bias.** Whoever pays for the research influences what is investigated, what is published, and how it is interpreted. This does not automatically make funded research suspect, but it requires examining the possible bias.

### 4.3 Narrative Fallacies

- **History is written by the victors.** This is not a cliché: it is a documented fact. Historical records reflect the perspective of those who had the power to write and preserve them. Reading history also requires reading what was not written, what was destroyed, and what was encoded to survive censorship.
- **Legal architecture as a concealment system.** Many of power's most significant operations are not illegal: they are a-legal. They occur in legal spaces designed not to answer to ordinary law (foundations with non-democratic control, shell companies in tax havens, 99-year leases that are covert sales). Research must look at the legal form, not only the visible content.
- **Media coverage as a commodity.** The media are not neutral. Their ownership is concentrated. Their editorial line responds to private, family, or institutional interests. Favorable or unfavorable coverage is sometimes the product of explicit agreements. Treating a news item as fact without examining who publishes it and why is negligence.

### 4.4 Fallacies of Omission

- **The truth that is not spoken.** The most effective form of lying is not saying something false. It is saying nothing. Sykes-Picot was secret. The Nakba is not taught. The Allies knew about Auschwitz in 1944 and did not act. When a source systematically omits certain facts, names, or events, the omission is the data point.
- **Consensus as absence of dissent.** That all media say the same thing does not mean it is true. It may mean they share an owner, an interest, or a source. Or it may mean the facts are overwhelming and the consensus is genuine. The question is not "is there consensus?" but "why is there consensus?"
- **Controlled opposition.** A system can have two parties, three TV networks, and five newspapers and still narrow the spectrum of what can be said without seeming crazy. The question is not how many voices there are, but which voices are missing.

### 4.5 Fallacies of Institutional Capture

- **The revolving door.** When the same people circulate between government, industry, media, and foundations, a conspiracy is not needed. It is enough that everyone knows each other. Armin Laschet connects the Krupp Foundation, NRW media, and the CDU. Frank Werneke, union leader, controls votes in ZDF. Oliver Burkhard went from the union foundation to CEO of the defense industry. The network does not need secret meetings.
- **The five-step model.** Institutional capture follows a documented pattern: (1) financial vulnerability, (2) opaque structure, (3) legal façade, (4) judicial ratification, (5) institutional defenselessness. It was seen in the Jerusalem Patriarchate. It applies to any vulnerable institution.
- **The foundation as shielded entity.** The German Stiftung, the Kuratorium Krupp (the controlling body of the Alfried Krupp Foundation, majority owner of ThyssenKrupp), the Fazit-Stiftung (owner of the Frankfurter Allgemeine publishing group), and the Roman Black Nobility (the aristocratic families that rejected the Kingdom of Italy in 1870 and maintained loyalty to the Pope) — all share the same architecture: an entity that owns, controls, and influences without answering to shareholders, without being listed on the stock exchange, with no expiration date, shielded by laws written for it.

## 5. On the Influence of Money and Power

### 5.1 In Science

Money is not an external agent that "corrupts" a science that would otherwise be pure. Money is the condition of possibility for modern science. There is no laboratory without funding. No experiment without equipment. No researcher without a salary.

This implies:

- **What is investigated depends on who funds.** For 24 years, the U.S. Department of Defense funded unrestricted basic research at universities. When the Mansfield Amendment (1969) restricted that funding to projects with direct military application, entire fields collapsed — not because they ceased to be scientifically interesting, but because they ceased to be financially viable.
- **What is published depends on who controls publication.** Results that contradict commercial, military, or political interests face barriers ranging from editorial rejection to classification.
- **Who investigates depends on who decides what counts as legitimate research.** Post-Mansfield NSF rules — peer review, solid preliminary data, punishment for paradigm deviation — are not neutral: they favor incremental science and punish paradigm disruption.
- **The market detects fraud when it benefits.** Amgen and Bayer only investigated the replication of papers because they were losing money. If the false papers had been profitable, they would never have published it.

### 5.2 In Geopolitics and Institutions

Money does not only fund science. It funds media, parties, churches, foundations, and wars. The mechanisms by which money and power construct "truth" follow patterns that this project has documented in specific investigations (see posts in contexto-complicado, alemania, italia, iglesia):

1. **Legal architecture.** Creating entities that do not answer to ordinary law: shielded foundations (German Stiftungen), dual sovereignty (Black Nobility), laws written for a single actor (Lex Krupp).
2. **Media control.** Concentrating ownership or trading regulatory favors for coverage (Case 4000 Netanyahu-Walla, five groups control the German press).
3. **Official history.** Omitting what contradicts the foundational narrative (secret Sykes-Picot, Nakba not taught).
4. **Directed funding.** Only what someone pays for is investigated (Mansfield Amendment, Amgen/Bayer).
5. **Encoded writing.** The truth survives hidden where the censor cannot read ("Edom = Rome" in medieval rabbinic texts).
6. **Intelligence as infrastructure.** Asymmetric information as advantage and currency (Jesuits 16th c., Pegasus, Russian Orthodox Church).
7. **Institutional capture.** Placing the right person in the right position (5-step model of the Jerusalem Patriarchate, German revolving door).
8. **Religion as geopolitical actor.** An institution that survives empires accumulates legitimacy that no State can buy (Ecumenical Patriarchate, IOR).
9. **Controlled opposition.** Creating the appearance of debate while narrowing the spectrum of what can be said (German Rundfunkrat, "two-state solution" as smokescreen).
10. **Selective impunity.** Applying the law depending on whom — or changing the law if the accused has the power (Netanyahu and judicial reform, Colombia).
11. **Shape-shifting power.** From political to financial, from titles to assets, from families to foundations (Black Nobility, German Stiftungen).

These patterns are not a theory. They are **documented observations in specific cases** whose sources are cited in the corresponding project posts. It is not assumed that every case meets them. They are verified case by case. **The list is descriptive, not prescriptive: not every investigation must fit one of the eleven, and the absence of a pattern is as informative as its presence.**

**The rule for this project:** Every investigation must ask who funded the study, who benefits from its conclusions, who owns the outlet that publishes it, what interest the outlet has in publishing it, under what legal architecture the source operates, and what interests might be being protected by silence or disqualification.

## 6. On the "Pseudoscience" Label

The term "pseudoscience" has a legitimate use: it designates claims that are presented as scientific without meeting the methodological standards of science (falsifiability, replicability, peer review, transparency of method).

But the term also has an illegitimate use: it functions as a tool of automatic disqualification that avoids substantive examination. Saying "that's pseudoscience" saves the work of explaining why it is false.

**Criterion for this project:**

1. **If a claim is false, it must be shown why it is false.** Showing that an experiment was not replicated, that a theory is not falsifiable, or that a hypothesis rests on anomalous evidence is an argument. Calling it "pseudoscience" is not.
2. **The pseudoscience label cannot be applied to questions, only to methods.** Asking whether gravity can be modified by superconductors is a legitimate scientific question. Claiming that it can, without evidence, is pseudoscientific. The distinction is in the method and the evidence, not in the topic.
3. **The pseudoscience label cannot be applied to investigations that do not claim to be science.** A historical analysis, an institutional genealogy, or a connection between events does not aspire to the standard of experimental replicability. Judging them by the criteria of natural science is a category error.
4. **There are questions that institutional science does not investigate because it cannot (funding, classification) or does not want to (interests, paradigms).** That science does not investigate something does not make it pseudoscience. It may simply be unexplored territory.

## 7. On Replicability as an Argument

The replication crisis changed what it means to say "this is not replicable." Before 2015, that phrase was a verdict. After 2015, it is a description of the normal state of published science.

### What the Data Show

- 36% of psychology studies in high-ranking journals could be replicated (Open Science Collaboration, 2015).
- Only 11% of preclinical cancer studies considered "landmark" could be confirmed (Begley & Ellis, Nature, 2012).
- 70% of researchers surveyed by Nature could not reproduce at least one experiment by another scientist (Baker, 2016).
- 50% could not reproduce their own experiments.
- Ego depletion: 23 laboratories and 36 laboratories attempted to replicate it. Zero effect (2016, 2021).
- Power posing: 42 participants in the original study. Zero replication. The lead author declared the evidence against it is "undeniable" (Carney, 2016).
- Diederik Stapel: 58 retracted papers, 15 years publishing fabricated data, peer review passed on every occasion.

### What This Implies

1. **"Not replicable" is not an argument against a claim; it is a description of the state of science.** If the majority of what is published in Nature, Science, and Psychological Science is not replicable, then non-replication is the rule, not the exception. Rejecting something because "it has not been replicated" is applying a standard that the most prestigious scientific institutions also do not meet.

2. **The demand for replication is asymmetric.** A heterodox hypothesis is demanded robust replication by multiple independent groups. A paper published in a high-impact journal is demanded nothing: it is assumed true until someone proves otherwise. That asymmetry is not scientific: it is institutional. It protects the center and punishes the periphery.

3. **Non-replication can mean several things, not just one.** As in the antigravity case: (A) the effect does not exist, (B) the effect is sensitive to conditions that were not reproduced, (C) the effect was classified. Saying "it did not replicate" without exploring (B) and (C) is a logical error, not a scientific conclusion.

4. **The non-replicable paper that passed peer review is identical in form to the non-replicable paper that did not.** Stapel published in Science for 15 years with fabricated data. If the peer review filter does not detect massive fraud, what authority does it have to disqualify what lies outside the circuit?

5. **Replicability is an ideal, not an operational reality of science.** As an ideal, it is correct to aspire to it. As a rhetorical weapon to disqualify uncomfortable investigations, it is hypocrisy. Institutional science does not replicate — it is not convenient, it is not funded, it is not published. Demanding replication from the other while not replicating oneself is a double standard.

**The rule:** The non-replication of a result must be evaluated in the context of what we know about the general replication rates in that field. If 64% of social psychology does not replicate, a heterodox study not having been replicated does not distinguish it from the majority of orthodox studies. The relevant question is not "was it replicated?" but "is there positive evidence that it is false?"

## 8. On the Authority of the Scientist

The word "scientist" describes a profession, not a state of epistemic grace. A scientist can:

- Have a doctorate and be wrong.
- Publish in Nature and commit fraud.
- Be widely respected and defend a false paradigm.
- Lack credentials and be right.
- Have no peer-reviewed publications and have made a valuable contribution to mapping a field.

**Examples documented in this project:**

- **Amy Eskridge:** Without substantive peer-reviewed bibliography, but her public cataloging of the field of antigravity and her identification of the "panning" pattern constitute a real contribution to the state of the question.
- **John Keely:** Never documented his experiments with enough detail to be replicable. His machines disappeared. But saying his work does not deserve examination because it does not meet modern standards is a prejudice, not an argument.
- **Dan Davidson (Shape Power):** Using the human eye as a detection instrument is one of the reasons his work is not accepted in academia. It is also the reason he was able to document phenomena that conventional instrumentation does not detect.

**Historical cases of scientists who were right and were crushed:**

- **Ignaz Semmelweis (1847):** Demonstrated that handwashing reduced maternal mortality from 18% to 1.2%. The medical community rejected him because he could not explain why it worked. He died in a mental asylum in 1865. Pasteur confirmed germ theory years later.
- **Barry Marshall (1984):** Proposed that gastric ulcers were caused by a bacterium, not stress. He was laughed at. He drank a broth with *H. pylori*, developed severe gastritis, cured himself with antibiotics. Nobel in 2005.
- **Barbara McClintock (1951):** Discovered transposons. No one understood. She stopped publishing. Nobel in 1983.
- **Dan Shechtman (1982):** Discovered quasicrystals. He was expelled from the laboratory. Linus Pauling, two-time Nobel laureate, declared: "There are no quasicrystals, only quasiscientists." Nobel in 2011.

The pattern is real — but it comes with a necessary warning: **for every Semmelweis who was right there are hundreds of outsiders who were wrong.** That some rejected by the establishment turned out to be geniuses does not imply that every rejected person is a genius. The lesson is not "believe the outsider." The lesson is **examine the evidence, not the résumé.** The establishment gets it right more often than it gets it wrong. But when it gets it wrong, it gets it wrong for decades and with catastrophic consequences. And that is the problem.

**The rule:** Credentials are useful information for calibrating expectations. They are not an argument for or against a claim. The argument is in the evidence, the method, and the logical coherence. And one must remember that **the history of science is full of people without credentials who were right and of Nobel laureates who were wrong.**

## 9. Working Method

### 9.1 What We Know, What Is in Dispute, What Appears Ruled Out

Every investigation must conclude with an explicit assessment in these three categories. It is the format used in the technical deep dive on the field of antigravity and should be the standard for investigations of a certain complexity:

1. **What we know with high confidence** — claims supported by solid and multiple evidence.
2. **What is in dispute** — claims with mixed evidence, contradictory sources, or inconclusive consensus.
3. **What appears ruled out** — claims that the available evidence robustly contradicts.

### 9.2 When Evidence Is Insufficient: Mutually Exclusive Possibilities

When public data do not allow a conclusion, the mutually exclusive hypotheses are listed and it is explicitly acknowledged that there is no current way to discriminate among them. This honesty is more valuable than a forced conclusion.

Example from the field of antigravity:
- (A) The effect does not exist.
- (B) The effect exists but is extremely sensitive to experimental conditions.
- (C) The effect exists and has been replicated, but in classified programs that do not publish.

Not being able to distinguish between A, B, and C is not a failure: it is a finding. It indicates exactly what information is missing and what would be needed to close the question.

### 9.3 Connections and Patterns

This project seeks patterns — connections between seemingly separate domains that, when documented, reveal underlying structures. But a connection is not a conclusion.

- **Documenting the connection** is research work.
- **Proving the connection** requires additional evidence.
- **Claiming a conspiracy where there is only a connection** is jumping from Layer 1 to Layer 3 without passing through Layer 2.

The "panning" pattern identified by Eskridge (Ning Li, Doug Torr, Podkletnov, Modanese) is a correct example: it documents an observable phenomenon without claiming a single cause. The possible causes — coincidence, diffuse institutional pressure, active classification — remain open.

### 9.4 Falsifiability as a Filter

When an investigation includes scientific or technical hypotheses, it must include — whenever possible — an experimental design that allows testing the central claim. The experiment can be a thought experiment if the resources do not exist, but it must be specific: what is measured, with what instrument, what result would confirm the hypothesis, and what result would refute it.

## 10. On the Tone and Position of the Researcher

### 10.1 The Researcher Is Not Neutral, but Is Honest

No one is neutral. Whoever writes has interests, biases, limitations, and a point of view. Honesty does not consist in having no position: it consists in declaring the position and not letting it contaminate the evidence.

### 10.2 The Researcher Is Not a Judge

This project does not issue verdicts. It presents evidence, traces connections, evaluates certainties, and leaves open the questions it cannot close. The reader decides.

### 10.3 The Researcher Is Not an Activist

There is a difference between investigating power and fighting it. This project investigates structures of power — scientific, political, religious, financial — but does not militate. Militancy clouds judgment. Investigation clarifies it.

### 10.4 Research Does Not Expire, but Is Updated

A published post is not an eternal truth. It is a snapshot of the state of the question on a given date. If new evidence emerges that contradicts, nuances, or expands what was said, an update is written. The past is not rewritten.

Corrections and updates are recorded visibly at the start of the post ("Updates" block with date and nature of the change). A reader who saw the previous version must be able to know what changed and why.

### 10.5 Anonymous Sources and Sources with Protection

In geopolitical and institutional investigations, some sources will only speak under condition of anonymity — due to occupational, legal, or physical risk. The criteria are:

1. **Anonymity is explicitly declared.** The reader must know that a claim comes from an unidentifiable source.
2. **Anonymity reduces the weight of the claim.** An anonymous source automatically drops one level on the certainty scale (§3.3). For example, a claim that with a public source would be "High" becomes "Medium" if the source is anonymous.
3. **Information from an anonymous source must be corroborated by public sources whenever possible.** If it cannot be corroborated, it is presented as an unverified allegation (Layer 3).
4. **The researcher protects the source, but does not obey it.** If the source asks that relevant information be omitted from the reader, the researcher decides — and explains the decision.
5. **If an anonymous source lies.** If it is discovered that an anonymous source provided false information, a correction is published identifying the refuted claim. The identity of the source is not revealed, but it is no longer used.

### 10.6 Conflict of Interest of the Project

This project demands transparency about funding and interests from its sources (§5, §11). It must apply the same standard to itself:

- **Funding:** The project receives no external funding. It is maintained by its author with their own resources.
- **Affiliation:** The author is not affiliated with any academic institution, political party, media outlet, church, or organization that could benefit from the conclusions of the investigations.
- **Interests:** The author has the convictions that their investigations reveal. The declaration of position does not replace the verification of evidence (§10.1).

### 10.7 How to Read a Source (Scientific or Geopolitical) Without Being Deceived

Based on what we know about the replication crisis and geopolitical manipulation mechanisms:

**For scientific papers and studies:**
1. Small sample (<100 per group) → suspicious.
2. Effect too large (d > 0.8 in social sciences) → almost certainly false.
3. Result too perfect (no contradictions or nuances) → distrust. "There are no perfect stories in biology."
4. Lack of pre-registration → assume p-hacking until proven otherwise.
5. p-value just below 0.05 (p = 0.049) → more suspicious than p = 0.001.
6. Many variables, few participants → classic data dredging.
7. Undeclared conflict of interest → the result is less credible by default.
8. The paper is "the first to demonstrate" → almost never true.

**Soft heuristic (does not disqualify, only calibrates):**
9. Strong personal brand of the author (TED talks, popular science book, high media presence). It does not invalidate the research, but it adjusts expectations: when the author's career depends on the finding being true, the incentive to defend it is greater than the incentive to verify it. Unlike criteria 1-8, this is a contextual judgment, not an objective indicator.

**For geopolitical and media sources:**
1. Who owns the outlet? Is it part of a larger group? What other media does it control?
2. Who funds the source? Is there a conflict of interest?
3. Under what legal architecture does the entity operate? Ordinary law or lex specialis?
4. What is not being said? What names, events, or interpretations are systematically omitted?
5. Who else says the same thing? Are they independent sources or do they share an owner/interest?
6. What happened to whoever said the opposite? Were they fired, silenced, classified, ridiculed?
7. Is the operation legal but the legal form conceals the substance? (99-year lease = sale; "absentee" = present)
8. Is there a revolving door between the source and power? Does the same person appear in government, industry, and media?

**For institutional claims:**
1. Did the institution survive the empires that attacked it? (Orthodox Church, Roman Black Nobility)
2. Did it change form rather than disappear? (from family to foundation, from estate to network)
3. Does legal truth contradict material truth? (legal but unjust)
4. Is the consensus genuine or is it controlled opposition? (many voices, same spectrum)

**For any source:**
- Check if someone has already tried to verify the claim and what they found.
- Verify if the source has retractions or corrections (Retraction Watch, Google Scholar).
- Ask: if this were false, how would I know? If there is no way to know, the claim is unverifiable, not true.

### 10.8 What Would Refute This Framework?

Every research framework must be able to answer: what evidence would show that it is wrong?

For this framework, the conditions of refutation are:

1. **If the documented patterns turned out to be isolated cases with no structural connection** — that is, if the investigations in Germany, Italy, Israel, Colombia, and the Vatican did not reveal recurring mechanisms but disconnected anecdotes.
2. **If institutional reforms (pre-registration, open science, registered reports) resolved the replication crisis** — demonstrating that the problem was technical, not structural. Concrete threshold: if replication rates in social psychology and preclinical biomedicine exceed 70% sustained over at least five years, the thesis that the system selects against truth would lose force.
3. **If the geopolitical control mechanisms described here were not found in other regions or periods** — suggesting they are idiosyncratic, not systemic.
4. **If a concrete prediction derived from this framework, applied to a future case, did not come true.** For example: if it is predicted that an institution with financial vulnerability + opaque structure + leadership without counterbalances will be captured within a given timeframe, and it does not happen. The framework cannot limit itself to explaining the past; it must be testable going forward.

### 10.9 Where the Safeguards Live

The warnings, self-caveats, and bias declarations (§1.4, §2.1, §10.6, §10.10) live in this document. Individual posts do not repeat each of these notices: they would be unreadable due to excessive hedging. The reader who wants the full dose of methodological self-critique finds it here. Posts cite §X when a specific safeguard applies to a concrete claim, but do not carry the entire apparatus.

### 10.10 On the Structural Bias of the Project

This project investigates hidden power, institutional capture, and the manipulation of truth. By definition, its findings point in one direction: showing how power operates. This generates an unavoidable bias:

- **Cases that confirm the pattern are published. Cases where nothing is found are not.** This is not bad faith: it is that "I investigated X and found no evidence of capture" rarely deserves a post. But the cumulative effect is an overrepresentation of confirmed patterns versus absent patterns.
- **The project has no editorial counterbalance.** It is a single person researching, writing, and publishing. There is no editor who challenges conclusions, no reviewer who demands more evidence, no colleague who points out confirmation bias.
- **The examples come from a limited set of regions and traditions.** The project has investigated Germany, Italy, Israel/Palestine, Colombia, and the Vatican because that is where it found patterns. It has not investigated China, India, Brazil, Nigeria, or Japan with the same depth. The absence of these cases does not mean the patterns do not exist there — but neither does it mean they do.

**What this implies for the reader:**
1. The documented patterns are real in the cases where they were documented. They are not automatically extrapolated to other contexts.
2. The consistent direction of the examples is not evidence that "all power is like this." It is evidence that this project looks for a specific type of phenomenon and finds it where it looks.
3. The question "where did you not find this?" is legitimate and must be answerable.

**What this implies for the researcher:**
1. Publish at least one investigation per year with a negative or inconclusive result, where a pattern was sought and not found. These posts are explicitly tagged (`resultado-negativo` / `inconcluso`) and listed in a public annual index so that compliance with this commitment is auditable.
2. Apply the framework to a case where the project's hypotheses predict an outcome that is not confirmed — for example, an institution with all the vulnerability markers that nevertheless was not captured.
3. Invite critical readers to point out biases, omissions, or errors — and correct them publicly. This is not editorial openness: the researcher remains ultimately responsible for the content. Feedback informs, it does not decide. This does not contradict §10.3 (non-militancy): accepting correction is epistemic honesty, not opening the project to an external agenda.

## 11. Operative Summary

**Before publishing an investigation, verify:**

**Sources and layers:**
- [ ] Sources are cited and traceable.
- [ ] The three layers (verifiable fact, source claim, third-party interpretation) are clearly separated.
- [ ] If the source is an institution of power, its claim is treated as Layer 2 (what power says about itself), not as Layer 1.
- [ ] Claims have an explicit indication of the level of certainty.

**Connections and patterns:**
- [ ] Connections are presented as connections, not as conclusions.
- [ ] If a pattern of institutional capture is documented, verify whether the five-step model or another documented mechanism applies.

**Fallacies to avoid:**
- [ ] A label ("pseudoscience," "conspiracy," "denialism") is not used as a substitute for an argument.
- [ ] The authority of a person or institution is not invoked as a substitute for evidence.
- [ ] Non-replication is not used as a verdict of falsehood, given that the majority of published science is also not replicable.
- [ ] Legal truth is not confused with material truth (a legal operation can be deeply unjust).
- [ ] It is not assumed that media consensus reflects reality (it may reflect concentration of ownership).

**Power and money:**
- [ ] It is mentioned who funded the cited studies, if that information is available.
- [ ] It is identified who owns the outlet that publishes the source, if relevant.
- [ ] It is asked under what legal architecture the analyzed entity operates (ordinary law or lex specialis?).

**Honesty:**
- [ ] If the evidence is insufficient to conclude, it is said explicitly.
- [ ] If there are mutually exclusive hypotheses, they are listed without forcing a choice.
- [ ] If a claim is rejected due to lack of replication, the replication rate in that field is indicated for context.
- [ ] If a relevant fact is systematically omitted by official sources, the omission is pointed out as a data point.
- [ ] If an anonymous source is used, it is explicitly declared and it is indicated that its epistemic weight is lower.
- [ ] It is declared whether the project or its author has any relevant conflict of interest for the investigation.
- [ ] It can be answered: what evidence would make me change my mind about this?
- [ ] The tone is one of investigation, not of militancy or verdict.
