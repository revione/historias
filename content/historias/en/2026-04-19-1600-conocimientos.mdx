# Diet: Kashrut, History, and Practice

Kashrut: Torah and the Talmud, the laws that regulate what is eaten and how it is prepared, the history of the shohet and the chalaf, the differences between communities, kashrut at home and in modern industry, and the spiritual dimension that turns eating into a conscious and meaningful practice.

---

## 1. Foundations

Kashrut defines which foods may be consumed and how they must be prepared.
Kashrut is the result of centuries of interpretation, debate, and refinement.

**Tamei** (טמא): ritual/legal category. Not fit for consumption according to Torah law.

### Mammals

Only those that have split hooves and chew the cud.

**Torah — Leviticus 11 (Vayikra):**

> "Every animal that has a divided hoof, split in two, and that chews the cud, that you may eat."

Examples from the same chapter:

- Camel → chews the cud but does not have a completely split hoof → forbidden
- Rabbit / hare → "chew the cud" according to the text, but do not have split hooves → forbidden
- Pig → has a split hoof but does not chew the cud → forbidden. Symbolically, it "shows" the external sign of what is permitted but does not fulfill the inner condition. It is associated with avoiding what appears correct without being so.
- Cow, sheep, goat → fulfill both → permitted

**Torah — Deuteronomy 14 (Devarim):**

> "These are the animals that you may eat: the ox, the sheep, and the goat..."

It reaffirms the rule: split hoof + chewing the cud.

Animals that meet no criterion: horse, dog, cat, lion, bear, elephant, monkey, rodents.

This is not about whether the animal is "clean" or "dirty" in modern terms: it is a precise classification system based on the criteria of the Torah.

---

### Birds

Those that have a mesorah — a continuous tradition from generation to generation recognizing them as kosher — such as chicken, duck, goose, or pigeon. The Torah does not establish physical criteria for birds: it directly lists the forbidden ones.

**Torah — Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14** name the forbidden birds:

Eagle, vulture, falcon, raven, carrion birds in general, owl, hawk, pelican, cormorant, stork, heron, bat (included in the biblical category of birds).

Common pattern:

1. Predatory: they hunt other animals and have claws for tearing
2. Scavenging: they eat dead animals
3. Aggressive behavior: they are not domesticable birds

Birds without a clear mesorah are not considered kosher even if they seem harmless.

---

### Fish

Only those that have fins and scales.

**Torah — Leviticus 11:9–12 (Vayikra):**

> "These you may eat of all that are in the waters: whatever has fins and scales in the waters, in the seas and in the rivers, these you may eat. But all that have no fins and scales in the seas and in the rivers... shall be an abomination to you."

**Torah — Deuteronomy 14:9–10 (Devarim):**

> "These you may eat of all that are in the waters: all that have fins and scales you may eat. But whatever does not have fins and scales you shall not eat; it is impure for you."

**Talmud — Chullin 66b:** every fish that has scales necessarily has fins. In halachic practice, finding visible scales is sufficient; it is not necessary to verify the fins separately.

Kosher scales: visible to the naked eye and removable without damaging the skin of the fish.

- Salmon, tuna, sardine → permitted
- Shark, eel, catfish → not permitted
- Shellfish and crustaceans — shrimp, lobster, crab, mussels, octopus — have neither fins nor scales: they meet neither condition

---

### Insects

Forbidden in the great majority of cases. That is why leafy vegetables — such as lettuce — are checked carefully to avoid eating them accidentally.

The Torah permits certain jumping insects, interpreted as some species of locusts. The Yemenite community preserves that tradition of identification. Most communities lost it; in modern practice they are universally avoided.

---

### Plant products, eggs, and processed foods

**Plant products**: fruits, vegetables, legumes, grains, and nuts — all permitted.

**Eggs**: from kosher birds.

**Processed products**: require kosher certification.

## 2. The separation between meat and dairy

The same phrase appears three times in the Torah:

**Exodus 23:19:** "You shall not boil a kid in its mother's milk."
**Exodus 34:26:** "You shall not boil a kid in its mother's milk."
**Deuteronomy 14:21:** "You shall not boil a kid in its mother's milk."

In rabbinic interpretation, the triple repetition indicates that this is not a minor detail.

**Talmud — Chullin 113a–115b:** the repetition teaches three levels of prohibition:

1. Do not cook meat with milk
2. Do not eat that mixture
3. Do not derive benefit from it (selling it or using it)

The law expands:

- "kid" → any meat from a kosher mammal
- "its mother's milk" → any kosher animal milk

It is no longer a specific case: it becomes a general legal category.

The Talmud develops the practical implications:

- What happens if meat and milk were not cooked together but become mixed?
- What happens if a utensil absorbed flavor?
- How long does that flavor remain?
- What about residue in the mouth?

From there emerge the concepts of **ta'am** (absorbed flavor), heat transfer, and residue in the teeth.

**Separation of utensils**: this is not in the Torah. It arises because the Talmud considers that utensils absorb flavor and that flavor can be reactivated during cooking. The solution: complete separation of meat and dairy.

**Waiting between meat and milk — Talmud, Chullin 105a:** meat leaves a persistent flavor and may remain between the teeth. Different communities set different times: 6h, 3h, 1h.

**Shulchan Aruch (sixteenth century):** codifies this whole system. What was Talmudic debate becomes stable practical law:

- Separation of utensils
- Waiting times
- Detailed rules of mixture

Chain of the system:
Torah → oral tradition → Talmud → rabbinic decrees → legal codes

**Ta'am and heat transfer** are the two central halachic ideas that explain domestic kashrut:

- **Ta'am**: utensils absorb and transmit flavor
- **Heat transfer**: heat causes that flavor to pass from one food to another

**The oven** creates additional complexity: hot air + vapor + grease.

Halachic problems:

- Vapor carries flavor
- Oven walls absorb residue
- Grease can splash

Solutions:

- Exclusive oven for meat or dairy
- Shared use: clean completely, wait, use closed or covered containers
- Modern ovens: some authorities allow more flexibility if there is no direct contact and foods are covered

## 3. The prohibition of blood and salting

**Torah — Leviticus 17:10–12 (Vayikra):**

> "Any man... who eats any blood, I will set my face against that person... For the life of the flesh is in the blood... no person shall eat blood."

Blood = life (nefesh). That is why it is forbidden.

**Torah — Deuteronomy 12:23–24 (Devarim):**

> "Only be sure that you do not eat the blood, for the blood is the life; you shall not eat the life with the flesh. You shall pour it out like water upon the earth."

It is not enough to avoid it: it must be actively extracted.

**Torah — Leviticus 7:26–27:**

> "You shall not eat any blood... whoever eats blood shall be cut off from his people."

**Talmud — tractate Chullin:** shechita allows the blood to flow quickly, but it is not sufficient by itself. Internal blood remains in the tissues and must be extracted.

### The removal process: melichah

1. **Initial washing**: removes surface blood
2. **Salting**: coarse salt covers the meat; it draws out the blood by osmosis for about one hour
3. **Final rinsing**: removes the salt and the extracted blood

### Special cases

**Liver**: contains so much blood that salting is not enough. It requires direct roasting over fire.

**Meat not treated in time**: if too much time passes without salting, the blood can no longer be properly extracted. It is permitted only through roasting.

The central idea: blood is not just another ingredient. It is the symbol of life. It may not be consumed, it must be actively separated, and even small amounts are halachically problematic.

## 4. Passover and chametz

Passover commemorates the Exodus from Egypt. It lasts 7 days in Israel, 8 in the diaspora. The central moment is the night of the Seder, the ritual dinner where the Exodus is recounted through the Haggadah.

**Chametz** (חמץ): any food from the five grains — wheat, barley, rye, oats, spelt — that has fermented after contact with water.

**Torah — Exodus 12:15:**

> "Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread; on the first day you shall remove leaven from your houses..."

**Torah — Exodus 12:19:**

> "For seven days no leaven shall be found in your houses..."

**Torah — Exodus 13:7:**

> "Nothing leavened shall be seen with you, nor shall leaven be seen with you in all your territory."

**Why it is forbidden:**

1. Historical reason: the Israelites left Egypt in such haste that the dough had no time to ferment. Avoiding chametz is reliving that moment.
2. Symbolic reading (later): leavened bread → expansion, pride, ego. Matzah → simplicity, humility.

**What this means in practice:**

- Chametz may not be eaten: bread, pasta, beer, fermented products from those grains
- It is not enough not to eat it: it may not be owned, it may not be in the home
- Process before the festival:
  - Deep cleaning of the home
  - **Bedikat chametz**: ritual search
  - **Biur chametz**: symbolic burning
- It is replaced by **matzah**: flour + water, without fermentation, baked quickly

## 5. From the Torah to the Talmud to the Shulchan Aruch

The **Talmudic period** (1st–6th centuries CE) builds the technical details that the Torah does not develop:

- The five errors that invalidate shechita
- The exact conditions of the knife
- The criteria for internal organ inspection
- Waiting times between meat and dairy
- The limits of what constitutes a forbidden mixture

The **Shulchan Aruch** (sixteenth century, Rabbi Yosef Karo) synthesizes all that debate into stable practical norms. It remains the standard reference to this day.

Kashrut is the result of centuries of interpretation, debate, and refinement. Each generation applied ancient principles to new situations.

### The five errors that invalidate shechita

Fixed by the Talmud. None appears explicitly in the Torah:

1. **Shehiyah**: pausing during the cut
2. **Derasah**: pressing instead of cutting smoothly
3. **Chaladah**: the knife hidden or not visible during the cut
4. **Hagramah**: cutting in the wrong place on the neck
5. **Ikkur**: pulling or tearing instead of making a clean cut

## 6. The chalaf: the shohet's knife

The ritual knife of the shohet is called **chalaf**. It must be long, perfectly straight, extremely sharp, and without any nick, not even a microscopic one. An imperfection on the edge can cause an involuntary pause or extra pressure — two of the five errors that invalidate shechita.

Since the Talmud, it has been established that the chalaf must be checked by passing the fingernail and finger along the edge before each use. Even an irregularity invisible to the eye makes it unusable until corrected.

In the Middle Ages, shohetim carried their own knives and were personally responsible for their condition. Today modern sharpening methods are used, but the prior inspection remains manual: fingernail and fingertip in both directions along the entire edge. If any imperfection is detected, the knife may not be used.

This practice combines technique, physical sensitivity, and ethical responsibility.

## 7. History of the shohet

The shohet performs kosher ritual slaughter. It is not only a technical function: it is a religious function of great responsibility.

### Origin in the Torah

**Deuteronomy** commands that animals be slaughtered "as I have commanded you." The Torah does not describe the details; the oral tradition — recorded in the Talmud — develops them. In the earliest times, any person with the knowledge could perform the slaughter; there was no distinct professional figure.

### The Temple period

The kohanim performed ritual sacrifices inside the Temple. For everyday consumption outside the Temple, shechita was practiced, and there the need for specialization began.

### After the destruction of the Temple (70 CE)

Ritual sacrifices inside the Temple ceased. Shechita for meat consumption remained obligatory. The role of the shohet then gained greater importance and specialization.

### Formalization in the Middle Ages

The shohet was already a fully established figure. He had to study extensively, be examined by rabbinic authorities, and demonstrate theoretical knowledge and practical skill. In many communities he also served as a teacher or minor religious leader.

---

## 8. The kabbalah: certification of the shohet

**Kabbalah** here — unrelated to the mystical current — designates the formal authorization to practice as a shohet.

The process:

- **Intensive study**: laws of shechita, Talmud, Shulchan Aruch, practical cases
- **Practical training**: use of the chalaf, practice of the cut under the supervision of an experienced shohet
- **Examination**: theoretical knowledge and practical ability before an authorized rabbi
- **Character evaluation**: responsibility, honesty, emotional balance

The kabbalah is a written authorization specifying under which conditions or communities one may practice. It is not permanent: it can be revoked if standards are not maintained.

---

## 9. A day in the life of a modern shohet

**Before beginning:**

- He checks the chalaf and sharpens it if necessary
- He inspects it with fingernail and finger several times
- In some cases, he recites a blessing

**During the work:**

- He verifies that the animal is of a kosher species and healthy
- He performs the shechita with full concentration: although the rhythm may be repetitive, each animal requires individual attention
- Afterward: the **bedikah** — internal inspection of organs, especially the lungs. If problems are found, the animal may be declared treifah

The emotional burden is real. The shohet is expected to maintain constant sensitivity and not become indifferent. That is why character and emotional balance are evaluated from the beginning.

## 10. Kosher slaughterhouses today

They combine religious norms with the sanitary regulations of the country. Three key figures:

- **Shohet**: performs the shechita
- **Bodek**: inspects the internal organs
- **Mashgiach**: supervisor who ensures that the entire process is kosher

### Stages of the process

1. Selection of healthy kosher animals of a permitted species
2. Shechita by a certified shohet. In many traditions there is no prior stunning, although this varies according to the laws of each country
3. **Bedikah** — internal inspection of organs. The bodek mainly examines the lungs, but also the liver, heart, and other organs. Each has specific criteria: lesions, adhesions, perforations, or anomalies can declare the meat treifah. The liver deserves special attention because, in addition to being checked for lesions, its high blood content requires it to be roasted directly over fire before it can be eaten, regardless of the general salting process
4. Processing: washing, salting, and rinsing to remove the blood
5. Continuous rabbinic supervision

### Differences from a conventional slaughterhouse

Every cut must be valid according to religious law. There are additional inspections that do not exist in other systems. Part of the meat may be discarded even if it is hygienically fit.

### Modern challenges

- Legal debates about animal welfare and methods of slaughter
- Maintaining artisanal standards in large-scale industrial production
- Shortage of professionals: training takes years

## 11. Glatt kosher

**Glatt** in Yiddish: "smooth." It refers to the state of the lungs of the slaughtered animal.

During bedikah, the bodek inspects the lungs looking for adhesions. According to standard law, certain adhesions may be examined and, if removed without leaving damage, the meat remains kosher. The **glatt** standard applies a stricter criterion in lung inspection: it reduces or eliminates the possibility of intermediate evaluations. In common usage it is associated with lungs without problematic adhesions, although the halachic debate over what kinds of adhesions are or are not admissible is more complex.

Historically it was the Sephardic norm. Over time many Ashkenazi communities adopted it. Today most certified slaughterhouses in Europe and America produce exclusively glatt meat.

The difference between kosher and glatt kosher is not in the shechita, but in the subsequent inspection. The term is also used colloquially — sometimes incorrectly — to refer to any product of high kosher strictness.

## 12. Differences between communities: kashrut is not monolithic

Kashrut is not a single, uniform system. Communities have developed their own interpretations and customs.

**Ashkenazi**: Central and Eastern European origin
**Sephardic**: Iberian, Mediterranean, and Middle Eastern origin

Same legal foundations in the Torah and Talmud, differences in practical application:

**Waiting time between meat and dairy**: six hours in many communities of both streams. There are traditions of three hours — communities of Dutch or German origin — or one hour in particular contexts. These are exceptions, not the norm in either stream.

**Kitniyot on Passover**: Ashkenazim prohibit legumes, rice, corn, and lentils. Sephardim permit them.

**Lung inspection**: Sephardim historically applied the glatt criterion more generally. Industrial standardization tends to blur that difference.

**Traditions about birds**: there are differences over which species have sufficient mesorah. Turkey was accepted more gradually in some traditions.

**Locusts**: the Yemenite community preserves the tradition of identifying which are kosher. Most communities lost it.

### Other communities

Ethiopian Jews — Beta Israel — Jews of India — Bene Israel — and communities of Africa and Asia had periods of isolation and developed their own practices, some later reevaluated in light of the predominant halachic frameworks in other regions.

Kashrut is a living system. It is not a law frozen in time: it has been interpreted, adapted, and debated for millennia.

## 13. Kashrut beyond meat

### Kosher wine

Rabbinic law restricted the consumption and handling of certain wines produced outside an observant framework, partly because of the historical association of wine with idolatrous contexts where it was used as a ritual offering. Its production and handling must take place under specific halachic supervision.

**Yayin mevushal** — cooked or pasteurized wine — once subjected to that process, may be handled in certain contexts without losing its kosher status. That is why many kosher wines intended for restaurants and events carry this additional certification.

### Bread and baked goods

**Pat Yisrael** — bread of Israel — bread baked with Jewish participation in the process. Some require it; others accept commercial bread with kosher certification.

During Passover, any food with leavening or contact with it is forbidden: bread, beer, pasta, fermented products from those grains. It is replaced by **matzah**.

### Cheese and rennet

The rennet used may not come from a non-kosher animal or from one not slaughtered correctly. This excludes many traditional European cheeses.

**Gevinas Yisrael**: some cheeses require supervision during the curdling process, especially in stricter communities. Cheeses without obviously problematic ingredients may still be unsuitable without the corresponding certification.

### Processed products and certification

An industrial product may contain dozens of ingredients with non-kosher elements: animal fats in cookies, gelatin in gummies, non-kosher lecithin in chocolates, colorings derived from insects such as cochineal.

Certification agencies: **OU** (United States), **KLBD** (United Kingdom), among others. The best-known symbol is the U inside a circle (OU). Knowing how to read these seals and understand their level of strictness is an essential practical part of kosher life.

## 14. Kosher eating and health

Following kashrut does not guarantee a healthy diet. It is best combined with good nutritional habits.

### Recommendations

- Plenty of fresh fruits and vegetables
- Lean proteins: fish, chicken, legumes
- Healthy fats: olive oil, avocado, nuts
- Whole grains instead of refined grains
- Enough water

### Limit

- Ultra-processed products, even if they carry a kosher seal
- Added sugars and sugary drinks
- Excess saturated fats and fried foods

### Example of a balanced kosher day

- **Breakfast**: dairy or parve — yogurt with fruit and oats, or whole-grain bread with avocado
- **Lunch**: meat or fish with vegetables and a grain
- **Dinner**: dairy or parve if lunch was meat
- **Between meals**: fruit, nuts

## 15. Blessings and the spiritual frame

The act of eating does not begin with the first bite. It begins with a **berachah** — blessing — and concludes with thanksgiving.

Before eating any food, a specific berachah is recited according to the type of food: it is not the same for bread as for fruit, for wine as for other liquids.

Bread activates a complete liturgical chain:

- Ritual handwashing: **netilat yadayim**
- Berachah before bread
- At the end: **Birkat Hamazon** — thanksgiving that gives thanks for food, the land, Jerusalem, and the covenant. It is one of the oldest prayers and one of the few whose recitation is considered a commandment of the Torah, not only of rabbinic origin.

For other foods: **berachah acharonah** — an abbreviated after-blessing, equally obligatory according to halachah.

Eating is not a biological need satisfied neutrally. It is a moment consciously framed in gratitude and recognition: a daily spiritual practice.

## 16. Kashrut at home: the kitchen as a regulated space

The prohibition against mixing meat and dairy does not end on the plate. It extends to everything that comes into contact with food.

**Separation of utensils**: a kosher home has at minimum two complete sets — one for meat, one for dairy — knives, cutting boards, pots, pans, plates, cutlery, sponges. Many homes add a third set for Passover.

**Sinks**: two separate sinks — one for meat, one for dairy — or separate basins inside one sink.

**The oven**: absorbs smells, vapors, and grease. Solutions:

- Exclusive oven for meat or dairy
- Shared use: complete cleaning, waiting time, closed or covered containers
- Modern ovens: some authorities allow more flexibility without direct contact

**Kashering**: when a non-kosher utensil needs to be incorporated, or when there is accidental contamination:

- Metals: immersion in boiling water or direct fire
- Glass: particular rules
- Porous ceramics: in most cases cannot be kashered and must be replaced

**Cross-contamination**: what happens if a meat spoon falls into a dairy pot? Halachah has detailed answers depending on temperature, quantity involved, and elapsed time. In cases of doubt: consult a rabbi.

**Passover dishes**: during the eight days, not only what is eaten changes but also the utensils. Everything that had contact with chametz during the year becomes forbidden. Many families keep a complete set exclusively for Passover. The preparation — exhaustive cleaning, ritual sale of chametz — is one of the most intense moments of the year.

## 17. Kashrut and modernity: the new challenges

Kashrut was born in a world where foods were simple and supply chains were short. Modernity raises questions that rabbinic legislators of centuries past could not have anticipated.

**Gelatin**: comes from animal collagen — bones and skins of pigs or cattle — and appears in gummies, marshmallows, medicine capsules, desserts, yogurts. To be kosher it must derive from a kosher animal slaughtered correctly. Plant-based gelatin — agar-agar, pectin — does not present this problem and is automatically parve.

**Enzymes**: many cheeses are made with enzymes of animal origin whose kosher status must be evaluated case by case. The same applies to emulsifiers, preservatives, and additives whose biological origin is not evident on labels.

**Biotechnology**: is lab-grown meat kosher if the cells come from a kosher animal but there is no shechita? The debate is active in the rabbinic community and has no universally accepted answer.

**Vegetarianism and veganism**: compatible with kashrut and greatly simplifying it — concern about shechita, separation of meat and dairy, and organ inspection disappears. In principle plant foods are parve, although industrial processing, additives, or cross-contamination can affect their status. Some thinkers point out that kashrut, at its origin, did not prohibit meat but regulated it and loaded it with responsibility, and that a deep reading can naturally lead to a more plant-centered diet.

**Kosher but ethically debated**: industrial meat may come from animals raised in conditions incompatible with the principle of **tza'ar ba'alei chayim** — the prohibition against causing unnecessary suffering to animals. Overcrowding, transport, and living conditions are not evaluated by standard kosher certification. Some movements propose broader frameworks — such as the **Magen Tzedek** seal, which incorporates animal welfare and labor conditions — although they are not recognized by Orthodox authorities.

The conversation between halachah and contemporary ethics is one of the liveliest. And food is always at the center of that conversation.

## 18. Closing: eating with awareness

Eating, understood through kashrut, is much more than a set of prohibitions. It is a way of ordering daily life through the accumulation of knowledge, memory, discipline, and gratitude. From the family table to the modern food industry, from shechita to the berachah before bread, everything forms part of a single vision: that even the most ordinary act can be lived with awareness and ethical responsibility.

The prohibitions fulfill several functions that overlap and reinforce one another: religious obedience by following the commandments of the Torah; personal discipline developed through daily self-control; cultural identity sustained by a distinctive practice over time; and ethical awareness regarding the treatment of animals, food production, and the impact of consumption choices.

The shohet embodies all of this in concentrated form. He does not merely execute a technique: he acts as a point of contact between a three-thousand-year-old law and the plate of someone who trusts that what they eat was treated with rigor and respect. In that bond, invisible to most, lives something essential to kashrut: the idea that eating is not a neutral act, but one that can — and must — be performed with intention.
