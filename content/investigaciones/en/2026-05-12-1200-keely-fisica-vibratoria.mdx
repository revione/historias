# Keely: the numbers, the 1899 closure, and what remained open

John Ernst Worrell Keely (1837-1898) founded the Keely Motor Company in Philadelphia in 1872. He died in November 1898. During those twenty-six years he gave demonstrations before shareholders, journalists, and scientists, always inside his own building, and denied inspection of the apparatus to anyone who asked for it.

Two things are treated separately here. Whether the mechanism he described exists, which is decided by an energy calculation and an examination of the apparatus. And how financing was sustained for twenty-six years, what those who paid saw, and what they received in exchange for continuing to pay, which is decided by the documentary trail: shareholder minutes, press, correspondence.

## The calculation

Keely's most repeated claim is the one about the amount of water. In the version recorded by Julius Moritzen in *The Cosmopolitan* in April 1899, Keely declared that one gallon of water would be enough to take a steamship from New York to Liverpool and bring it back.

The numbers from the period are published. The White Star *Teutonic* crossed the Atlantic in six days with engines of 17,000 horsepower, about 12.7 MW. The crossing consumes on the order of 6.7 × 10¹² joules. The round trip, on the order of 1.3 × 10¹³.

One gallon of water is 3.8 kg, about 211 moles. The energy of the chemical bonds that hold them together is 286 kJ per mole, 6 × 10⁷ joules in total. Breaking those bonds costs exactly what recombining them gives back, so that figure is a ceiling, and that energy is also out of reach.

The difference is five orders of magnitude. The version about going around the world gives the same result: about 10¹³ joules required versus 1.6 × 10⁸ in a ten-liter bucket.

The calculation closes the water claim, and reaches no further than that.

What Lascelles-Scott declared in 1896 and the three engineers declared in 1897 still stands after it, and is decided with other material: what inspection was granted, and whether any effect remained outside ordinary explanation.

**The three versions.** The same claim circulated with three different destinations and the same amount of water.

A quart to take a thirty-car train from Philadelphia to New York. That is 145 km. A locomotive capable of pulling thirty cars develops on the order of 750 kW, and the trip at the speed of the period requires about 10¹⁰ joules. The quart contains 1.5 × 10⁷ joules in bonds. Three orders of magnitude are missing. *Warning:* the locomotive power is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a technical specification.

The same quart to take that train from Philadelphia to San Francisco. The route is thirty-three times longer. Four orders of magnitude are missing.

One gallon for the Atlantic round trip. Five orders of magnitude are missing.

The amount of water is the same in all three and the destination moves farther away. That progression is a datum about how the claims were formed, and it does not decide anything about the apparatus.

**The limit of the calculation.** It applies to chemical energy. Keely claimed an interatomic source, and in that version water would be the working substance. Moritzen already noted in 1899 that the energy for a crossing fits in the hydrogen of half a pint of water if it is converted into helium. What remains to be shown there is the mechanism by which that energy is accessed, and there is still nothing measured.

## The demonstrations, 1874-1897

Keely showed his work for twenty-three years, to selected audiences, with inspection closed at the point that decided the matter.

**1874.** After a series of experiments at the Gilsey House in New York, capital of 90,000 dollars is raised. On November 10, in Philadelphia, he blows into a mouthpiece for half a minute, pours in five gallons of tap water, and a pressure gauge reads 10,000 psi. Those present form a company and pay 50,000 dollars for the patent rights for the six New England states.

**1875 to 1881.** Scientific American follows the case and publishes that everything its envoys witnessed could be obtained with hidden compressed air. Keely adds the vaporic gun, which fires a bullet through several boards, and by about 1881 incorporates tuning forks and violin bows into the sessions. Other demonstrations broke thick cords and twisted iron bars.

Those effects admit both explanations. A tank at 10,000 psi goes through boards, bursts cords, and bends bars. A demonstration decides something when it produces an effect that the ordinary explanation does not give, and none of these did.

**September 20, 1884.** Exhibition of the gun at Sandy Hook, aboard the steamer *Laura M. Starin*, authorized by the Secretary of War. It is the demonstration with the broadest audience of his whole career. The *New York Times* covers it the next day.

**1887.** Experiments for the United States government at Fort Lafayette.

**November 17, 1888.** Keely enters Moyamensing Prison for contempt, after refusing the court order to operate and explain the motor before a committee of experts. He is released on bail on the 20th. On January 28, 1889, the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania revokes the order.

**November 1895.** Addison B. Burk and the engineer Alexander Scott obtain three sessions in greater detail. They report against Keely to Bloomfield-Moore: the weights that rose and fell were hollow spheres and disks.

**1896.** Bloomfield-Moore brings the physicist Wentworth Lascelles-Scott from London, and he investigates for a month with access to what he wished to examine and operating instructions given by Keely. He declares before the Franklin Institute that Keely had unquestionably demonstrated to him a force until then unknown.

The two Scotts disagreed, so they were placed together to witness more demonstrations. Bloomfield-Moore then proposed the decisive test: cut the cable that Alexander Scott maintained was an air line. Keely refused. She reduced his allowance to 250 dollars per month.

**June 18, 1897.** Demonstration of a new etheric motor before the general manager of the Manhattan Elevated Railway, the chief engineer of Western Union, and a representative of Metropolitan Traction. A motor of about 200 pounds is described that would develop 10 horsepower. The three declare themselves surprised by the force and refrain from giving an opinion on its value.

In the record, the refusal of 1896 carries more weight than the dismantling of 1899. The test was proposed by the person financing him, was cheap, preserved any principle, and decided the matter in one minute. What is on record is that it was not done.

## Two months between death and dismantling

Keely died on November 18, 1898, at his Philadelphia home, 1632 Oxford Street, of pneumonia. He had fallen ill the previous week and seemed to be recovering. He worsened that morning and died shortly after three in the afternoon. He was sixty-one.

Pneumonia, grouped with influenza, was then the leading cause of death in the United States, ahead of tuberculosis and gastrointestinal infections, according to the 1900 mortality records collected by the CDC.

A press retrospective published two days later adds that his health had been good until about three years earlier, and that being run over by a team of horses on Broad Street left him with aftereffects from which he never fully recovered. *Warning:* that comes from a wire report, without confirmation in another source.

While he was alive, he controlled access to the workshop. The shareholders went so far as to take him to court to force him to reveal the mechanism, and the formula that came out of that was an arbitrator who inspected under oath of secrecy and later declared himself satisfied. No open examination was made while he lived.

His death undid that control in two steps.

The first emptied the building. The December shareholders' meeting directed the machines to Boston, and in early January they left Philadelphia. The property remained.

The second changed who paid for it. Clara Jessup Bloomfield-Moore died in London on January 5, 1899, seven weeks after Keely. Her son Clarence took the building, hired the engineer Alexander Scott, and entered with press and photographers. False floors and ceilings were raised and a brick wall was demolished.

She was seventy-four, had been tied to the same affair for twenty years, and was ill, in a country where pneumonia led mortality. Access control to the building depended on Keely, and would have fallen just the same if she had been alive.

Two things would turn the coincidence of dates into a datum. Her cause of death, if it turns out to be something other than illness at seventy-four. And the date on which her son took the building, because a date before January 5 leaves her death outside the chain. Neither is verified here.

## What was found and who closed it in 1899

Compressed-air tubes appeared under the floorboards, a hollow cast-iron sphere of about three tons in the basement, and a silent water motor two floors below. *The New York Times* published it on January 20, 1899.

That is the closure cited by later summaries. Three facts about the person who produced it are usually missing.

It was directed by Clarence Bloomfield Moore, son of Clara Jessup Bloomfield-Moore, Keely's main financier, who had put into him about 100,000 dollars of the period in addition to a monthly allowance. The inheritance had been reduced through that route.

He had full physical access to the building, the access that was not granted to anyone while Keely lived.

The engineer he hired, Alexander Scott, had examined Keely in 1895 and had reported against him. He knew the apparatus and arrived with a position already taken. That explains why he was used, and at the same time it is a datum about the examination.

He had a direct interest in the outcome.

The tubes were there and the apparatus was explained. What changes with those three answers is what can be asserted from the report without further verification.

The four separately verifiable parts are these: Clarence Bloomfield Moore had title to the building, hired an examination, did it with press witnesses, and published it. None of them decides whether what was found in January 1899 explains what witnesses had seen in that building over twenty years.

*Unresolved:* secondary sources disagree on whether Moore bought the workshop or rented it, and on whether the commission came from him or from *The Philadelphia Press*. Nor is there any record of a relationship between him and Keely's widow, who was the one who sent the machines to Boston. The two parties acted separately as far as the record reaches.

## The machines in Boston

At the first shareholders' meeting after the death, on December 20, 1898, the widow's lawyer declared that Keely had named Thomas Burton Kinraide, of Boston, as the only person capable of continuing the work.

On January 2, 1899, the *Boston Daily Globe* reported that the essential parts of the workshop had been sent to Kinraide's laboratory in Jamaica Plain. The *New York Times* published on January 4 that Kinraide would continue the experiments.

The dismantling of the Philadelphia workshop was published on January 19 in *The Philadelphia Press* and on the 20th in the *Times*.

On May 7, 1899, the *Times* reported that Kinraide had abandoned all work on the motor and was returning machines and papers to the company. According to the *Globe*, he stopped working on the mechanism after that publication, which he considered a breach of trust.

What is on record is the transfer, four months of custody, and the return. There is no record of any documented attempt to operate the machines by someone with the knowledge to do so, nor any published result from those four months. With the reason for abandonment included, those four months are an attempt that never came to be made.

*Warning:* the line about the reason comes from the *Boston Daily Globe*, cited in a local Jamaica Plain blog. I have not checked it in the original. The five dates in this section are in identifiable mastheads, and the three from the *Times* have facsimiles accessible in TimesMachine.

*Unverified:* a recent compilation claims that Kinraide himself found evidence of fraud inside the motor and decided not to publish it out of friendship with Keely. It gives no contemporary source. If one appears, the meaning of these four months changes entirely, and for that reason it is noted here instead of being used.

## Who discussed it after the closure

Charles Fort wrote about the case in *Wild Talents*, 1932, thirty-three years after the verdict. He brings two facts that later versions do not bring.

The shareholders already knew about the basement sphere, because Keely did not hide it.

Nobody ever demonstrated that the motor could work by means of it.

The second is what decided the case, and it was available without searching. A compressed-air tank in the basement explains how the effect could have been made. Showing that it was made that way requires connecting the tank to the apparatus and making it work. That is not on record.

*Warning:* Fort wrote in favor of the anomalous and had his own interest. What is taken from him are statements checkable in another source.

## What he wrote himself

Two texts by Keely are accessible and describe his method in the first person.

*Latent Force*, in *Lippincott's Monthly Magazine*, volume 47, 1891, pages 639-644. Digitized in HathiTrust.

*The Operation of the Vibratory Circuit*, in *The New Science Review*, April 1895.

The summaries that circulate about Keely almost always rely on Bloomfield-Moore or the press. These two texts change what can be attributed to him.

## The 40 laws and the Dynasphere

Keely formulated forty laws describing a universe where everything is vibration. The central concepts are the atomoles, particles of the ether in perpetual vibration; the atomic tone, the proper frequency of each element; vibratory sympathy, by which two bodies in harmony attract each other; the neutral center, a point of convergence of opposing forces; and a scale of 105 octaves where sound, heat, light, gravity, and electricity are the same energy at different frequencies.

The Musical Dynasphere was the central device: a hollow sphere with an inner ring of seven resonator tubes graduated in the diatonic scale, strings tensioned inside it, and a neutral center where the axes converge. Keely maintained that the sphere organized etheric flow through harmonic resonance.

*Source of these data:* the formulation of the forty laws and the measurements of the Dynasphere come from Dale Pond's compilation. Pond spent decades gathering fragments of Keely's work and built the replica called Atlin. The original writings, which according to Bloomfield-Moore amounted to thousands of pages, remain unrecovered. Pond's replica rotated inconsistently. The effects attributed to it appear in his own bulletin, without independent measurement.

## The translation into the flow model

*Warning:* this section is my own interpretation and has no supporting datum.

Keely's vocabulary predates quantum physics and is musical. Put in the terms of the unified flow model, the ether corresponds to Φ, vibratory sympathy to synchronization of O, the mass chord to the O(ω) profile of each material, and the neutral center to a point of convergence of ∇O.

A vocabulary translation predicts zero by itself. It would become worth something if the correspondence yielded a calculable magnitude that the original formulation did not give, and that magnitude were measured. Until that happens, this is a map between two glossaries.

## Where the knowledge stands and what would close it

In 1899 it was stated that the apparatus worked with compressed air, the person who stated it had a direct interest in the outcome, and the connection between the tank and the motor remained undemonstrated.

There are no publications explaining how Keely produced the effects that witnesses described, nor an independent examination of the building done by someone without an interest in the outcome.

The machines spent four months of 1899 in the hands of the person he himself named as capable of operating them, and no result from that was published.

The chemical version of the energy claim is closed by five orders of magnitude. The interatomic version still has no measured mechanism. In June 1895 the journal *Electricity* offered to reproduce all his phenomena in sixty days, and Keely left that offer unanswered.

What would close what remains open is concrete. On the apparatus side, a reconstruction of the 1899 workshop that shows the tank actuating the motor, with enough detail to repeat it. On the theory side, a replica of the Dynasphere with instrumented measurement, published protocol, and blind control, producing sustained rotation or a measurable field above noise.

Neither exists today.

## Graphic material

What exists in images, with its provenance:

- Portraits of Keely posing with the motor, around 1895. Public domain, on Wikimedia Commons.
- Diagram of the 1879 motor, published in the New York Journal and Advertiser of January 29, 1899, page 9. Public domain.
- Illustrated article on the dismantling. The Philadelphia Press, January 19, 1899.
- Stock certificate of the Keely Motor Company, in the holdings of the Library of Congress.
- Glass plates taken by Kinraide at his house in Jamaica Plain during the four months of 1899. They are the last known photographs of the apparatus. The negatives appeared in 2005 in Kinraide's former laboratory, among several hundred plates, located by Jeff and Rita Behary.

Of all the recovered plates, three correspond to Keely's motor. They are published in high resolution on Internet Archive, in the Turn Of The Century Electrotherapy Museum collection, with rights declared by Jeff Behary in 2019. The most detailed preserves the original envelope with Kinraide's handwriting noting that it is a side view of the motor. The lamp, curtains, and candles in the room can be seen.

Those three plates document the apparatus after it left the Philadelphia building and before it returned to the company.

*Unverified:* the same collection claims that Kinraide discovered that the motor was a fraud. Behary has the physical material, and that claim arrives without a contemporary document supporting it. It remains noted as pending.
