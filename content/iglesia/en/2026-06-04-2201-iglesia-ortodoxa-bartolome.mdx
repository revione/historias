# Bartholomew I and the Orthodox Church — The Last Christian Empire

---

## 1054 — The Great Schism: Rome and Constantinople excommunicate each other

On July 16, 1054, Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida entered the Basilica of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople and placed a bull of excommunication on the altar. The document excommunicated Patriarch Michael I Cerularius and his followers, accusing them of heresy. Hours later, Michael Cerularius convened a synod and excommunicated the papal legates. Christianity split in two.

It did not come out of nowhere. Tensions had been building for centuries. The theological differences were real: the Latins had added the *Filioque* to the Nicene Creed — the doctrine that the Holy Spirit proceeds "from the Father and the Son," not just from the Father — without consulting the Eastern churches. The Greeks considered this a heresy and a violation of the ecumenical councils. But the schism was equally political. The Pope in Rome claimed universal primacy over all Christendom. Constantinople, the New Rome, responded that the bishop of Rome was first "in honor," not in jurisdiction. The linguistic and cultural divide — the Latin West versus the Greek East — did the rest.

At the time, contemporaries did not understand that this was definitive. The excommunications were personal, not institutional. They believed it was just another dispute between prelates that would be resolved with the next emperor or the next council. But the attempts at reunification failed one after another. The Council of Lyon (1274) was rejected by the Byzantine clergy and people. The Council of Florence (1439) came too late: Constantinople fell to the Ottomans fourteen years later. By then, the schism was already irreversible.

What was born in 1054 was not simply an administrative rupture. It was the bifurcation of Christendom into two civilizations with different theologies, liturgies, languages, political structures, and mentalities. The history of Europe, from the Crusades to NATO, passes through that fracture.

---

## 1453 — Constantinople falls; the Patriarch survives the Empire

On May 29, 1453, Mehmed II took Constantinople. The last Byzantine emperor, Constantine XI, died fighting on the walls. The Eastern Roman Empire — which had lasted 1,123 years since Constantine's founding of Constantinople — ceased to exist.

And yet, the Church survived. Mehmed II, in a statesman's calculation, not only allowed the Patriarchate to continue functioning, but appointed Gennadius II Scholarius as the new patriarch, handed him the pastoral staff, and recognized him as the civil leader of Orthodox Christians within the Ottoman Empire. The *millet* system turned the patriarch into an ethnarch: religious, civil, and fiscal head of all Orthodox under Turkish rule.

The Ecumenical Patriarchate had lost its empire. But it gained something perhaps more durable: an institutional structure that did not depend on a State, an ecclesiastical bureaucracy seasoned in diplomacy, and moral authority over millions of faithful scattered across the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Middle East. The earthly empire faded. The institution remained.

---

## 1589 — Moscow becomes its own patriarchate

For centuries, the Russian Church was a metropolis dependent on Constantinople. Its metropolitans were appointed by the ecumenical patriarch. But with the fall of Byzantium, Moscow began to cultivate its own political theology. The monk Philotheus of Pskov formulated the doctrine of the "Third Rome": the first Rome fell to heresy, the second (Constantinople) fell to the Turks, Moscow is the third and there will be no fourth.

In 1589, Patriarch Jeremias II of Constantinople — pressured by Tsar Boris Godunov and in need of funds — accepted to elevate the Metropolitan of Moscow to the rank of patriarch. The Patriarchate of Moscow was born, the fifth in the Orthodox hierarchical order. The seed was planted. Constantinople remained "first among equals," but Moscow already saw itself as the true successor of Byzantium.

---

## 1721 — Peter the Great abolishes the Russian patriarchate and replaces it with an imperial ministry

Peter I did not trust an ecclesiastical counter-power. In 1721, he abolished the Moscow Patriarchate and replaced it with the Most Holy Governing Synod, a body of bishops supervised by a lay procurator appointed by the tsar — literally an imperial functionary, often a military man, who could veto any ecclesiastical decision. The Russian Church became a department of the State.

This is the deep tradition that explains what we see today. The Russian Orthodox Church has operated for three centuries as an extension of state power. The tsars controlled it. The Soviets persecuted, infiltrated, and domesticated it. And when the USSR fell, the KGB's successor, Vladimir Putin, understood perfectly the value of having a Church at his service.

---

## 1948 — Stalin restores the Moscow Patriarchate and turns it into a geopolitical instrument

The 1917 Revolution unleashed a fierce persecution of the Russian Church. Lenin ordered mass executions of clergy. Stalin closed churches, shot bishops, and sent tens of thousands of priests to the Gulag. But in 1943, with the war against Germany, Stalin changed tactics. He needed patriotism, national cohesion. He allowed a council to convene and elect Metropolitan Sergius as patriarch.

In 1948, with the war over and the Soviet bloc consolidated, Stalin convened a Pan-Orthodox Conference in Moscow. The goal: to wrest from Constantinople the leadership of the Orthodox world and turn Moscow into a kind of "Orthodox Vatican." He did not fully succeed, but he laid the groundwork for what would become the Russian Church's foreign policy during the Cold War: a satellite Church at the service of the Kremlin's geostrategic interests.

---

## 1991 — Bartholomew I is born: a monk from Imbros becomes the 270th successor of St. Andrew

Dimitrios Archondonis was born in 1940 on the island of Imbros (today Gökçeada), an Aegean island that was then mostly Greek and today, after decades of Turkish demographic pressure, has almost lost its Orthodox population. He was ordained a priest in 1969, studied canon law in Rome and Switzerland, spoke seven languages — Greek, Turkish, Latin, Italian, French, English, and German — and was ordained Metropolitan of Chalcedon in 1990.

On October 22, 1991, the Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate elected him Archbishop of Constantinople, New Rome, and Ecumenical Patriarch. He became Bartholomew I.

Unlike his predecessors, who managed a cloistered and almost decorative institution, Bartholomew understood that the 21st century offered the Patriarchate a unique opportunity. The fall of the USSR in 1991 had left a vacuum of moral authority in Eastern Europe. Orthodox churches that had lived under communist control for decades sought to reconnect with the rest of the world. And Constantinople, which had been a shadow of its former self for centuries, could become relevant again.

---

## 2007 — Putin and Kirill: the alliance that unites cross, throne, and Kremlin

In 2007, Vladimir Putin signed a cooperation agreement with the Russian Orthodox Church that formalized something already real: the Church as the spiritual agency of the Russian state. The document granted the Church tax exemptions, privileged access to schools, hospitals, and armed forces, and de facto status as a national religion. In return, the Church blessed Kremlin policies.

At the head of the Moscow Patriarchate since 2009 is Kirill I (born Vladimir Mikhailovich Gundyayev). His political theology is summed up in the concept of the *Russkiy Mir* (Russian World): the idea that Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus constitute a unique civilization united by the Orthodox faith, the Russian language, and a shared historical destiny that Moscow has the right — and duty — to protect.

In December 2007, Metropolitan Kirill celebrated a joint liturgy in Moscow with bishops of the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia, sealing the reunification of the Moscow Patriarchate with the anti-communist diaspora that had broken with Moscow after 1917. The service was held in the rebuilt Cathedral of Christ the Savior — blown up by Stalin in 1931 and rebuilt in the 1990s as an emblem of national rebirth.

---

## 2016 — Bartholomew surprises the world: the Holy and Great Council in Crete

For centuries, the Orthodox world had not held a pan-Orthodox council. In June 2016, Bartholomew convened the Holy and Great Council in Crete. Ten of the fourteen autocephalous churches attended — Moscow and three others boycotted the event. It was a gesture of leadership: Constantinople demonstrated it could convene, gather, and preside over the Orthodox world without Moscow's permission.

The council approved documents on the diaspora, ecumenism, fasting, and marriage. But its true meaning was political: Bartholomew claimed the center of gravity of Orthodoxy. Moscow responded with fury. Patriarch Kirill described the absence of the absent churches as a "wound" in the council and accused Constantinople of proceeding without consensus. It was clear that the two visions of the Orthodox world — Constantinople as a real primus inter pares or Moscow as the dominant Third Rome — were on a collision course.

---

## 2018 — Bartholomew grants autocephaly to Ukraine and unleashes a geopolitical schism

On October 11, 2018, the Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate announced it would proceed to grant autocephaly — ecclesiastical independence — to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine. Until then, the Moscow Patriarchate claimed exclusive canonical jurisdiction over Ukraine. Bartholomew's decision was equivalent to declaring that Ukraine was not Russian canonical territory.

The measure was preceded by years of diplomatic preparation. Bartholomew made a threefold decision: 1) He revoked the 1686 act by which Constantinople had temporarily ceded Kyiv's jurisdiction to Moscow. 2) He canonically rehabilitated Patriarch Filaret of Kyiv and Metropolitan Makari, previously excommunicated by Moscow. 3) He granted the *Tomos* (decree) of autocephaly to the newly unified Orthodox Church of Ukraine.

On January 6, 2019, in St. George's Cathedral in Istanbul, Bartholomew signed the Tomos and handed it to Metropolitan Epiphanius, the new primate of the Orthodox Church of Ukraine. The next day, Ukrainian President Petro Poroshenko attended the Christmas liturgy in Kyiv carrying the document like a diplomatic trophy.

The Moscow Patriarchate broke communion with Constantinople. Kirill declared that Bartholomew had "crossed a red line" and that his act was "schismatic." Russian priests stopped commemorating the ecumenical patriarch in the liturgy. The most serious fracture in the Orthodox world since 1054 was a fact.

---

## 2022 — The Russian invasion of Ukraine splits the Orthodox world

On February 24, 2022, Russia invaded Ukraine. The Moscow Patriarchate, far from condemning the aggression, blessed it. Kirill described the war as a metaphysical conflict against a degenerate West. Russian soldiers went to the front with icons blessed by the patriarch.

Bartholomew, in contrast, publicly condemned the invasion. In a CNN interview in March 2022, he declared that the war was "a crime of aggression" and called on Putin to stop it.

The result was a cascading division within Orthodoxy. Churches such as those of Greece, Cyprus, Alexandria, and Constantinople itself recognized Ukrainian autocephaly. Others, such as those of Serbia, Georgia, Antioch, and several Slavic churches, aligned with Moscow. The theological war ran parallel to the military war.

In Ukraine, the Orthodox Church loyal to Moscow suffered a collapse of legitimacy. Entire parishes defected to Epiphanius's autocephalous church. The shadow of the Moscow Patriarchate in Ukraine was fading.

## How the Orthodox Church works today

Unlike the Catholic Church — monarchical, vertical, with a Pope exercising universal jurisdiction —, the Orthodox Church is a communion of autocephalous churches: independent in their internal government, but united by faith, sacraments, and mutual recognition. Today there are fourteen universally recognized autocephalous churches, plus some whose status is disputed (such as Ukraine's). The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople is "first among equals": he presides over the communion but does not govern the other patriarchs.

The differences with Catholicism are profound and not only theological. The Orthodox do not recognize papal infallibility nor the Immaculate Conception of Mary. Priests may marry (before ordination). The Eucharistic bread is leavened, not unleavened. There is no Purgatory in their eschatology. The liturgy is always sung, without instruments, in the local language. With some 260 million faithful — of whom approximately 100 million are in Russia —, the Orthodox Church is the second largest Christian communion on the planet.

---

## The Third Rome against the New Rome: Constantinople and Moscow as two models of power

The confrontation between Bartholomew and Kirill is far more than a dispute over Ukraine. It is a clash between two rival conceptions of what the Orthodox Church should be.

Constantinople's model is that of a spiritual institution exercising moral leadership without armies, without territory, and without state budget. Bartholomew governs from the Phanar, a walled compound in Istanbul, with fewer than 100 monks and seminarians. His power does not lie in divisions or missiles. It lies in his canonical authority, his diplomatic sophistication, and the historical prestige of being the direct heir of the Church of the ecumenical councils.

Moscow's model is that of a Church-State: funded by the Kremlin, integrated into the security apparatus, deployed as an instrument of soft power. Kirill has golden cathedrals, an extensive bureaucracy, a presence in 60 countries, and an explicit alliance with a president who uses her to bless wars.

Paradoxically, the weak one has more real power than the strong one. Bartholomew, without troops or funds, is capable of giving Ukraine what no army can grant: a national Church that does not answer to Moscow. And that makes him a more dangerous adversary for the Kremlin than many heads of state.

---

Bartholomew I does not govern a country. He presides over an institution that survived the Eastern Roman Empire, the Ottoman Empire, Turkey's secularization, and the tsars. That is precisely what makes him fearsome for the Kremlin: he represents an authority that precedes the modern Russian state by more than a millennium, that does not depend on military budgets or oil prices, and that can redraw borders with a document and a signature.

In these pages we have described the power of corporations, states, dynastic families, media, unions, and militaries. All of them are powers that operate within the limits of a territory, a legislation, or a generation. The Church — and in particular the Orthodox Church — is of another nature. It is a power measured in millennia, not fiscal quarters. Its currency is not capital or weapons: it is legitimacy. And in the geopolitics of legitimacy, Bartholomew I, the patriarch without an empire, continues to move pieces that presidents cannot ignore.
