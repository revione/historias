# The Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem and the Zionist Project — Land, Faith, and Power

---

## 4th Century — The Church of Golgotha: How the Patriarchate Built Its Real Estate Empire

The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem is, alongside Constantinople, Alexandria, and Antioch, one of the four original patriarchates of Christendom. Its foundation dates back to the Council of Chalcedon in 451. But its roots go deeper: the Christian community of Jerusalem considers itself the mother church of all churches, founded at Pentecost.

The Patriarchate's real estate patrimony was built over fifteen hundred years. Under the Ottoman Empire (1517–1917), the Patriarchate consolidated its territorial power. The Ottoman *millet* system granted each religious community legal and fiscal autonomy. The Patriarchate not only administered holy places and ecclesiastical properties but exercised civil jurisdiction over Orthodox Christians. Today, the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem is the second largest landowner in Israel, second only to the Israel Land Authority. Its properties include the land under the Knesset, the prime minister's residence on Balfour Street, and vast extensions in Jerusalem.

---

## 1845 — The Greek Coup: The Patriarchate Becomes a Fief of Hellenism

Until the 19th century, the Patriarchate of Jerusalem was a predominantly Arab institution. But in 1845, the Ecumenical Patriarchate and Orthodox churches of Greece and Russia imposed exclusively Greek leadership. Arab bishops were displaced. Greek became the exclusive liturgical language. This internal colonial structure — ethnically Greek leadership over an overwhelmingly Palestinian flock — is the foundational fact that explains the Patriarchate's vulnerability to the Zionist project.

---

## 1948-1967 — The Nakba, Divided Jerusalem, and the Patriarchate as a Real Estate Power

The 1948 war split Jerusalem in two. The Patriarchate's properties were distributed on both sides of the armistice line. After the 1967 war, when Israel occupied and later annexed East Jerusalem, the game changed in scale.

---

## 1967 — The Occupation of East Jerusalem

For the Patriarchate, the Israeli occupation of East Jerusalem introduced an existential dilemma. On one hand, its historic seat now fell under Israeli sovereignty. On the other, the overwhelming majority of its faithful were Palestinian, living under military occupation. The Patriarchate tried for decades to maintain an impossible balance.

---

## 2001 — Irineos Ascends to the Throne of James

Irineos I was elected patriarch in August 2001. He found the Patriarchate in desperate financial straits. In this context, purchase offers from Jewish settler organizations were not only political temptations. They were financial lifelines.

---

## 2004 — The Sale of the Imperial Hotel and the Petra Hotel: The Jaffa Gate Scandal

In March 2005, the Israeli newspaper *Maariv* revealed that two emblematic properties at Jaffa Gate had been sold with 99-year leases to Ateret Cohanim. The buyer was not directly Ateret Cohanim but a network of shell companies registered in Caribbean tax havens. The total price of the operation was around $65 million. Palestinian tenants received eviction orders.

---

## 2005 — Irineos Falls: The Synod Deposes the Patriarch

The revelation triggered an unprecedented crisis. On May 6, 2005, the Synod voted overwhelmingly to depose Irineos. Jordan withdrew its recognition. The Palestinian Authority declared it would not deal with him.

---

## 2005 — Theophilos III Takes the Throne

On August 22, 2005, the Holy Synod elected Theophilos III as the new Patriarch. Theophilos came with an explicit promise: to stop the sales of Patriarchate properties to settler organizations.

---

## 2007-2010 — Israeli Courts Shield the Sales

Between 2007 and 2010, Israeli courts ruled in favor of the buyers in every case. The formal legal reasoning was impeccable: signed contracts existed. But that formal impeccability concealed a stark political reality: courts were validating property transfers from a Palestinian Christian institution to Jewish settler organizations.

---

## 2012 — Nahalat Shimon and the Battle for Sheikh Jarrah

In 2012, Nahalat Shimon claimed land rights in the Sheikh Jarrah neighborhood, alleging to have acquired rights from the Patriarchate. The case became a global emblem in 2021, when protests against evictions in Sheikh Jarrah were one of the triggers of the May 2021 Gaza war.

---

## 2017 — The West Jerusalem Land Sale Scandal

In 2017, a *Haaretz* investigation revealed that under Theophilos III, the Patriarchate had continued transferring properties to companies linked to settler groups.

---

## 2022 — The Secret Nazareth Deal

In 2022, a new investigation revealed the Patriarchate had transferred long-term lease rights over land in central Nazareth to investors with connections to the settlement movement.

---

## 2023 — The Knesset and Balfour Street

The land on which the Knesset and the prime minister's residence stand belongs to the Patriarchate. Israel pays rent to the Patriarchate for both. This relationship creates an interdependency that limits the Patriarchate's room for action.

---

## The Mechanism: How a Church Is Hollowed Out from Within

The pattern of sales is remarkably consistent: **First**, financial vulnerability. **Second**, opaque structure with companies in tax havens. **Third**, 99-year leases that are de facto permanent property transfers. **Fourth**, judicial ratification. **Fifth**, institutional defenselessness.

---

## The Colonial Dimension: Greek Leadership, Palestinian Flock

The Patriarchate is governed exclusively by clergy of Greek ethnicity. This has its roots in the 19th century. The result is a deep dissociation between the institution and its community. That dissociation is the crack through which buyers enter.

---

## Corruption, Structural Vulnerability, or State Strategy?

Three explanations — and probably all three are true in different proportions: the corruption explanation, the structural explanation, and the strategic explanation. Most likely, all three operate simultaneously.

---

## The Future: Can the Patriarchate Survive Itself?

The Palestinian Orthodox community — some 50,000 faithful — watches its Church with a mixture of filial love and despair. The reform demands are clear: ordination of Palestinian bishops, creation of a mixed governing council with lay participation, independent audit of finances. None of these demands has been substantially met.

The Patriarchate of Jerusalem is, in a sense, a microcosm of the entire Palestinian tragedy: land lost not through military conquest — which also happens — but through the slow, methodical, bureaucratic transfer of property that history books will one day call the true geography of the occupation.
