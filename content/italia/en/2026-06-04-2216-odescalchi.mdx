# The Odescalchi — The Pope Who Saved Europe from the Ottomans

---

## 13th–16th Centuries — Origins in Como: Lombard Bankers on the Alpine Frontier

The Odescalchi hail from Como, the Lombard city situated on the shores of the lake that bears its name, at the southern edge of the Alps. Como was, since the Middle Ages, a center of trade between Italy and northern Europe —its merchants controlled the routes crossing the Alpine passes to Switzerland and Germany— and a thriving city in the production of silk, wool, and textile manufactures. The patriciate of Como was formed by families of merchants and bankers who had accumulated capital through international trade, and the Odescalchi were one of them.

The surname Odescalchi appears in the notarial records of Como from the late 13th century. The first documented Odescalchi worked as bankers, wool merchants, and moneylenders. They were members of the Lombard urban patriciate, a social class that in the 14th and 15th centuries grew wealthy through the trade of cloth, silk, and precious metals, and extended their financial networks throughout the Italian peninsula and beyond the Alps. Like other families of mercantile Lombardy —the Borromeo of Milan, the Gallio of Como—, the Odescalchi understood that true power in the Italy of city-states lay not in the possession of feudal lands but in the control of credit and trade routes.

During the 15th and 16th centuries, the Odescalchi consolidated their financial position and diversified their investments. They established banking branches in Milan, Venice, and Rome, purchased urban properties in Como and in the Lombard countryside, and cultivated relations with the papal administration —the Roman Curia had been, since the 15th century, the main financial client of Italian bankers—. However, unlike the Sienese Chigi or the Milanese Borromeo, the Odescalchi did not fully integrate into high Roman aristocracy during the Renaissance: they remained a family of provincial bankers, prosperous but removed from the papal court and its great networks of patronage and nepotism.

The man who would change the destiny of the Odescalchi —and of Europe— was born in Como on May 16, 1611. His name was Benedetto.

---

## 1611–1676 — Benedetto Odescalchi: Training and Curial Career of a Reformer

Benedetto Odescalchi was born into a family of Como bankers who had reached a respectable but not dominant position in 17th-century Lombardy. The early death of his father in 1621 left the family in a delicate financial situation, and the young Benedetto was sent to Genoa to train in banking under the tutelage of a relative. But his vocation was not commerce: in 1626, at the age of fifteen, he moved to Rome and entered the service of the Curia.

The curial career of Benedetto Odescalchi was a model of meritocratic ascent within the papal administration. He studied civil and canon law at the University of La Sapienza, earned his doctorate, and began climbing positions in the ecclesiastical tribunals and in the Apostolic Chamber —the finance ministry of the Papal States—. His administrative competence and reputation for personal integrity caught the attention of successive popes: Urban VIII Barberini appointed him apostolic protonotary; Innocent X Pamphili sent him as papal commissioner to the Marche region, where he distinguished himself by aiding the population during a famine; and Alexander VII Chigi created him a cardinal in 1645, at only thirty-four years of age.

As a cardinal, Odescalchi served as bishop of Novara —in Piedmont— for several years, where he managed the diocese with a fiscal and pastoral rigor that contrasted with the nepotism and ostentation of other prelates. He sold his personal property to pay the debts of the diocese, reduced the expenses of the episcopal curia, and personally dedicated himself to the pastoral visitation of the parishes. In 1650, he resigned the bishopric and returned to Rome, where he partially withdrew from public life, dedicating himself to prayer and the administration of charitable works. His austere lifestyle and his aversion to nepotism made him an atypical figure within the College of Cardinals of the 17th century.

On September 21, 1676, after two months of conclave and after the main factions —French, Spanish, the cardinal nephews of Clement X— failed to impose their candidates, Benedetto Odescalchi was elected Pope. He was sixty-five years old. Initially, he resisted accepting the office —he yielded only to the insistence of the college of cardinals— and took the name **Innocent XI**, in honor of Innocent X Pamphili, who had promoted him in his career. He imposed as a condition for accepting the tiara that all the cardinals sign a document pledging to respect his reforms. It was the first time in history that a candidate for the papacy imposed conditions on the conclave.

---

## 1676 — The War Against Nepotism: The Pope Who Refused to Enrich His Family

Innocent XI's first decision after his election was revolutionary in its simplicity: he refused to make his nephew a cardinal —nor any other relative— and forbade the members of his family from receiving offices, benefices, or rents from the Papal State. In a Rome accustomed to the systematic nepotism of the Barberini, the Borghese, the Chigi, or the Pamphili —where each Pope had turned his nephews into cardinals, princes, and owners of immense fortunes—, the decision was a political earthquake.

Papal nepotism was not a mere tradition: it was the mechanism by which popes built political alliances within the College of Cardinals, secured the loyalty of the Curia, and, above all, guaranteed the survival of their family in the Roman aristocracy once the pontificate ended. To renounce nepotism meant, in practice, to renounce the most powerful tool of internal governance of the Holy See.

Innocent XI not only refused to appoint cardinal nephews, but promulgated the bull *Romanus Pontifex* in 1678 with written norms to combat nepotism in future pontificates. The bull was not fully applied by his successors —Alexander VIII Ottoboni restored nepotism partially in 1689—, but it set a precedent that would culminate in the bull *Romanum decet Pontificem* of Innocent XII in 1692, which formally abolished the figure of the cardinal nephew.

Innocent XI's nephew, Livio Odescalchi, was not made a cardinal or a prince. He was appointed, significantly, commander of the pontifical troops —a military, not an ecclesiastical or curial post—. The Odescalchi family did not enrich itself during the pontificate of Innocent XI: the Pope used ecclesiastical revenues to restore the papal finances, which were on the verge of bankruptcy after the spendthrift pontificates of Urban VIII and Innocent X, and to finance the war against the Ottomans.

Innocent XI's refusal of nepotism earned him the hostility of broad sectors of the Curia —accustomed to dividing among themselves the benefits of each new pontificate— but granted him a moral authority that no other 17th-century Pope possessed. He could not be accused of enriching his family because, precisely, he refused to do so.

## 1681–1684 — The Challenge to Louis XIV: The Pope Against the Sun King

Innocent XI's second great battle was against Louis XIV of France, the most powerful monarch in Europe, who in the 1680s was at the height of his power —Versailles was being built at full speed, the French armies dominated the continent, and Bourbon absolutism openly challenged papal authority—.

The conflict began in 1681, when Louis XIV extended the *régale* —the king's right to collect the revenues of vacant dioceses and to appoint bishops in them— to all the dioceses of France, including those of the south that had historically been exempt. Innocent XI responded with three consecutive pontifical briefs condemning the extension of the régale and threatening canonical sanctions. Louis XIV, in turn, convoked in 1682 the **Assembly of the French Clergy**, which promulgated the *Declaratio Cleri Gallicani* —the famous Four Gallican Articles—, drafted by Bishop Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, which denied the Pope's supremacy over councils and limited his authority over national churches.

Innocent XI responded with maximum severity: he refused to grant canonical confirmation —the bulls of institution— to the French bishops who had participated in the Assembly of 1682, leaving episcopal sees vacant for years. By 1688, thirty-five French dioceses lacked a canonically confirmed bishop. The Church of France was on the verge of schism.

The French ambassador in Rome, the Duke of Estrées, was expelled from the papal court. Relations between France and the Holy See were frozen. Louis XIV seriously considered the possibility of a Gallican schism —the creation of a French national Church independent of Rome— but the need to maintain Catholic unity in Europe, especially in the face of the Ottoman advance, finally dissuaded the Sun King.

The conflict between Innocent XI and Louis XIV is one of the most tense episodes in the history of relations between the papacy and the French monarchy. Innocent XI —a Lombard Pope, without his own armies, without a family to back him, without the network of nepotistic alliances that sustained the Curia— stood alone against the most powerful monarch in Europe. And he did not yield. The historian Ludwig von Pastor describes Innocent XI as "the bravest Pope of the 17th century."

---

## 1683 — The Holy League and the Battle of Vienna: The Pope Who Stopped Islam in Europe

On July 14, 1683, an Ottoman army of approximately one hundred and fifty thousand men, commanded by Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa, laid siege to Vienna, the capital of the Habsburgs and the key to the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation. If Vienna fell, the road to Germany, Italy, and the heart of Europe would be open to the Ottoman Empire. Catholic Christendom was facing its greatest military threat since the fall of Constantinople in 1453.

Innocent XI played a decisive role in the salvation of Vienna. He did not fight with the sword —he never left Rome— but he fought with money, diplomacy, and papal authority. His action unfolded on three simultaneous fronts:

**First, diplomacy.** Innocent XI used all the influence of the Holy See to build the **Holy League**, a military alliance between the Holy Roman Empire —under Emperor Leopold I of Habsburg— and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth —under King John III Sobieski—. The alliance united two Catholic powers that historically distrusted each other —the Habsburgs and the Poles— but who, under papal mediation, agreed to cooperate against the common Ottoman enemy. The treaty of alliance was signed in Warsaw on March 31, 1683, thanks largely to the diplomacy of the apostolic nuncio in Poland, Cardinal Pallavicini.

**Second, financing.** Innocent XI emptied the pontifical coffers to finance the Holy League. He not only invested revenues from the Papal States, but imposed an extraordinary tithe —the *subsidium turcicum*— on the clergy of all Christendom, raising funds in Spain, Portugal, Italy, Germany, and Poland. The sums sent to Emperor Leopold I and John III Sobieski were the financial backbone of the military campaign of 1683. Without Innocent XI's money, the defense of Vienna would not have been possible.

**Third, symbolic mobilization.** Innocent XI declared 1683 a year of *extraordinary jubilee* for the defense of Christendom, exhorting all European Catholics to pray and contribute financially to the cause. The religious mobilization was massive: processions, rogations, collections, and sermons throughout Catholic Europe prepared the psychological ground for war. The Pope transformed the military conflict into a war of religion —the last great war of religion in European history—.

On September 12, 1683, the Polish king John III Sobieski, at the head of the Polish heavy cavalry —the famous *Winged Hussars*—, launched a massive charge from the hills of Kahlenberg that shattered the Ottoman camp and broke the siege of Vienna. Kara Mustafa fled, abandoning his tent, his banner, and his treasure —the tent of the grand vizier was sent by Sobieski as a trophy to the Pope, who kept it in the Vatican—. The Battle of Vienna was the greatest Ottoman defeat since the capture of Constantinople and marked the beginning of the Turkish retreat in central and eastern Europe. In the following years, the Holy League —always financed in part by Rome— would reconquer Hungary, Belgrade, and broad areas of the Balkans.

Innocent XI did not live to see the final outcome of the war —he died in 1689— but his role in the defense of Vienna was unanimously recognized by his contemporaries. King John III Sobieski wrote him a letter after the victory with the famous words: *"Veni, vidi, Deus vicit"* —"I came, I saw, God conquered"—. The Pope of the Como bankers had saved Christian Europe from the greatest Islamic army since the age of the caliphs.

## 1689 — Death and Legacy: The Incorruptible Pope

Innocent XI died in Rome on August 12, 1689, at the age of seventy-eight, after thirteen years of pontificate. He died poor —he had spent the papal revenues on the war against the Ottomans and on reforming the finances of the Holy See— and left instructions that his funeral be austere, without the usual pomp of papal funerals. All Rome mourned his death: the Romans, who had satirized the Barberini and despised the Pamphili, recognized in Innocent XI a different Pope.

The papal coffers, which in 1676 were on the verge of bankruptcy, were restored in 1689 thanks to the rigorous fiscal administration of Innocent XI. And the moral prestige of the papacy, which had fallen to its lowest level during the nepotistic pontificates of the Baroque —Urban VIII, Innocent X—, had been restored by the first reforming Pope in more than a century.

Innocent XI was beatified by Pius XII in 1956, in recognition of his austere life, his personal integrity, and his defense of Christendom against the Ottomans. His tomb, in Saint Peter's Basilica, bears an epitaph that sums up his legacy: *"Pauperes evangelizavit, pacem dedit, hostes Ecclesiae debellavit"* —"He evangelized the poor, gave peace, defeated the enemies of the Church"—.

## 18th Century — The Odescalchi Accumulate Wealth by Conventional Means

Unlike other papal families that enriched themselves through direct nepotism during their Pope's pontificate, the Odescalchi accumulated their principal fortune *after* the death of Innocent XI, and by conventional means: strategic marriages, land purchases, and prudent estate management.

The Pope's lay nephew, **Livio Odescalchi** (1652–1713), who had served as commander of the pontifical troops during his uncle's pontificate, inherited the task of consolidating the family's power. Livio was a competent soldier and a skillful administrator. After the death of Innocent XI, he received from Emperor Leopold I —in gratitude for the Pope's support during the war against the Ottomans— the title of **Prince of the Holy Roman Empire** and the Duchy of Syrmia —in present-day Serbia—. But Livio did not aspire to a Balkan fief: he wanted a territory in Italy.

The great acquisition of the Odescalchi took place in **1696**, when Livio bought the **Duchy of Bracciano** from the Orsini family. Bracciano —about thirty kilometers northwest of Rome, next to the lake that bears its name— was a strategic fief with a formidable castle: the **Castello Orsini**, a 15th-century fortress built by the Orsini on a hilltop above the lake, one of the most imposing fortresses in Latium.

The Orsini —one of the oldest families of the Roman Black Nobility, with centuries of feudal power in Latium— sold the duchy and the castle to the Odescalchi because they needed liquidity. The Odescalchi, who had banking capital and had not squandered their fortune during the pontificate of Innocent XI, were able to pay the sum. The transaction transformed the Odescalchi from Lombard bankers into feudal lords of Latium with a castle, a lake, and a ducal title.

The castle was renamed **Castello Orsini-Odescalchi**, a name it retains to this day. Livio Odescalchi became the first Odescalchi Duke of Bracciano, and his descendants held the title and the property during the following centuries.

## 18th–19th Centuries — The Odescalchi in the Black Nobility: Castle, Lake, and Title

During the 18th and 19th centuries, the Odescalchi followed the pattern of the papal families of the Black Nobility: settle in the Roman aristocracy, preserve the patrimony, and connect with other houses through strategic marriages. The family merged with the Erba —another Lombard family—, forming the **Odescalchi-Erba** branch, and connected with the Chigi, the Pallavicini, and other papal houses.

The Castello Orsini-Odescalchi of Bracciano was consolidated as the main seat of the lineage. The fortress, originally built by Napoleone Orsini in the 15th century, was partially restored and modernized by the Odescalchi, who added halls decorated with frescoes, gardens, and art collections. The castle, which dominates Lake Bracciano from a hill, is one of the best-preserved examples of Renaissance military architecture in Italy —and, since the purchase of 1696, a symbol of the rise of the Odescalchi from Alpine bankers to princes of Latium—.

The Odescalchi did not play a leading political role in 19th-century Rome —the era of the *Risorgimento* and Italian unification— but maintained their social position, their real estate holdings, and their title of Princes Odescalchi. Like the rest of the Black Nobility, they refused to recognize the Kingdom of Italy in 1870 and remained loyal to the Pope in the Vatican.

## 20th–21st Centuries — The Castello Orsini-Odescalchi: From Military Fortress to Tourist Destination and Wedding Venue

The Castello Orsini-Odescalchi of Bracciano experienced a remarkable transformation during the 20th and 21st centuries: from a private residence of a Black Nobility family, it became one of the most visited historical monuments in Latium and a wedding destination for international celebrities.

The castle has been open to the public since the second half of the 20th century. Its Renaissance halls —with original 15th-century frescoes, arms collections, furniture, and tapestries—, its medieval towers, and its panoramic gardens over the lake attract tens of thousands of tourists each year. The fortress has been used as a setting for film and television productions —including historical series and documentaries— and is one of the most photogenic medieval castles in Italy.

The moment of greatest international media exposure for the Castello Odescalchi occurred on **November 18, 2006**, when the American actors **Tom Cruise and Katie Holmes** celebrated their wedding at the castle. The wedding, which attracted the attention of media outlets from around the world, was held in a private ceremony in the castle chapel, followed by a reception in the Renaissance halls. The international press covered the event exhaustively, and the images of the castle —its crenellated towers, its frescoes, the lake in the background— went around the planet. For weeks, the Castello Odescalchi was the fashionable destination for international luxury tourism.

The Cruise-Holmes wedding, which ended in divorce in 2012, was a milestone in the history of the castle as a tourist destination. But, beyond the media anecdote, the episode illustrates the economic and social transformation of the Black Nobility in the 21st century: the families who for centuries based their wealth on land ownership and ecclesiastical benefices have turned their castles and palaces into tourist and cultural assets. The Castello Odescalchi, like the Palazzo Colonna, the Galleria Borghese, or the Palazzo Barberini, is today a heritage business that combines tourist revenues, event space rentals, and cultural management.

## 2026 — The Odescalchi Today: The Castle, the Title, and the Shadow of the Incorruptible Pope

In 2026, the Odescalchi remain one of the most singular families of the Black Nobility. Their singularity does not lie in the number of popes they gave to the Church —only one— nor in the extent of their properties —the Castello of Bracciano is their only iconic asset—, but in the nature of their Pope and in the moment when he governed.

Innocent XI was the only 17th-century Pope who systematically fought nepotism —rather than practicing it—, the only one who confronted Louis XIV at the height of French absolutism, and the only one who financed a decisive war against the Ottoman Empire at the very moment when Christian Europe was on the verge of falling to Islam. Had Vienna fallen in 1683, the history of Europe would have been radically different. And the man who prevented Vienna from falling —with his diplomacy, his money, and his moral authority— was an Odescalchi.

The current Odescalchi —Prince **Carlo Odescalchi** and his heirs— maintain ownership of the Castello Orsini-Odescalchi of Bracciano and the title of Princes Odescalchi. They manage the castle as a museum and event space, and participate in the cultural and social life of the Roman and Lombard aristocracy —they maintain ties with Como, their city of origin—. Their public profile is discreet: they do not need the media exposure of other families because they have the castle. And having a 15th-century castle over a volcanic lake is a form of public presence more powerful than any appearance in the press.

Few papal families can claim a legacy as tangible as an intact Renaissance castle. The Borghese have the Galleria Borghese —a state museum since 1902—. The Barberini have the Palazzo Barberini —also state-owned—. The Odescalchi have the Castello Orsini-Odescalchi —and they manage it themselves, without state intermediation—. And they rent it out for Hollywood star weddings. That is adaptation, survival, and, in a way, a very Italian form of historical continuity.

## The Odescalchi Pattern: The Reforming Pope Who Did Not Enrich His Family

The Odescalchi represent an inverse pattern to that of most papal families. Instead of a Pope who enriches his family through nepotism —the Barberini, the Borghese, the Chigi, the Pamphili, the Aldobrandini—, Innocent XI was a Pope who *impoverished* his family by refusing to practice nepotism. The fortune of the Odescalchi was built *after* the pontificate, through land purchases —the Duchy of Bracciano— and the prudent management of the patrimony accumulated by Livio Odescalchi.

This inversion of the nepotistic pattern is the key to the durability of the Odescalchi. The families that enriched themselves through nepotism —the Barberini, the Borghese— experienced a cycle of rapid accumulation, financial crisis, and sale of heritage to the state. The Odescalchi, by contrast, enriched themselves slowly, through the classic mechanisms of European aristocracy —marriages, land purchases— and preserved their castle intact. It is a historical irony: the Pope who did the least for his family is, in the long run, the one who left his family in the best patrimonial position.

The Castello Orsini-Odescalchi is not a state museum —like the Galleria Borghese—, nor a palace sold to the government —like the Palazzo Chigi—. It is a private property of the Odescalchi, managed by themselves, which generates income and keeps the surname alive. It is, in a way, the posthumous triumph of the Pope who refused to do what all the other popes did.
