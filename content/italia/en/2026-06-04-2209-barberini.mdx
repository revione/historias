# The Barberini — "What the Barbarians Did Not Do"

---

## 14th–16th Centuries — From Tuscan merchants to the pontifical court

The Barberini were not Romans. They were of Tuscan origin, coming from the small village of Barberino Val d'Elsa, in the Chianti hills between Florence and Siena. The family name itself —Barberini— derives from Barberino, and their coat of arms —three golden bees on a blue field— would become one of the most recognisable emblems of Baroque Rome.

During the 14th to 16th centuries, the Barberini were merchants of wool, cloth, and grain in Florence. They belonged to that Tuscan mercantile class that produced bankers, administrators, and bureaucrats for the Italian courts. They were not nobles: they were enriched bourgeois who rose through money and professional competence, not through sword or blood. The Italian historian Claudio Rendina, in *Le grandi famiglie di Roma* (2006), traces the ascent of the Barberini as a typical process of social mobility in Renaissance Tuscany: accumulate commercial capital, place oneself at the service of princes and popes, and, at the right moment, jump to the nobility.

The man who initiated the leap was **Francesco Barberini** (1528–1600), a cloth merchant who moved to Rome in the second half of the 16th century. Francesco accumulated a considerable fortune in the wool and grain trade, invested in urban properties, and placed his children in the pontifical administration. His brother **Maffeo Barberini** (1568–1644) —Francesco's nephew— was sent to study with the Jesuits at the Collegio Romano, the elite educational institution of the Counter-Reformation, where he acquired the classical training that would distinguish him as one of the most cultured popes of the Baroque.

Maffeo Barberini earned his doctorate in law from the University of Pisa in 1589, entered the Roman Curia under Clement VIII Aldobrandini, and was appointed cardinal in 1606 by Paul V Borghese. He served as apostolic nuncio in Paris and as legate in Bologna. He was an accomplished poet —he wrote verse in Latin and Italian, published in 1620 under the title *Poemata*— and a man of refined taste, a natural patron of artists and architects. When Pope Gregory XV died in July 1623, Maffeo Barberini presented himself to the conclave at the age of fifty-five with a reputation as a humanist, diplomat, and prince of the Church. On 6 August 1623, he was elected Pope. He took the name **Urban VIII**.

---

## 1623 — Maffeo Barberini becomes Urban VIII: the humanist on the throne

Urban VIII governed the Church for twenty-one years —the longest pontificate of the 17th century—, from 1623 until his death in July 1644. His pontificate coincided with the most destructive phase of the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), the war that devastated the Holy Roman Empire and reshaped the political map of Europe. Urban VIII, as Pope, faced dilemmas no pontiff had faced before: a continent in flames, a Catholic French monarchy allied with Protestants against the Catholic Habsburgs, and a papacy that could no longer impose its arbitration but only manage its decline as a temporal power.

The historian Leopold von Ranke, in his classic *Die römischen Päpste in den letzten vier Jahrhunderten* (1834–1836), describes Urban VIII as a Pope of "extraordinary personal culture but limited political capacity." He governed with a princely style: he loved poetry, music, architecture, and sculpture, and conceived the papacy as an absolute monarchy whose power was expressed as much in marble as in decrees. His patronage transformed Rome: during his pontificate, Gian Lorenzo Bernini —his personal protégé— became the most influential artist in Europe, and St Peter's Basilica received its most emblematic element: the baldachin.

But Urban VIII was also the Pope who tried Galileo. The Pope who drained the pontifical coffers in the War of Castro —a private war against an Italian noble family, the Farnese of Parma. The Pope whose family —the Barberini— accumulated so much wealth and provoked so much resentment that Rome coined a phrase that has lasted four centuries: *"Quod non fecerunt barbari, fecerunt Barberini"* — "What the barbarians did not do, the Barberini did."

---

## 1623 — Three cardinal nephews: the machine of Barberini nepotism

Scarcely elected Pope, Urban VIII deployed the most aggressive and systematic nepotism operation of the 17th century. In less than a year, he appointed two of his nephews as cardinals and placed a third as the secular head of the family. The three would dominate papal politics for the next two decades.

**Francesco Barberini** (1597–1679), the Pope's older nephew, was appointed cardinal in 1623 at the age of twenty-six. He was the intellectual of the family: educated at the Collegio Romano, doctor of law, polyglot, book collector, and founder of the Barberini Library —one of the most important of Baroque Rome, which today forms part of the Vatican Apostolic Library. Francesco assumed the office of Cardinal Nephew (*Cardinalis Nepos*), controlling papal diplomacy and correspondence with nuncios. He was, according to contemporaries, the most moderate of the Barberini: opposed to the War of Castro, favourable to reconciliation with France, and reluctant towards his brothers' extravagances. But moderate or not, he was a Barberini. And the money flowed into his house.

**Antonio Barberini** (1607–1671), the Pope's younger nephew, was appointed cardinal in 1628 at the age of twenty-one. He was the warrior of the family: he took command of the pontifical troops during the War of Castro, accumulated ecclesiastical benefices worth more than 130,000 scudi annually, and led a princely lifestyle that scandalised even a Rome accustomed to cardinalatial luxury. Antonio was the cardinal the Romans hated: arrogant, spendthrift, militaristic. The historian Ludwig von Pastor, generally indulgent with the popes, describes him as "the most problematic member of the Barberini family."

**Taddeo Barberini** (1603–1647), the third nephew, did not receive the cardinalate: he was named Prince of Palestrina and Prefect of Rome —the highest secular office of the pontifical administration. Taddeo was the lay prince, the dynastic heir, responsible for ensuring that the wealth accumulated during his uncle's pontificate would be transmitted to future generations. He married Anna Colonna, daughter of Prince Filippo Colonna —a matrimonial alliance that united the upstart Barberini with one of the oldest families of the Roman Black Nobility. The marriage was a geopolitical contract: the Barberini bought lineage; the Colonna bought access to the Pope.

Together, the three Barberini nephews received from Urban VIII a cascade of benefices, revenues, properties, and offices that made them, in less than a decade, one of the richest families in Europe.

## 1624–1633 — The Baldachin of St Peter's: Bernini and the bronze of the Pantheon

The most visible work of Urban VIII's pontificate was not a decree or a bull. It was the **Baldachin of St Peter's**, the gigantic gilded bronze canopy that rises over the high altar of the Vatican basilica, directly above the tomb of St Peter. Designed and sculpted by Gian Lorenzo Bernini between 1624 and 1633, the baldachin is one of the absolute masterpieces of the Baroque and the most recognisable symbol of papal Rome.

The baldachin measures twenty-eight metres in height —equivalent to an eight-storey building— and is supported by four twisted Solomonic bronze columns, decorated with laurel leaves, Barberini bees, and figures of angels. At the summit, four angels guard a globe crowned by a cross. The work required colossal quantities of bronze —more than 100,000 pounds, according to art historians' estimates.

And here enters the phrase that immortalised the Barberini in popular Roman resentment.

To obtain the necessary bronze, Urban VIII ordered the stripping of the beams of the portico of Agrippa's Pantheon —the best-preserved Roman temple of Antiquity, converted into the church of Santa Maria dei Martiri in the 7th century. The Pantheon had survived sixteen centuries of invasions, earthquakes, sackings, and neglect. Neither the Goths of Alaric, nor the Vandals of Genseric, nor the Saracens of the 9th century, nor the troops of the Sack of Rome of 1527 had touched its bronze structure. Urban VIII did.

The bronze of the Pantheon was melted down and reused for the baldachin and for the cannons of Castel Sant'Angelo —because Urban VIII also fortified Rome during the Thirty Years' War, fearing a Protestant invasion. The stripping operation was supervised by Bernini himself.

The Romans, who have always had a special talent for political satire, coined the phrase that has endured four centuries: *"Quod non fecerunt barbari, fecerunt Barberini"* — "What the barbarians did not do, the Barberini did." The phrase, first recorded by the Roman antiquarian Carlo Fea in the 18th century, became the public epitaph of the family and a permanent reminder that Rome never forgives those who damage its monuments.

## 1625–1633 — Palazzo Barberini: the palace of Bernini and Borromini

While the baldachin was being built in St Peter's, the Barberini were building their family palace on the Quirinal. The **Palazzo Barberini** is one of the masterpieces of civil Baroque architecture and one of the most important buildings of papal Rome.

The project began in 1625 on land bought from the Sforza family. The initial design was entrusted to **Carlo Maderno** —the architect of St Peter's façade—, who died in 1629. The direction of the work then passed to **Gian Lorenzo Bernini**, assisted by a young architect of Swiss-Italian origin called **Francesco Borromini**. The collaboration between Bernini and Borromini at the Palazzo Barberini was brief but intense —and legendarily conflictive. Borromini designed the helical staircase of the left wing, a masterpiece of spatial engineering without a single central support. Bernini designed the façade, the great central hall, and the main staircase. Both geniuses detested each other, and their rivalry —which definitively erupted in the following years, when Borromini was displaced by Bernini as papal favourite— would become one of the most celebrated artistic dramas in the history of art.

The Palazzo Barberini incorporated a revolutionary innovation: a great central hall with a ceiling fresco painted by **Pietro da Cortona** —the *Triumph of Divine Providence* (1633–1639)—, an allegorical apotheosis of the Barberini and Urban VIII covering a surface area of more than six hundred square metres. The Barberini bees —the family emblem— appear everywhere: on the columns, in the frescoes, in the stuccoes, in the tapestries. No one who entered the Palazzo Barberini could forget who governed Rome.

Today, the Palazzo Barberini houses the **Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica** —the National Gallery of Ancient Art—, one of the most important museums in Italy, with works by Raphael, Caravaggio, Titian, Holbein, and El Greco. The palace that the Barberini built to display their power is today a national museum. But the bees remain on the walls.

## 1633 — The trial of Galileo: Urban VIII versus science

On 22 June 1633, in the Dominican convent of Santa Maria sopra Minerva in Rome, **Galileo Galilei** knelt before the tribunal of the Inquisition and abjured the Copernican doctrine —the theory that the Earth revolves around the Sun and not the reverse. He was condemned to life imprisonment, immediately commuted to house arrest in his villa in Arcetri, near Florence. There he spent the last nine years of his life, blind and watched, until his death in 1642.

The trial of Galileo is one of the most controversial episodes in the history of the Church, and Urban VIII was the Pope who presided over it.

The story is more complex than the popular myth of "the Church versus science." Galileo and Maffeo Barberini had known each other personally for years. Barberini, as cardinal, had admired Galileo's work and had even written a poem in his honour. In 1623, when Barberini was elected Pope, Galileo travelled to Rome to congratulate him and was received six times in private audience. Urban VIII granted him permission to write about the Copernican system, on one condition: that he present it as a mathematical hypothesis, not as physical truth, and that he include the Pope's arguments on divine omnipotence —the argument that God, being omnipotent, could have created the universe in any way, and that humans could not limit His creative freedom with astronomical theories.

Galileo wrote the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632) and committed a fatal error. He placed the Pope's argument in the mouth of **Simplicio** —the foolish character of the work, whose name in Italian means "simpleton." Urban VIII felt personally betrayed and humiliated. The trial of 1633 was as much a matter of doctrine as of personal revenge.

The historian of science Pietro Redondi, in *Galileo eretico* (1983), proposed an even more polemical thesis: that Galileo was condemned not for Copernicanism —which was a secondary matter— but for his atomistic theory of matter, which conflicted with the doctrine of Eucharistic transubstantiation defined at Trent. According to Redondi, the trial of 1633 was a containment operation: Galileo was condemned for a lesser heresy —Copernicanism— to protect him from a more serious accusation —Eucharistic atomism. Redondi's thesis has been debated and partially rebutted by other historians, but the central fact is indisputable: Urban VIII was the Pope who humiliated Galileo.

The Catholic Church formally rehabilitated Galileo in 1992, under John Paul II. The Barberini were no longer there to answer.

## 1641–1644 — The War of Castro: the hole that drained the pontifical treasury

The War of Castro was the jewel of Barberini political incompetence. It was a private war, disguised as a pontifical military operation, that pitted the Barberini against the Farnese family —dukes of Parma and Piacenza— for control of the small Duchy of Castro, a Farnese fief in northern Latium, near Lake Bolsena.

The conflict began in 1641, when the Barberini —who already controlled the pontifical administration and aspired to acquire Castro for the family— pressured Urban VIII to declare war on the Farnese under the pretext that Duke Odoardo Farnese had failed to fulfil his feudal obligations to the Holy See. The reality was simpler: the Barberini wanted Castro, and the Pope was willing to use the pontifical armies to get it.

The war was a disaster. The pontifical troops, commanded by Cardinal Antonio Barberini —the warrior cardinal—, invaded the Duchy of Castro in 1642, sacked the city, destroyed the cathedral, and exiled the bishop. But the military campaign consumed astronomical sums: the pontifical coffers, which Urban VIII had inherited healthy from Gregory XV, were emptied. The Pope resorted to forced loans, new taxes on the subjects of the Papal States, and the sale of ecclesiastical offices to finance the war. The fiscal pressure provoked revolts in several cities. The cardinals who were enemies of the Barberini —and there were many— protested in the Consistory.

To Urban VIII's greater humiliation, the war ended in 1644 without the Barberini obtaining Castro. The duchy was restored to the Farnese through the mediation of France and Venice. The Barberini had spent a fortune, drained the pontifical treasury, provoked the hatred of the Roman nobility and of foreign powers, and had nothing to show for it.

When Urban VIII died on 29 July 1644, the pontifical treasury was empty. His nephews had to flee Rome —the people would have lynched them— and take refuge in France, under the protection of Cardinal Mazarin. The new Pope, Innocent X Pamphili, ordered an investigation into the Barberini's financial management, which resulted in the partial restitution of goods and the voluntary exile of the family for several years.

---

## 1644 — The flight from Rome: the price of limitless nepotism

The death of Urban VIII unleashed an explosion of fury against the Barberini. The new Pope, **Innocent X** (Giovanni Battista Pamphili, 1644–1655), had been an enemy of the Barberini during the previous pontificate, and his election was celebrated by the anti-Barberini faction of the College of Cardinals. One of Innocent X's first measures was to order a formal investigation into the enrichment of Urban VIII's nephews.

Cardinals Francesco and Antonio Barberini, together with Prince Taddeo, fled Rome in 1645 and went into exile in Paris, under the protection of Cardinal Giulio Mazarin —who had been nuncio in Paris and was an ally of the Barberini. The family's properties in Rome were temporarily embargoed. The palaces stood empty. The Barberini bees ceased to buzz in the Curia.

But the Barberini were not finished. They had connections. Mazarin pressured Innocent X to forgive them. In 1648, the Barberini were rehabilitated: the properties were returned, the cardinals returned to Rome, and the family recovered its position in the Roman aristocracy. They would never again have the omnipotent power of the years of Urban VIII —there would be no other Barberini Pope—, but they preserved their palaces, their lands, and their name.

## 17th–18th Centuries — The Barberini in the Black Nobility

After the death of Urban VIII and the brief persecution under Innocent X, the Barberini reintegrated into the Roman Black Nobility. They never again produced a Pope —no subsequent conclave elected a Barberini— but maintained their position through marriages with the other great papal families: the Colonna —Taddeo had already married Anna Colonna—, the Corsini, the Sacchetti, the Chigi.

During the 17th and 18th centuries, the Barberini managed their real estate and agricultural patrimony in Latium, maintained the Palazzo Barberini and the Villa Barberini in Castel Gandolfo, and occupied honorary offices at the pontifical court. They were now a family of consolidated aristocracy, not the aggressive upstarts of the years of Urban VIII. Time had smoothed the edges.

The main branch of the Barberini became extinct in the male line in 1738 with the death of Prince Francesco Barberini, but the surname survived through the Colonna di Sciarra branch, which inherited the Barberini name and patrimony. Today, the descendants of the Barberini bear the compound surname Barberini-Colonna.

---

## 19th–20th Centuries — The Barberini in contemporary Italy

During Italian unification (1860–1870), the Barberini —like the rest of the Black Nobility— refused to recognise the Kingdom of Italy and remained loyal to the Pope. The Palazzo Barberini, however, was sold to the Italian State in 1949 and converted into a public museum —the Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica—, in an operation reminiscent of the sale of the Galleria Borghese to the State in 1902.

The Barberini family survived the 20th century as all great papal families survived: transforming their territorial and political power into cultural and patrimonial capital. The Barberini name remains recognisable to any visitor to Rome: Piazza Barberini, Via Barberini, Palazzo Barberini, the baldachin of St Peter's with its golden bees —although tourists rarely know what those bees mean, the Barberini are there, buzzing in the centre of Christendom for four centuries.

---

## 2026 — The Barberini legacy: bees at the centre of St Peter's

In 2026, the Barberini are no longer a political power. But their mark on Rome is indelible. The baldachin of St Peter's still stands —restored in 2023–2024 on the occasion of the 2025 Jubilee—, and the Barberini bees continue to decorate its Solomonic columns. The Palazzo Barberini remains one of the most important museums in Italy. The phrase *"Quod non fecerunt barbari, fecerunt Barberini"* continues to be quoted in tourist guides, history books, and academic articles.

The Barberini did not endure like the Orsini —who have been at the summit of the Black Nobility for eight centuries— nor did they leave a legacy of universal patronage like the Borghese. But no other papal surname evokes so vividly the excess of Baroque nepotism. The Barberini are the family that arrived in Rome with cloth money, produced a humanist and poet Pope, built the baldachin with the bronze of the Pantheon, tried Galileo, emptied the pontifical treasury in a private war, and fled Rome pursued by popular fury. And four centuries later, their bees continue to fly at the centre of St Peter's.

That is power. Not the power measured in territories or cardinals. The power measured in the capacity to survive one's own excess.

---

## The Barberini paradox: nepotism, art, and ruin

The Barberini represent, better than any other papal family, the apogee and crisis of nepotism as a system of government. Urban VIII was not more corrupt than Paul V Borghese or Paul III Farnese —all Renaissance and Baroque popes practised nepotism. But Urban VIII took the system to a breaking point: he extracted so much from the Holy See for his family that the papacy was left on the verge of financial collapse.

His legacy is a paradox. The baldachin of St Peter's —built with bronze plundered from the Pantheon— is one of the masterpieces of Western sculpture. The Palazzo Barberini —paid for with ecclesiastical revenues extracted from all Christendom— is today a public museum housing some of the most important paintings in Europe. What the Barberini stole beautified Rome. What they accumulated is today universal heritage.

But the Romans' phrase remains the definitive verdict: *Quod non fecerunt barbari, fecerunt Barberini*. Neither the Goths, nor the Vandals, nor the Saracens dared to touch the Pantheon. The Barberini did. And that, in Rome, is not forgotten.
