# The Pallavicini — From the Holy Empire to the Banks of Genoa

---

## Before the Year 1000 — Origins in Liguria: The Obertenghi and the Feudal Dawn

The Pallavicini are one of the oldest noble families of Italy. Their origins go back to the March of Obertenga, the vast feudal territory that the Obertenghi —a dynasty of marquesses of Frankish-Lombard origin— governed in northern Italy during the 10th and 11th centuries. The Pallavicini descend from a branch of the Obertenghi that settled in eastern Liguria, between the present-day provinces of Genoa and La Spezia, and that took its name from the castle of Pallavicino, in the area of Pontremoli.

The first Pallavicini documented with certainty is **Oberto Pallavicini**, who appears in Genoese notarial sources around the year 1000. The family belonged to the feudal aristocracy of the March of eastern Liguria, a border territory between the Holy Roman Empire, the Republic of Genoa, and the nascent Italian communes. They were lords of castles, owners of lands, administrators of feudal justice in a world where power was measured in fortresses, not in money.

The Italian historian Pompeo Litta, in his monumental *Famiglie celebri italiane* (1819–1883), devotes an entire volume to the Pallavicini and traces their genealogy from Oberto to the 19th century. Litta documents that the Pallavicini were one of the few families of the Italian feudal nobility who managed to maintain their territorial power during the transition from early medieval feudalism to the world of the city-republics and principalities.

---

## 11th–13th Centuries — The Pallavicini State: Feudal Lords in the Po Valley

During the 11th to 13th centuries, the Pallavicini built what Italian historiography calls the **Pallavicini State** (*Stato Pallavicino*): a collection of fiefs, castles, and territories in the Po Valley, between the present-day provinces of Parma, Piacenza, and Cremona, which the family governed with a high degree of autonomy under the nominal authority of the Holy Roman Empire.

The Pallavicini were vassals of the Emperor, not of the Pope. That is a crucial distinction. While other families of the future Black Nobility —the Orsini, the Colonna, the Savelli— were barons of Latium and vassals of the Holy See, the Pallavicini belonged to the imperial sphere. They were Ghibellines. They supported the Emperor in the wars against the Papacy, against the Guelph cities of northern Italy, and against the Angevins in the south.

Marquess **Oberto Pallavicini** (1197–1269) was the greatest exponent of this Ghibelline policy. Oberto was one of the closest captains of Emperor Frederick II Hohenstaufen and, after the emperor's death in 1250, assumed the leadership of the Ghibelline party in northern Italy. He governed as imperial vicar in Cremona, Piacenza, and Pavia, and fought the Guelphs led by the Republic of Milan and the Republic of Genoa. The medieval chronicler Salimbene de Adam, in his *Chronicle* (1288), describes Oberto Pallavicini as "the most powerful man in Lombardy" in the 1260s.

The Pallavicini State reached its maximum extent under Oberto: it controlled a territory that stretched from the hills of Piacenza to the plains of Cremona, with dozens of castles, agricultural rents, forests, and toll rights over the Po River. But Oberto's death in 1269 and the definitive defeat of the Ghibellines by the Angevins at the battle of Tagliacozzo (1268) marked the beginning of the decline of the Pallavicini's territorial power in Lombardy.

---

## 13th–15th Centuries — Division and Dispersal: One Branch Becomes Genoese

After the Ghibelline defeat, the Pallavicini fragmented into multiple branches. Some remained in Lombardy as minor local lords; others moved to the Veneto, to Ferrara, and to Mantua. But the branch that would attain the greatest historical relevance was the one that moved to Genoa.

Genoa was, from the 13th to the 15th century, one of the most powerful maritime republics of the Mediterranean, alongside Venice and Pisa. The Pallavicini who settled in Genoa progressively abandoned feudal military activity and integrated into the mercantile and banking elite of the city. They went from being lords of castles to shipowners and financiers. The historian Edoardo Grendi, in *La repubblica aristocratica dei genovesi* (1987), studies this process of integration of the ancient Ligurian feudal families into the Genoese mercantile oligarchy and mentions the Pallavicini as a paradigmatic example of how the nobility of the sword transforms into nobility of money.

The Genoese Pallavicini participated in the trade of wool, silk, and spices, in international finance —lending money to kings and popes— and in the administration of the Republic of Genoa. They occupied positions in the city government and established branches of their businesses in Antwerp, Madrid, Naples, and Rome.

---

## 16th–17th Centuries — Princes of the Holy Empire and the Roman Branch

In the 16th century, the Pallavicini reached a status that few Italian families could claim. The emperor of the Holy Roman Empire granted them the title of **Princes of the Holy Empire** (*Reichsfürsten*), a dignity that placed them on the highest rung of European nobility. The title of imperial prince was hereditary and made its holders members of the high nobility of the Reich, with voting rights in the Imperial Diet until the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806.

In parallel, a branch of the Genoese Pallavicini moved to Rome in the 17th century and integrated into the Black Nobility via the Genoese route —the same followed by the Doria, the Spinola, and others—. **Lazzaro Pallavicini** (1602–1680), a member of the Genoese banking branch, entered the Roman Curia and was made a cardinal by Pope Innocent X Pamphili in 1669. Cardinal Lazzaro Pallavicini was a formidable financier: he used the capital accumulated by the family bank in Genoa to buy properties in Rome, finance the operations of the Curia, and establish the Pallavicini as one of the families of the Roman Black Nobility.

The historian Massimo Petrocchi, in *La politica finanziaria del papato nel Seicento* (1960), documents the role of the Genoese bankers —including the Pallavicini— in financing the papacy during the 17th century. Genoa was the financial center of the Hispanic Monarchy and of a good part of Italy, and the Genoese bankers were the natural lenders to the Holy See.

## 17th–18th Centuries — The Palazzo Pallavicini-Rospigliosi: A Palace Shared with History

The most visible symbol of the Pallavicini presence in Rome is the **Palazzo Pallavicini-Rospigliosi**, located on the Quirinal, one of the noblest areas of the city. The palace was originally built in 1605 by Cardinal **Scipione Borghese** —the cardinal nephew of Paul V— on the ruins of the Baths of Constantine, probably to a design by Carlo Maderno or Flaminio Ponzio. In 1611, Scipione Borghese completed the Casino dell'Aurora, a pavilion annexed to the palace with frescoes by Guido Reni —the famous *Aurora* (1614), one of the masterpieces of Baroque classicism—.

In 1704, the palace was acquired by the Pallavicini, who shared it with the Rospigliosi family —another papal family, descended from Clement IX— through a complex co-ownership agreement that reflects the intricate alliances of the Black Nobility. The building was renamed Palazzo Pallavicini-Rospigliosi. The **Galleria Pallavicini**, inside the palace, houses a collection of Baroque painting that includes works by Pietro da Cortona, Luca Giordano, Guercino, and Anton van Dyck, which the Pallavicini have preserved to this day.

The palace still belongs to the Pallavicini in 2026. It is one of the few palaces of the Black Nobility that remains in the hands of the original family and is open to the public by appointment.

## 18th–19th Centuries — The Pallavicini in the Europe of Revolutions

The Pallavicini survived the Napoleonic wars —which abolished the Holy Roman Empire in 1806— better than many other Italian noble families. Their diversification among Genoa, Lombardy, Parma, and Rome allowed them to preserve their patrimony even when their imperial titles lost political validity. The Parma branch served the Bourbon dukes and, subsequently, Marie Louise of Austria; the Genoese branch integrated into the Kingdom of Sardinia and then into the Kingdom of Italy; the Roman branch remained loyal to the Pope during the Roman Question.

The family preserved multiple titles: Prince of the Holy Empire, Marquess of various historical fiefs in Lombardy and Emilia, and patricians of Genoa. During the Risorgimento, the Pallavicini divided as so many other Italian families did: some supported unification, others remained loyal to the old regimes. The Pallavicini family of the Lombard branch actively participated in the government of the Kingdom of Italy: Marquess **Giorgio Pallavicini Trivulzio** (1796–1878) was a patriot of the Risorgimento, imprisoned by the Austrians in the Spielberg —the prison where Silvio Pellico was also held— and subsequently senator of the Kingdom of Italy.

---

## 20th–21st Centuries — The Pallavicini in Contemporary Italy

In the 20th century, the Pallavicini followed the same pattern as the rest of the Black Nobility: loss of formal political power, preservation of artistic and architectural heritage, and silent adaptation to the Italian Republic. The Roman branch kept the Palazzo Pallavicini-Rospigliosi and the Galleria Pallavicini as private properties, managed with discretion. The Genoese branch continued linked to the banking and industry of northern Italy.

In 2026, the Pallavicini continue to exist. The title of **Prince Pallavicini** is hereditary and is one of the few Italian princely titles still transmitted within the same family that originally received it from the Holy Empire. The family preserves properties in Rome —the Palazzo Pallavicini-Rospigliosi and the Galleria Pallavicini—, in Genoa, and in Lombardy, and maintains a discreet public profile, centered on the conservation of its artistic heritage and participation in the cultural and social life of the Italian nobility.

---

## The Pallavicini Pattern: From the Ghibelline Fief to the Genoese Bank

The Pallavicini represent a singular pattern in the history of Italian nobility: the transition from medieval feudal power to modern financial power without losing family identity. They began as lords of castles in the Liguria of the year 1000, became princes of the Holy Empire governing a state in the Po Valley, and one branch transformed into Genoese bankers who financed the Papacy and bought their place in the Roman aristocracy.

Unlike the Orsini and Colonna —Romans from time immemorial— or the Borghese —upstarts of a single pope—, the Pallavicini have a thousand years of uninterrupted history. They did not need a Pope of their own to enter the Black Nobility: the money of the Genoese bank and a banker cardinal sufficed. Their Roman palace, with the frescoes of Guido Reni in the Casino dell'Aurora, is the testimony of a trajectory that began in the hills of Liguria and ended on the Quirinal.
