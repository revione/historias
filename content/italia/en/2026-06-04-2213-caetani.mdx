# The Caetani — The Pope Who Thought He Was Greater Than God

---

## 12th Century — Origins in the Gaeta Countryside

The Caetani —or Gaetani, in the older Italian spelling— emerged in the 12th century in the region of Gaeta, on the coast of southern Latium, on the border between the Papal States and the Kingdom of Sicily. Their name derives from the city of Gaeta —in Latin, _Caieta_—, the fortified port that for centuries was the maritime key to southern Rome. The first documented Caetani is **Giacomo Caetani**, lord of lands in the Pontine countryside, who appears in a notarial deed of 1154 as a witness to a land transaction in the diocese of Gaeta.

The Caetani were not first-rank barons in the 12th century. They were local lords, landowners in the Pontine Marshes —the swampy plain south of Rome extending from Cisterna to Terracina—, an unhealthy and underdeveloped region that the great Roman families —Colonna, Orsini, Savelli— considered marginal. But that marginality was their advantage: while the Orsini and Colonna disputed the northern and southern approaches to Rome, the Caetani peacefully consolidated their dominion over a territory that no one coveted.

The family's rise changed radically with the entrance onto the scene of an exceptional jurist: Benedetto Caetani.

The historian Agostino Paravicini Bagliani, in his monumental _Bonifacio VIII_ (Einaudi, 2003), documents that Benedetto Caetani studied canon law at Bologna —the great factory of jurists of medieval Europe— and earned a doctorate in _utroque iure_ —civil and canon law— around 1260. His training was not that of a shepherd of souls: it was that of a technician of power. And that technician was about to reach the highest throne of Christendom.

---

## 1294 — The Abdication of Celestine V: The Jurist Who Fabricated a Vacancy

On July 5, 1294, the hermit Pietro da Morrone was elected Pope under the name Celestine V. He was a holy, ascetic man, without legal training, who governed the Church from an improvised cell in the castle of Fumone. His election was an institutional disaster: the hermit did not understand the curial machinery and was manipulated by Charles II of Anjou, King of Naples. The most capable cardinals of the Sacred College understood that Celestine V was untenable.

Among them: Benedetto Caetani.

It was Caetani who, according to the tradition documented by the contemporary chronicler Jacobus de Voragine in his _Chronica civitatis Ianuae_, advised Celestine V on the legal possibility of papal abdication. On December 13, 1294, Celestine V publicly read his resignation before the College of Cardinals —an unprecedented act in Church history—. Ten days later, on December 23, the conclave met in Naples. On December 24, 1294 —Christmas Eve—, Benedetto Caetani was elected Pope. He took the name Boniface VIII.

The speed of the election —less than twenty-four hours— reveals that the jurist Caetani had meticulously laid the groundwork. The historian T.S.R. Boase, in his classic _Boniface VIII_ (Constable, 1933), maintains that the election was "a masterpiece of canonical precision and political maneuver." Celestine V was confined in the castle of Fumone, where he died in 1296 —probably of natural causes, but the legend of murder by order of Boniface was never extinguished—.

The Caetani had just placed one of their own on the throne of Peter. And what followed was the most ambitious —and tragic— pontificate of the 13th century.

---

## 1302 — _Unam Sanctam_: The Most Radical Declaration in Papal History

On November 18, 1302, Boniface VIII promulgated the bull _Unam Sanctam_, the most extreme document of medieval papal theocracy. Its final sentence —one of the most famous in the history of Christianity— resonated like doctrinal thunder in all the courts of Europe:

_"Therefore we declare, affirm, and define that it is absolutely necessary for salvation that every human creature be subject to the Roman Pontiff."_

The bull was not a rhetorical outburst: it was the logical culmination of two centuries of theocratic doctrine, from Gregory VII to Innocent III. Boniface VIII maintained that the Pope possessed two swords —the spiritual and the temporal— and that all earthly power —of kings, emperors, princes— derived from papal power and was subordinate to it. The Pope was not one sovereign among others: he was the sovereign of sovereigns, the judge of all nations, the ultimate arbiter of political legitimacy.

The context was an open war with Philip IV the Fair, King of France. Philip IV had begun to tax the French clergy without papal permission to finance his war against England. Boniface VIII responded with a series of bulls —_Clericis laicos_ (1296), _Ausculta fili_ (1301)— that affirmed the fiscal immunity of the clergy and papal supremacy over royal power. Philip IV replied by convening the first Estates General of France —an assembly of nobles, clergy, and burghers— to legitimize his challenge to the Pope. The war between the most powerful king in Europe and the most ambitious pope in Christendom was declared.

Boniface VIII was preparing the excommunication of Philip IV. But the French king had other plans.

---

## 1297–1303 — The War with the Colonna and the Price of Arrogance

Boniface VIII's war with the Colonna was, in the words of the historian Ferdinand Gregorovius, "the fiercest civil war Rome had seen since the times of the Caesars." Cardinals Giacomo Colonna and Pietro Colonna —uncle and nephew— had questioned the legitimacy of Boniface VIII's election, arguing that Celestine V's abdication was canonically invalid. In May 1297, the Colonna published the _Manifesto of Lunghezza_, denouncing Boniface VIII as an antipope and calling for a council to depose him.

Boniface VIII responded with the bull of excommunication _In excelso throno_ against the entire Colonna family and launched a crusade against their territories. In the summer of 1298, the papal army, reinforced with troops from the Orsini and the Caetani themselves, besieged Palestrina —the main fortress of the Colonna—. After months of siege, the Colonna capitulated. Boniface VIII ordered the total destruction of Palestrina: the walls were demolished, the houses burned, and, according to tradition, the earth was plowed and salted —the Roman rite of _damnatio memoriae_—.

The Colonna fled to France. And there they found the ally they needed: Philip IV.

---

## 1303 — The Outrage of Anagni: The Pope Humiliated

On September 7, 1303, a contingent of French troops under the command of Guillaume de Nogaret —counselor to Philip IV— and Sciarra Colonna —the military leader of the exiled Colonna— stormed the papal palace of Anagni, the birthplace of Boniface VIII. The sixty-eight-year-old Pope dressed in the pontifical vestments, grasped the keys of Saint Peter, and waited seated on his throne.

Sciarra Colonna, according to contemporary chronicles, slapped the Pope with his iron gauntlet —the episode known as the _schiaffo di Anagni_, "the slap of Anagni"— while shouting: "Die, villain, die!" Boniface VIII was held prisoner for three days while Nogaret read a bill of accusations —heresy, simony, sodomy, idolatry— and demanded his immediate abdication.

The historian Agostino Paravicini Bagliani reconstructs the episode with documentary precision: the Pope was not physically tortured but was psychologically destroyed. The citizens of Anagni, who had initially collaborated with the French, eventually rose up against the invaders and freed the Pope. But Boniface VIII returned to Rome a broken man. He died on October 11, 1303, barely a month after the outrage.

His pontificate had been the most ambitious of the 13th century. His fall was the most humiliating. And the lesson was clear: not even the pretension of absolute supremacy over kings and emperors could survive a direct clash with the military power of a determined monarch.

---

## Early 14th Century — Dante Condemns Boniface VIII While He Was Still Alive

Boniface VIII died in 1303. But Dante Alighieri condemned him to Hell while he was still alive.

In canto XIX of the _Inferno_ —written around 1307, four years after the death of Boniface VIII but with the action set in 1300, three years earlier—, Dante places the Caetani Pope in the eighth circle of Hell, among the simoniacs, head down in a hole from which only his feet protrude, enveloped in flames. The condemnation is fierce: the poet calls Boniface VIII "the prince of the new Pharisees," accuses him of having prostituted the Church for gold, and presents him as the destroyer of Christendom.

Dante —Ghibelline, supporter of the Empire, political enemy of theocratic papacy— was the natural enemy of Boniface VIII. But what is extraordinary is not that he condemned him: it is that he did so while the Pope was still alive. In the internal chronology of the _Divine Comedy_, the action takes place in Holy Week of the year 1300. Boniface VIII then governed the Church. And Dante had already cast him into the flames of the eighth circle. No other Pope received such treatment while alive.

Dante's masterpiece ensured that Boniface VIII passed into posterity not as the defender of papal theocracy but as the archetype of the corrupt and arrogant ecclesiastic. The Caetani bore that stigma for centuries.

## 14th–16th Centuries — The Territorial Builders: Sermoneta, Ninfa, and the Pontine Marshes

After the death of Boniface VIII, the Caetani faced an existential problem: they had built their power around a Pope who had fallen into disgrace, humiliated and condemned by the greatest poet of Christendom. But the family did not retreat. It transformed.

The heirs of Boniface VIII —led by his nephew Pietro Caetani and his descendants— executed one of the most brilliant territorial operations of medieval Italy. Instead of disputing Rome with the Colonna and Orsini, the Caetani consolidated their dominion over the Pontine Marshes and the countryside of southern Latium. They built castles, drained marshes —as far as medieval technology permitted—, founded villages, and created a compact lordship that, at its height, extended from the Lepini Mountains to the Tyrrhenian Sea.

**Sermoneta.** The castle of Sermoneta, built on a rocky promontory dominating the Pontine plain, was the main fortress of the Caetani. Expanded by the family in the 13th and 14th centuries, the castle —with its cylindrical towers, walls, and forty-meter-high keep— is one of the best-preserved medieval fortresses in Latium. In 1503, Pope Alexander VI Borgia confiscated it for his daughter Lucrezia, but the Caetani recovered it after the fall of the Borgias. Today, the castle of Sermoneta still stands and belongs to the Fondazione Caetani.

**Ninfa.** The garden of Ninfa, at the foot of the Lepini Mountains, is one of the most beautiful places in Italy. But before it was a garden, it was a city. Ninfa was a prosperous medieval city under Caetani dominion, with walls, churches, a communal palace, and more than one hundred and fifty houses. It was sacked in 1382 during the wars of the Western Schism and never recovered. The Caetani abandoned the city, which remained in ruins. In the 20th century, Gelasio Caetani —Duke of Sermoneta— transformed the ruins into a world-famous botanical garden. Today, the garden of Ninfa —property of the Fondazione Roffredo Caetani— welcomes visitors from around the world and is one of the most extraordinary examples of how a medieval family turned an urbanistic defeat into an aesthetic triumph.

**Torre Astura.** The coastal tower of Astura, on the Tyrrhenian coast, was one of the maritime fortifications of the Caetani and controlled naval access to southern Latium. Today it is a ruin, but its strategic location reminds us that the Caetani not only dominated the marshes: they controlled the coast.

The historian Sandro Carocci, in _Baroni di Roma_ (École Française de Rome, 1993), documents that the Caetani, unlike the Orsini or Colonna, built their power on agriculture and the control of secondary trade routes —not on the military control of Rome—. They were lords of the marsh and of wheat, not of the city. And that strategy, less brilliant but more sustainable, allowed them to survive when other families fell.

## 14th–20th Centuries — The Caetani Cardinal Network

The Caetani did not produce another Pope after Boniface VIII. But they maintained a continuous presence in the Sacred College of Cardinals for six centuries.

Among the most notable Caetani cardinals:

- **Niccolò Caetani** (1526–1585), created cardinal by Paul III in 1536, was archbishop of Conza and governor of several cities of the Papal States. His rise —a Caetani in the circle of the Farnese— demonstrates the family's ability to integrate into the networks of other papal dynasties.
- **Luigi Caetani** (1595–1642), created cardinal by Urban VIII in 1629, was archbishop of Capua and actively participated in curial politics during the Thirty Years' War.
- **Onorato Caetani** (1742–1797), cardinal created by Clement XIV, was one of the most influential members of the Sacred College in the second half of the 18th century.
- **Michelangelo Caetani** (1804–1882), though not a cardinal —he was Duke of Sermoneta—, was an intellectual and political figure of the first order: Dante scholar, friend of Wagner and Franz Liszt, senator of the Kingdom of Italy after unification, and one of the men who represented the Caetani transition from the papal world to the Italian national world.

The historian Gaetano Moroni, in his _Dizionario di erudizione storico-ecclesiastica_ (Venice, 1840–1879, 103 vols.), documents the continuous presence of the Caetani in papal consistories from Boniface VIII to the 19th century.

---

## 19th Century — The Black and White Division: The Caetani and Italian Unification

The capture of Rome by the troops of the Kingdom of Italy on September 20, 1870, divided the Black Nobility into two camps: those who remained loyal to the Pope —closing the doors of their palaces, boycotting the Italian state— and those who accepted the new reality —collaborating with the Savoy monarchy—. The Caetani divided in a particularly dramatic way.

Onorato Caetani, Duke of Sermoneta, accepted a seat in the Senate of the Kingdom of Italy and collaborated with the new state. He was one of the Roman aristocrats who integrated the so-called "white nobility" —loyal to Italy— in contrast to the "black nobility" that boycotted the Italian state until 1929. But other members of the Caetani family remained faithful to the Pope and rejected all contact with the Kingdom of Italy. The Caetani division encapsulated the fracture of the entire Roman aristocracy after unification.

---

## 20th Century — Gelasio Caetani, Ninfa, and the Cultural Legacy

**Gelasio Caetani** (1877–1934), Duke of Sermoneta and mining engineer, was one of the most singular figures of the family in the 20th century. He fought in the First World War, served as Italian ambassador to the United States (1922–1925) during the early years of the fascist regime, and dedicated the last years of his life to transforming the ruins of Ninfa into the botanical garden that is now famous throughout the world. Gelasio Caetani represents the definitive transition of the Caetani: from feudal lords of Latium to stewards of a cultural and natural heritage of international reach.

His brother, **Roffredo Caetani** (1871–1961), was a composer and patron —a student of Franz Liszt— and the last Duke of Sermoneta of the direct line. With him, the ducal title of Sermoneta was extinguished in the princely male line, although the surname Caetani endures through collateral branches and through the Fondazione Roffredo Caetani and the Fondazione Camillo Caetani, which preserve the family archive, the castle of Sermoneta, and the garden of Ninfa.

---

## 21st Century — The Caetani Today

In 2026, the Caetani are no longer a papal dynasty with territorial power. No living cardinal today bears the surname Caetani. But the family —through their foundations and real estate holdings— continues to occupy a notable place in the Italian cultural and heritage landscape. The castle of Sermoneta, the garden of Ninfa, the family archive preserved in Rome: the Caetani today manage what they accumulated over eight hundred years. Their power is no longer political: it is cultural. And their name remains linked to that of the Pope who tried to subject all the kings of the earth and ended up humiliated, alone, and condemned by the greatest poet of the Italian language.
