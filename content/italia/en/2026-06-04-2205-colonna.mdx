# The Colonna — From Emperor to Pope, from Palestrina to the Vatican

---

## 11th Century — The Counts of Tusculum and the origin of the Colonna

The Colonna claim descent from the Counts of Tusculum —Theophylacti in the Latin sources—, the family that dominated the papacy during the 10th and early 11th centuries. The Tuscolani placed seven popes on the throne of St Peter between 904 and 1049, including the infamous John XII —deposed for immorality in 963— and the reformer Benedict VIII. When the Tuscolan dynasty lost control of the papacy after the death of Benedict IX in 1055, their descendants fell back to their fiefs in the Alban Hills, south of Rome. From that retreat were born the Colonna.

The name comes from the castle of Colonna, a fortress built on the ruins of ancient Labicum, about twenty kilometres southeast of Rome, on the Via Labicana. The first documented member of the family with the surname Colonna is **Pietro de Columna**, who appears in a notarial document of 1101 as a witness to a land transaction in the district of Tusculum. By then, the Colonna already possessed castles in Palestrina —ancient Praeneste—, Genazzano, Paliano, and Zagarolo. The historian Sandro Carocci, in _Baroni di Roma_ (1993), calculates that the territorial patrimony of the Colonna in the 12th century spanned more than eight hundred square kilometres of southern Latium, including control of the Via Casilina —the route to Naples— and the passes of the Prenestine Mountains.

Unlike the Orsini —who built their power from within the Curia—, the Colonna built it from the outside. They were rural barons with a feudal mentality: their power emanated from the land, from the castles, and from the capacity to mobilise private armies. Rome was, for them, an occasional battlefield; the Alban Hills were their permanent base.

---

## 13th Century — The Colonna, Ghibelline leaders in papal Rome

While the Orsini aligned themselves with the Pope and led the Guelph faction, the Colonna became the standard-bearers of Roman Ghibellinism: the Emperor's party. The position was seemingly contradictory —being pro-imperial in the Pope's city— but had a strategic logic: the Emperor was the only power capable of countering the power of Popes who favoured the Orsini.

The English chronicler Matthew Paris, in his _Chronica Majora_ (c. 1250), described the Colonna as "men of iron who bow to no one." The phrase captured the essence of the family: unlike the Orsini, who negotiated and inserted themselves into the curial structure, the Colonna openly defied the Pope when their territorial interests were threatened.

During the pontificate of Innocent III (1198-1216), the Colonna maintained a tense neutrality. Innocent III —Lotario dei Conti di Segni— was the most powerful pope of the Middle Ages and tolerated no challenges to his authority. But the Colonna controlled the southern routes and could cut off Rome's supplies if provoked. Innocent III opted for a strategy of containment: not attacking them directly but strengthening their Orsini rivals. The pattern would repeat for centuries: each time a Pope strengthened the Orsini, the Colonna tightened their links with the Empire.

The historian Ferdinand Gregorovius, in volume V of his _Geschichte der Stadt Rom im Mittelalter_ (1862), documents how the Colonna cardinals formed, around 1250, an opposition bloc within the Sacred College that systematically voted against papal candidates supported by the Orsini. The schism between the two families was not only military: it was institutional. Each conclave was a battlefield between two factions representing irreconcilable visions of power —papal supremacy (Orsini) versus imperial sovereignty (Colonna).

## 1288–1298 — The war with Boniface VIII: Palestrina razed

The most brutal conflict in Colonna history was not against the Orsini but against a Pope from the Caetani family: Boniface VIII.

Boniface VIII (Benedetto Caetani, pope from 1294 to 1303) was a formidable jurist and a ruthless politician. His pontificate marked the zenith of the medieval theocratic claim —the bull _Unam Sanctam_ (1302) declared that every human creature must submit to the Pope to be saved—, but his most persistent enemies were not kings or emperors: they were the Colonna.

The rupture began in 1288. Cardinals Giacomo Colonna and Pietro Colonna —uncle and nephew— were part of the Sacred College. They were two of the richest and most influential cardinals in Rome. When Boniface VIII ascended the papal throne in 1294 after the abdication of Celestine V —whose resignation he himself had orchestrated—, the Colonna began to question the legitimacy of his election. They argued that Celestine V's abdication was invalid and that Boniface VIII was therefore an antipope.

In May 1297, the Colonna cardinals published the so-called _Manifesto of Lunghezza_ —drafted at the Colonna castle of Lunghezza, east of Rome—, a document declaring Boniface VIII's pontificate illegitimate and calling a council to depose him. It was an act of open war. Boniface VIII responded with a bull of excommunication —_In excelso throno_— against the entire Colonna family and launched a crusade against their territories.

In the summer of 1298, the papal army, reinforced with troops from the Orsini and the Caetani, besieged Palestrina —the principal fortress of the Colonna, built over the temple of Fortuna Primigenia of ancient Praeneste—. After months of siege, the Colonna capitulated in September 1298. Boniface VIII demanded unconditional surrender and the total destruction of Palestrina. The chronicles describe scenes of systematic devastation: the papal soldiers razed the walls, demolished the towers, burned the houses, and, according to tradition, ploughed the land and spread salt over it —the Roman ritual of _damnatio memoriae_ applied to an entire city.

The Colonna were exiled. Their goods confiscated and distributed among the Orsini and the Caetani. But Boniface VIII made a mistake: he humiliated the Colonna without exterminating them. The survivors fled to France, where they took refuge at the court of Philip IV the Fair —the king who, a few years later, would become Boniface VIII's worst enemy.

In 1303, the Colonna had their revenge. Sciarra Colonna —one of the family's military leaders— participated in the episode known as the _Outrage of Anagni_: on 7 September 1303, French troops under Guillaume de Nogaret, counsellor to Philip IV, burst into the papal palace of Anagni with the support of Sciarra Colonna and his men. They slapped Boniface VIII and held him prisoner for three days. Although the Pope was freed by the citizens of Anagni, he died a month later, on 11 October 1303, psychologically destroyed. The Colonna revenge was complete.

## 1417 — Martin V: the Colonna Pope who ended the Western Schism

The Western Schism (1378-1417) was the worst crisis of authority in the history of the Church. For nearly forty years, two —and later three— rival popes simultaneously claimed legitimacy: one in Rome, another in Avignon, and a third elected by the Council of Pisa in 1409. The nations of Europe divided into rival obediences. Latin Christendom fractured.

The Council of Constance (1414-1418), convened by Emperor Sigismund of Luxembourg, set out to resolve the schism. The conciliar fathers deposed the three disputed popes —John XXIII, Benedict XIII, and Gregory XII— and, on 11 November 1417, the conclave gathered in the cathedral of Constance elected a new Pope. He was Oddo Colonna, cardinal deacon of San Giorgio in Velabro.

Oddone Colonna —who took the name Martin V in honour of St Martin of Tours, whose feast was celebrated on the day of his election— was a member of the Colonna branch of Genazzano. He was forty-eight years old. He had been created cardinal by Innocent VII in 1405 and had served as papal legate in several European courts. He was a natural diplomat, a man of consensus who represented what the Church needed: a figure who could unite the warring factions without belonging fully to any of them.

Martin V governed the Church for fourteen years (1417-1431) and achieved what seemed impossible: reunifying Latin Christendom under a single Pope. He dissolved the remnants of the rival obediences, negotiated with the kingdoms that still hesitated, and returned the papal see to Rome —since 1309, the popes had resided in Avignon. His entry into Rome on 28 September 1420 was an event of enormous symbolic dimensions: the Pope was returning to the eternal city after more than a century of absence.

But Martin V was, above all, a Colonna. During his pontificate, the family experienced an unprecedented territorial expansion. The Colonna Pope returned to the family the fiefs confiscated by Boniface VIII —Palestrina, Genazzano, Paliano, Zagarolo— and added new possessions. The Colonna went from being a powerful baronial family to being the dominant territorial power in southern Latium. The Pope's nephew, Prospero Colonna, was named Prince of Salerno. Another nephew, Antonio Colonna, received the Duchy of Amalfi.

The historian Peter Partner, in _The Lands of St. Peter_ (1972), describes the pontificate of Martin V as "the restoration of Colonna power with the Church's money." But he also acknowledges that this restoration was, in a sense, necessary: to govern Rome and the Papal States, Martin V needed a solid territorial base. The family was that base.

Martin V died on 20 February 1431. He was the only Colonna Pope in history. But the family's influence in the Vatican did not end with his death: a new phase was just beginning.

## 15th Century — The network of fiefs: Palestrina, Genazzano, Paliano and the Colonna territorial empire

The pontificate of Martin V transformed the Colonna from rural barons into territorial lords with a domain comparable to that of a small sovereign state. The family's fiefs in southern Latium formed a continuous territorial block that spanned the Prenestine Mountains and the Lepini Mountains, from the gates of Rome to the Abruzzi.

**Palestrina.** Rebuilt by Martin V after the destruction by Boniface VIII, Palestrina once again became the principal fortress of the Colonna. The baronial palace, built on the terraces of the ancient sanctuary of Fortuna Primigenia —one of the largest religious complexes of the Roman world—, combined the solidity of a medieval fortress with the magnificence of a Renaissance residence. The Colonna hired some of the best architects and artists of the era to decorate their chambers with frescoes glorifying the family's history.

**Genazzano.** The castle of Genazzano, about fifty kilometres southeast of Rome, was the fief of the main branch of the Colonna. It was here that Martin V was born. The castle, situated atop a rocky promontory dominating the valley of the river Sacco, was impregnable. It still stands today and, although partially ruined, preserves the 15th-century frescoes commissioned by the family.

**Paliano.** The fortress of Paliano, south of Palestrina, controlled the border between the Papal States and the Kingdom of Naples. In the 16th century, the Colonna transformed Paliano into a duchy and converted the medieval fortress into a Renaissance palace. The Duchy of Paliano remains one of the family's principal titles.

**Zagarolo.** The castle of Zagarolo, east of Rome, was acquired by the Colonna in the 12th century and expanded in the 16th with a ducal palace and gardens that rivalled those of the great Roman villas. Today, the Palazzo Rospigliosi of Zagarolo —heir of the Colonna— houses a museum.

The geography of Colonna power was not random. Each fief controlled a strategic route: Palestrina dominated the Via Prenestina; Genazzano watched over the Via Casilina; Paliano blocked the passage towards the Kingdom of Naples; Zagarolo closed the eastern access to Rome. Together, the Colonna could isolate Rome from its supply sources in the south within hours. It was a power based on physical geography as much as on dynasty.

## 1492–1547 — Vittoria Colonna: the poet who spoke as an equal with Michelangelo

Vittoria Colonna (1492-1547), Marchioness of Pescara, is the most singular figure of the family and one of the most important women of the European Renaissance. Her poetic work, her intellectual circle, and her friendship with Michelangelo place her at the centre of the cultural and spiritual life of the 16th century.

Born in the castle of Marino, in the Alban Hills, in April 1492, Vittoria was the daughter of Fabrizio Colonna —condottiero in the service of the Kingdom of Naples— and Agnese di Montefeltro, daughter of the Duke of Urbino. Her lineage united the military ferocity of the Colonna with the humanistic refinement of the court of Urbino, one of the most cultured in Italy. At the age of three she was betrothed to Fernando de Ávalos —future Marquis of Pescara—, a young Neapolitan nobleman of Spanish origin whom she married in 1509, at the age of seventeen.

The marriage was brief and tragic. Fernando de Ávalos was one of the most brilliant commanders of the imperial army of Charles V. He fought at the battle of Ravenna (1512), where he was captured, and at the battle of Pavia (1525), where he defeated and captured King Francis I of France. But he died in December 1525 from wounds received in combat, leaving Vittoria a widow at thirty-three. Childless, she retreated first to the convent of San Silvestro in Capite in Rome and then to Viterbo, where she joined the circles of spiritual reform known as the _spirituali_ —a current of religious renewal within the Church that sought reconciliation with the Protestants through the theology of grace.

It was during this period of retreat that Vittoria Colonna produced her most important poetic work: the _Rime amorose_ —dedicated to the memory of her husband— and, above all, the _Rime spirituali_, a collection of more than a hundred sonnets of profound theological meditation. The historian Abigail Brundin, in _Vittoria Colonna and the Spiritual Poetics of the Italian Reformation_ (2008), argues that Vittoria's spiritual sonnets were not mere devotional exercises: they were theological interventions in the debate on grace, faith, and the reform of the Church.

The friendship between Vittoria Colonna and Michelangelo Buonarroti is one of the most documented and fascinating episodes of the Renaissance. They met around 1536, when Michelangelo was sixty-one and Vittoria forty-four. The artist dedicated drawings to her —including a celebrated _Pietà_— and several sonnets. Francisco de Holanda, the Portuguese painter who visited Michelangelo in Rome, left in his _Diálogos de Roma_ (1548) a direct testimony of the relationship: Vittoria Colonna was one of the few people with whom Michelangelo spoke as an equal, and the only woman who influenced his spiritual thought.

Vittoria died in Rome in February 1547. Michelangelo was with her in her final days. "Nothing pains me more than not having kissed her forehead," he wrote later in a letter. The Colonna poet, who had been born into a family of warriors, left a legacy of words that is still studied in universities as one of the pinnacles of Italian Renaissance poetry.

## 1571 — Marcantonio Colonna and the Battle of Lepanto

On 7 October 1571, the fleet of the Holy League —an alliance of Spain, Venice, and the Papal States— defeated the Ottoman armada in the Gulf of Lepanto, off the western coast of Greece. It was the largest naval battle of the 16th century: more than four hundred galleys and nearly two hundred thousand men faced each other in a single day that halted Turkish expansion in the western Mediterranean.

At the head of the papal fleet —a contingent of twelve galleys— was **Marcantonio Colonna** (1535-1584), Duke of Paliano and Viceroy of Sicily, appointed captain general of the pontifical squadron by Pope Pius V. Marcantonio was an experienced soldier who had fought the Turks in the Mediterranean since his youth.

The naval historian Roger Crowley, in _Empires of the Sea_ (2008), reconstructs Marcantonio's participation at Lepanto in detail. The papal fleet was positioned in the centre of the Christian formation, alongside the galleys of Venice and Spain. Marcantonio Colonna's flagship, the _Capitana_, fought directly against the admiral ship of Ali Pasha —the Ottoman commander— and participated in the boarding that culminated in the Turkish admiral's death and the capture of his standard.

After the victory, Marcantonio Colonna was received in Rome with a triumph of imperial dimensions —a ceremony that evoked the parades of ancient Roman generals. Pius V, who had spent the day of the battle praying the rosary in the basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore, attributed the victory to the intercession of the Virgin and instituted the feast of Our Lady of Victories, later transformed into Our Lady of the Rosary.

Marcantonio Colonna was immortalised in the frescoes of the Galleria Colonna of the family palace in Rome. His statue, together with that of other commanders of Lepanto, adorns the basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore. The victory at Lepanto not only stopped the Turks: it consolidated the military prestige of the Colonna as defenders of Christendom —an ironic counterpoint for a family that, for centuries, had fought the Pope.

## 16th–18th Centuries — From barons to princes: the transformation of the Colonna

Throughout the 16th and 17th centuries, the Colonna completed a transformation that had already begun with Martin V: from territorial barons to princes of the papal court. The family maintained its fiefs and castles, but its centre of gravity shifted to the family palace in Rome.

The **Palazzo Colonna**, in Piazza dei Santi Apostoli, in the very centre of Rome, is one of the largest and most sumptuous palatial complexes in the city. Its construction began in the 14th century on the remains of an ancient Roman temple, but the current form dates from the 17th and 18th centuries, when the family commissioned some of the best architects and artists of the era —including Gian Lorenzo Bernini and Carlo Fontana— to expand and decorate the palace. The **Galleria Colonna** is its jewel: a hall seventy-six metres long, with a vault decorated with frescoes celebrating the glory of the family —including the battle of Lepanto— and a painting collection that includes works by Tintoretto, Veronese, Carracci, and Guercino. The Colonna art collection is one of the most important in private hands in Europe.

The Colonna hold, together with the Orsini, the hereditary dignity of **Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne** (_Princeps Assistens Solio_), one of the oldest and most prestigious offices of the papal court. The dignity, created by Pius IV in 1560, places the Colonna prince at the right hand of the papal throne during the great liturgical ceremonies. The office was not —nor is— merely ceremonial: it is a recognition of the Colonna's unique position within the papal nobility.

The family also expanded into southern Italy. Through marriages and acquisitions, the Colonna obtained titles and fiefs in the Kingdom of Naples and in Sicily, including the Principality of Avella, the Duchy of Tagliacozzo, and the Marquisate of Patrica. This southward expansion completed the transformation of the Colonna from a Roman dynasty to a pan-European lineage.

## 19th Century — The Black Nobility and loyalty to the imprisoned Pope

When the troops of the Kingdom of Italy took Rome on 20 September 1870, the Colonna —like the Orsini and the rest of the Black Nobility— closed the doors of their palace as a sign of mourning and protest. They did not recognise the Kingdom of Italy. For fifty-nine years, until the signing of the Lateran Pacts in 1929, the Colonna maintained a silent boycott of the Italian state: they did not accept public office, did not attend official events at the Quirinal, and behaved as subjects of the Pope in a state they considered usurper.

But the Colonna, unlike other families of the Black Nobility, found a pragmatic path of adaptation. The Palazzo Colonna opened its doors to the public —on certain dates and with restricted access—, becoming one of the most visited private museums in Rome. The Galleria Colonna generated revenue that allowed the palace to be maintained without depending on papal favour. The Colonna, pragmatic Ghibellines, survived the end of the Papal States better than many Guelph families.

---

## 20th Century — The Colonna in the Italian Republic

After the signing of the Lateran Pacts in 1929 and the fall of the Italian monarchy in 1946, the republican constitution abolished noble titles. But, as in the case of the Orsini, the abolition did not touch the true wealth of the Colonna: the land, the palaces, and influence in the Vatican.

The **Palazzo Colonna** remains the property of the family. The Galleria Colonna opens to the public on Saturday mornings —with entrance on Via della Pilotta— and is one of the best-kept secrets of Roman tourism: less known than the Vatican Museums or the Galleria Borghese, but of comparable artistic wealth. Access to the palace is through an arch that connects directly with the inner garden, and visitors walk through the same halls that popes, emperors, and kings have trodden for six centuries.

The current head of the Colonna family is **Don Prospero Colonna** —Prince of Avella, Prince of Paliano, Duke of Tagliacozzo—, who holds the dignity of Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne. The family maintains its presence in Roman and Vatican aristocratic circles, participates in pontifical ceremonies, and continues to manage the artistic and real estate heritage of the dynasty.

---

## The Colonna paradox

The Colonna occupy a unique position in the history of the Black Nobility: they are the only family that fought the Pope for centuries —as leaders of the Ghibelline, pro-imperial party— and that, at the same time, gave the Church the Pope who resolved its worst institutional crisis —Martin V and the end of the Western Schism. They are the only family that was excommunicated en bloc and whose fiefs were razed by a Pope —Boniface VIII at Palestrina—, only to return, rebuild, and place themselves at the right hand of the papal throne as Prince Assistants. They are the only family that produced the greatest poet of the Italian Renaissance —Vittoria Colonna— and the admiral who led the Christian fleet at Lepanto —Marcantonio Colonna.

In the words of the British historian David Abulafia, in _The Great Sea: A Human History of the Mediterranean_ (2011): _"The Colonna represent the continuity of oligarchic power in Italy like no other family. They were there before the Papal States and are still there after them."_
