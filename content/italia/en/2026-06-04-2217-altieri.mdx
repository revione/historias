# The Altieri — An 80-Year-Old Pope to Govern 6

---

## 15th–16th Centuries — The Altieri: Roman Patricians Without a Pope

The Altieri were Romans by origin, belonging to the urban patriciate of the city, not to the feudal aristocracy of the Orsini and Colonna. The surname Altieri appears in the records of Rome from the 15th century, linked to municipal administration, the grain trade, and civic magistracies. They were a respectable family, but of second rank: they had modest palaces, lands in the Roman countryside, and some members in the diocesan clergy, but no cardinal, no Pope, and no feudal title.

During the 15th and 16th centuries, the Altieri remained on the periphery of papal power, watching how other families —the Farnese, the Borghese, the Barberini, the Pamphili— took turns on the throne of Peter and turned their nephews into princes and cardinals. The family produced jurists, merchants, and priests, but not leading figures in the Curia. They were one of those Roman families that the historian Peter Partner, in *The Lands of St. Peter* (1972), describes as "the civic nobility of the second rank that orbited around the pontifical court without ever entering its core."

The man who would change the destiny of the Altieri was born in Rome on July 13, 1590. His name was Emilio. And his rise was, to a large extent, an accident: the product of a conclave that could not agree on any strong candidate.

---

## 1590–1670 — Emilio Altieri: Eighty Years Waiting for the Throne

Emilio Altieri had a long, discreet, and gray ecclesiastical career —exactly the type of career that does not lead to the papacy except in exceptional circumstances—. Doctor of civil and canon law from the University of La Sapienza, he entered the Curia under the pontificate of Urban VIII Barberini and served as consistorial advocate, judge of the Tribunal of the Rota, and secretary of several Roman congregations. He was not a brilliant diplomat —he was never sent as nuncio to foreign courts—, nor an exceptional administrator —he did not modernize any dicastery—, nor an outstanding intellectual —he wrote neither theological treatises nor poetry—. He was, simply, a competent, discreet, and long-lived ecclesiastical functionary.

In 1627, Urban VIII appointed him bishop of Camerino, a small diocese in the Marche. Altieri governed Camerino for more than three decades with the same discretion with which he had served in the Curia: without scandals, without innovations, without enemies, and without admirers. In 1666, Alexander VII Chigi called him back to Rome and appointed him prefect of the Consulta —the supreme tribunal of the Papal States—. And in November 1669, Clement IX Rospigliosi, on his deathbed, created him a cardinal. Altieri was seventy-nine years old and had been a cardinal for barely five months when Clement IX died on December 9, 1669.

The conclave that followed the death of Clement IX was one of the longest and most blocked of the 17th century. The cardinal factions —the French, the Spanish, the cardinal nephews of previous pontificates— could not agree on any candidate. For four months, the cardinals voted over and over again without reaching the two-thirds majority. The conclave became a war of vetoes: France vetoed pro-Spanish candidates, Spain vetoed pro-French candidates, and the cardinal nephews —Barberini, Chigi, Pamphili— blocked anyone who threatened their interests.

On April 29, 1670, exhausted after four months of deliberations, the cardinals resorted to the classic expedient of blocked conclaves: electing a compromise candidate, an elderly and seemingly harmless cardinal who would weaken no faction and could not govern for long. Emilio Altieri met all those conditions: he was eighty years old, had been a cardinal for five months, lacked enemies —because he had never had power— and lacked allies —for the same reason—. No one feared him. No one coveted him. He was perfect.

When the cardinal dean communicated the result of the vote, Altieri resisted. "I am too old —he replied—. I cannot carry this burden." But the cardinals insisted. Altieri accepted and took the name **Clement X** —in honor of Clement IX, the Pope who had created him a cardinal—. He was eighty years old, the most advanced age of any Pope elected up to that point. And the Romans, who knew how to read the signs, immediately understood what it meant: a brief pontificate and a family that would try to make the most of it.

## 1670 — The Paluzzo Solution: The Cardinal Nephew Who Was Not a Nephew

Clement X, newly elected, faced a problem: he was an octogenarian, had no experience of government, and lacked a papal family to lean on. The Altieri, as a clan, were a second-rank Roman family without cardinals, without princes, and without experience in the handling of papal power. But Clement X had a nephew —an adopted nephew, to be precise— who was willing to fill that void.

**Paluzzo Paluzzi Altieri degli Albertoni** (1623–1698) was not an Altieri by birth. His original surname was Paluzzi Albertoni: he came from a noble family from the Marche region that had connected with the Altieri through marriage in the previous generation. But in 1669, shortly before the conclave, Cardinal Emilio Altieri had legally adopted Paluzzo, incorporating him into the family and giving him the surname Altieri. When Emilio became Clement X, Paluzzo was ready.

Three days after the election, Clement X made Paluzzo a cardinal. The new cardinal —who came to be called **Paluzzo Paluzzi Altieri degli Albertoni**— immediately assumed the office of Cardinal Nephew and became the true ruler of the Church during the six years of his adoptive uncle's pontificate. Clement X, old and frail, delegated to him all the management of state affairs: diplomacy, finances, ecclesiastical appointments, relations with European powers, and the administration of the Papal States.

The historian Ludwig von Pastor, in volume XXXI of his *History of the Popes*, describes Paluzzo as "a man of great administrative capacity and unlimited ambition." Under his direction, the pontificate of Clement X became an operation of wealth extraction: in six years, Paluzzo amassed an immense fortune, built the Palazzo Altieri, bought lands in the Roman countryside, and transformed the Altieri from second-rank Roman patricians into one of the richest houses of the Black Nobility.

---

## 1670–1676 — Six Years of Extraction: The Pontificate as a Window of Opportunity

The pontificate of Clement X —six years, from 1670 to 1676— was a pontificate almost entirely managed by Paluzzo Altieri. Clement X, an octogenarian, delegated the functions of government to his adopted nephew while he devoted himself to liturgical ceremonies, canonizations, and protocolary acts. The Church was governed by Paluzzo; the Pope simply presided.

The Cardinal Nephew deployed an operation of wealth accumulation that, while not reaching the levels of the Barberini under Urban VIII —who practically emptied the pontifical treasury—, was notable for its efficiency and speed. In six years, Paluzzo:

- Accumulated ecclesiastical benefices —rents from dioceses, abbeys, and monasteries— at an estimated value exceeding one hundred thousand scudi annually, one of the highest cardinal incomes in 17th-century Rome.
- Acquired lands and fiefs in the Roman countryside and in the Marche, including the fiefs of Monterotondo and Vejano.
- Built the **Palazzo Altieri** in Piazza del Gesù, one of the largest aristocratic palaces in Rome, which remains the principal seat of the family.
- Controlled ecclesiastical and diplomatic appointments, placing allies in nunciatures and key dioceses.
- Managed diplomatic correspondence with the European courts —France, Spain, the Holy Roman Empire—, though without the energy or independence of an Innocent XI or an Alexander VII.

The pontificate of Clement X was, from the standpoint of international politics, mediocre. There were no great crises nor great triumphs. The conflict with Louis XIV over the régale was already latent, but would erupt under Innocent XI —the successor—. The Ottoman Empire was advancing toward Vienna, but the Holy League would not be formed until the following pontificate. Clement X simply administered the Church while Paluzzo Altieri administered his fortune.

Clement X's most famous canonization was that of **Saint Rose of Lima** in 1671 —the first saint of the Americas, an event of great resonance in the Hispanic world—. But even that canonization was managed by Paluzzo, who used the event to reinforce diplomatic relations with the Spanish monarchy.

Clement X died on July 22, 1676, at the age of eighty-six —the most advanced age of any Pope up to that point—. His pontificate had lasted exactly six years, three months, and twenty-three days. Cardinal Paluzzo Altieri, who had governed de facto during the entire pontificate, survived his adoptive uncle by twenty-two years —he died in 1698— and continued to accumulate influence in the Curia, although he was never elected Pope: subsequent conclaves were not willing to crown another greedy cardinal nephew.

## 1670–1676 — The Palazzo Altieri: The Altieri Footprint in Piazza del Gesù

The **Palazzo Altieri**, located in Piazza del Gesù, in the very heart of Rome, is the most visible architectural legacy of the pontificate of Clement X and of the building ambition of Paluzzo Altieri. It is not as famous a palace as the Palazzo Farnese —seat of the French embassy— or the Palazzo Barberini —the National Gallery of Ancient Art—, but it is one of the most imposing in the city and one of the most notable examples of late Baroque civil architecture in Rome.

The palace was built on a preexisting building that the Altieri had owned since the 16th century. The renovation and expansion —begun in 1670, as soon as Paluzzo assumed power— was entrusted to the architect **Giovanni Antonio De Rossi** (1616–1695), a disciple of Bernini who had worked on the fabric of Saint Peter's and on several cardinalitial palaces. De Rossi transformed the original building into a monumental palace of three stories, with a facade of windows crowned by alternating pediments —triangular and curved—, a large central portal, and an inner porticoed courtyard.

The interior of the Palazzo Altieri is sumptuous. The main staircase —one of the most spectacular of Baroque Rome— ascends in monumental flights decorated with stuccoes, marbles, and frescoes. The great reception hall —the *Salone d'Onore*— is decorated with frescoes representing the cardinal virtues and the glory of the Altieri family, with the heraldic emblems of the house —the Altieri star— omnipresent on ceilings, walls, and furnishings.

The Palazzo Altieri was designed to compete with the great papal palaces of Rome —the Palazzo Farnese, the Palazzo Borghese, the Palazzo Barberini—. Paluzzo wanted the Altieri to have a residence worthy of the great papal families, and he achieved it. The palace remains, in 2026, one of the largest private residences in Rome. It is partially open to the public on special occasions —concerts, exhibitions, cultural events— and preserves most of its original Baroque decoration.

## 17th–18th Centuries — The Altieri in the Black Nobility: Cardinals, Palaces, and Marriages

After the pontificate of Clement X and the death of Paluzzo Altieri in 1698, the Altieri consolidated themselves as a first-rank Black Nobility family. They did not produce another Pope —no subsequent conclave ever elected an Altieri again— but they produced several cardinals throughout the 17th and 18th centuries: **Giovanni Battista Altieri** (1673–1740), created cardinal in 1724, and **Lorenzo Altieri** (1671–1741), cardinal since 1730, among others. The presence of Altieri cardinals in the College of Cardinals —though never with Paluzzo's influence— guaranteed that the family kept a foot in the Curia for generations.

The Altieri connected with other Black Nobility families through strategic marriages: with the Colonna, the Borghese, the Pallavicini, and the Sacchetti. These matrimonial alliances —the classic mechanism of integration into the Roman aristocracy— consolidated the Altieri position and guaranteed the continuity of the lineage.

The Palazzo Altieri continued to be the main residence of the family and one of the centers of the social life of the Roman aristocracy during the 18th and 19th centuries. The family also maintained rural properties in the Roman countryside —the fiefs acquired by Paluzzo— and in the Marche, from where the Paluzzi Albertoni branch originated.

## 20th Century — The Altieri in Contemporary Italy: Discreet Survival

During the 20th century, the Altieri experienced the same process of adaptation as the rest of the Black Nobility: from feudal and ecclesiastical lords to managers of cultural and urban heritage. The abolition of noble titles by the Italian Republic in 1946 legally annulled the titles of Princes Altieri —which the family held by papal concession— but did not affect their real assets: the Palazzo Altieri in Piazza del Gesù remained family property.

Unlike other papal families that sold their palaces to the Italian state —the Borghese sold the Galleria Borghese; the Chigi sold the Palazzo Chigi; the Barberini sold the Palazzo Barberini—, the Altieri kept ownership of the Palazzo Altieri. The palace did not become a public museum: it remained a private residence, although partially used for cultural events, concerts, and receptions. This conservation strategy —retaining ownership of the palace but not opening it to mass tourism— is one of the heritage management options adopted by several Black Nobility families, who prefer discretion and exclusivity to public exposure.

The 20th-century Altieri maintained a low profile, without the media visibility of the Colonna —whose palace opens regularly to the public— or the Borghese —whose art collection is one of the most visited museums in Italy—. Their main asset continues to be the Palazzo Altieri in Piazza del Gesù, one of the most valuable properties in Rome for its location, size, and historical-artistic value.

---

## 2026 — The Altieri Today: A Palace in Piazza del Gesù

In 2026, the Altieri remain one of the least known families of the Black Nobility outside of Rome —the average tourist does not recognize the surname Altieri as they recognize Borghese or Colonna— but they preserve one of the most impressive real estate assets in the city: the Palazzo Altieri in Piazza del Gesù, a few meters from the Pantheon, from the Church of Gesù —the mother church of the Jesuits— and from the administrative heart of historic Rome.

The Palazzo Altieri is a building of colossal dimensions —it occupies an entire city block—, with Baroque halls, frescoes, monumental staircases, and a porticoed courtyard. Its market value is incalculable —in Piazza del Gesù there are no comparable properties for sale—. The Altieri family manages the palace as a private residence and, occasionally, as a space for high-level cultural and corporate events.

The story of the Altieri summarizes, in a way, the paradox of the Black Nobility: an elderly Pope elected because no faction managed to impose its candidate, a nephew who governed de facto for six years and amassed a fortune for the family, and a Baroque palace that still stands three hundred and fifty years later. Clement X is probably one of the most forgotten popes in history —no tourist seeks out his tomb in Saint Peter's—, but the palace that his nephew built with the rents of the Church still dominates Piazza del Gesù.

---

## The Altieri Pattern: The Compromise Pope and the Window of Opportunity

The Altieri represent the pattern of the **compromise Pope** in its purest form: an elderly and seemingly harmless cardinal, elected because the opposing blocs of the conclave cannot agree on any weighty candidate, who governs for a short time and whose family uses the window of opportunity of the pontificate to extract maximum wealth in the shortest possible time.

The pattern repeated itself before and after the Altieri. Clement IX Rospigliosi was, in a sense, a compromise Pope —elected in 1667 to prevent the conclave from dragging on indefinitely— and governed only two years. Innocent XII Pignatelli, elected in 1691, was also a compromise between the factions. But Clement X Altieri took the model to the extreme: eighty years old, no real power, a nephew who governed for him, and a palace that still stands as testimony that, in Baroque Rome, even the weakest Pope could enrich his own.

The lesson of the Altieri is that in the 17th-century papacy, power did not always reside in the Pope. Sometimes it resided in the nephew. And when the Pope was an eighty-year-old old man who did not wish to govern —but only to survive—, the nephew was, de facto, the Pope.
