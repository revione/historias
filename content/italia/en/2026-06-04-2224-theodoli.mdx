# The Theodoli — Marquesses Since the 14th Century

---

## 14th–15th Centuries — Roman Origins: The Theodoli in Early Documentation

The Theodoli appear in the notarial documentation of Rome in the 14th century, although their ultimate origin is uncertain. Family traditions link them to Romagna —specifically to the city of Forlì, from where one branch is said to have emigrated to Rome in the Late Middle Ages—, but the Roman notarial sources of the 14th century already record them as a family established in the city, owners of lands in the Roman countryside, and linked to the administrative circles of the pontifical government.

The historian and genealogist Vittorio Spreti, in his monumental _Enciclopedia storico-nobiliare italiana_ (1928–1936), places the origin of the Theodoli in the 14th century and traces their genealogy —with the documentary interruptions typical of families of the petty nobility that did not produce Popes— to modern times. Unlike the Colonna or Orsini, whose origins go back to the 11th century and are wrapped in genealogical legends, the Theodoli are a family of documented lesser nobility that rises gradually, without spectacular leaps, over four centuries.

During the 14th and 15th centuries, the Theodoli were not feudal barons or magnates of the Curia: they were a family of the lesser Roman aristocracy, with agricultural properties, some minor administrative posts in the papal court, and a network of kinship with other families of the same rank. Their rise was slow, organic, almost imperceptible: the type of rise that does not produce chronicles but which, accumulated over generations, builds a solid position.

---

## 16th Century — The Silent Ascent: Chamberlains and Servants of the Papal Court

Throughout the 16th century, the Theodoli consolidated their presence at the papal court through service, not nepotism. Members of the family occupied the post of **chamberlain** —_cameriere segreto_— of various Popes, a position that did not confer direct political power but granted something perhaps more valuable: access. The papal chamberlain slept in the rooms adjacent to the Pope's, controlled who entered and left the private chambers, listened to the conversations of the pontiff with cardinals and ambassadors, and administered the personal objects of the Holy Father. It was a position of intimate trust that turned its holders into depositaries of secrets and informal intermediaries of papal power.

The Theodoli also served as diplomats of the Papal States in Italian courts and as military commanders of the papal troops in the wars that the Holy See waged during the Renaissance against neighboring states. They were not condottieri of the stature of the Orsini or the Vitelli: they were loyal, competent officers who carried out orders without protagonism and returned to Rome to continue their curial career.

The historian Paolo Prodi, in _Il sovrano pontefice: un corpo e due anime_ (Il Mulino, 1982), describes the ecosystem of the papal court as "a constellation of families that orbited around the Pope at different distances, from the nephews who occupied the center to the lesser servants who occupied the outer edges." The Theodoli were in that intermediate ring: neither nephews nor marginal, present without fanfare, accumulating social capital and favors that they could redeem in the next generation.

---

## 1622 — The Fief of San Vito: The Theodoli Become Marquesses

In the 17th century, the Theodoli made the qualitative leap that transformed a family of service nobility into a family of territorial nobility. They acquired the fief of **San Vito** —today San Vito Romano, in the hills east of Rome— and that of **Ciciliano**, also in the countryside of Latium, and received from the Holy See the title of **Marquess of San Vito** and **Count of Ciciliano**.

The acquisition of fiefs was the rite of passage of the pontifical aristocracy: buying lands and titles was safer than depending on curial offices that could disappear with the Pope who granted them. The fief of San Vito, with its agricultural lands and rents, provided the Theodoli with stable incomes independent of the vicissitudes of the papal court, and the title of marquess gave them precedence over the families of the Roman civic nobility that lacked feudal titles.

Ciciliano, for its part, was a fief of lesser extent but with a medieval castle strategically situated on the route between Rome and Subiaco. The two fiefs, combined, made the Theodoli territorial lords of a modest but respectable portion of eastern Latium.

The _Annuario della Nobiltà Italiana_ —the most authoritative genealogical publication on the Italian aristocracy— records the title of Marquess of San Vito as active to the present day, transmitted in the male line within the Theodoli family.

---

## 1643–1650 — Cardinal Mario Theodoli: The First Purple-Bearer of the Family

On July 13, 1643, Pope Urban VIII Barberini created **Mario Theodoli** a cardinal. He was the first purple-bearer of the family —and the one who would inaugurate a pattern that would be repeated for generations: the presence of a Theodoli in the Sacred College of Cardinals at the key moments of pontifical politics—.

Mario Theodoli had served as governor of several cities of the Papal States and as cleric of the Apostolic Chamber —the body that administered the finances of the Papal State— before receiving the red hat. He was a functionary of the Curia, not a theologian or a brilliant diplomat, but his administrative competence and his loyalty to the Barberini won him the Pope's trust.

Cardinal Theodoli participated in the conclave of 1644 —which elected Innocent X Pamphili— and in that of 1655 —which elected Alexander VII Chigi—, systematically aligned with the moderate faction of the College of Cardinals. He was not a faction leader: he was a second-rank cardinal, reliable, who voted with the dominant group and who ensured that the Theodoli family kept one foot inside the Curia while the other trod the lands of San Vito.

Gaetano Moroni, in his _Dizionario di erudizione storico-ecclesiastica_ (1840–1879), mentions the Theodoli cardinals as an example of "those purple-bearers who, without aspiring to the pontifical throne, sustained with their presence the dignity of the families of the Roman nobility in the Sacred College."

---

## 18th Century — Cardinal Carlo Theodoli and Curial Continuity

The 18th century brought another Theodoli cardinal —**Carlo Theodoli**—, created by Benedict XIV Lambertini in 1743, although some sources place the exact date of the red hat in 1744. The election of a second Theodoli to the cardinalate, a century after the first, confirmed the family's position as part of the stable furniture of the Roman Curia.

Carlo Theodoli was the nephew —or perhaps great-nephew— of Cardinal Mario and, like his predecessor, came from the pontifical administration rather than from the academic or diplomatic world. He served in the Fabbrica di San Pietro —the administration of the Vatican basilica— and in other administrative posts of the Curia before receiving the red hat.

The fact that the Theodoli produced cardinals in the 17th and 18th centuries but never a Pope is symptomatic of their position in the Black Nobility. The families that produced Popes —Barberini, Chigi, Borghese, Pamphili— did so because they accumulated the necessary power to place one of their own in the Apostolic See. The Theodoli never aspired to that much: their goal was to have a cardinal in each generation —or in each couple of generations— who kept the family connected to the Curia without overshadowing the great papal dynasties. It was a defensive, modest, and extraordinarily effective strategy.

---

## 19th Century — 1870: The Closed Door

On September 20, 1870, the troops of the Kingdom of Italy stormed into Rome through the breach opened in the walls next to Porta Pia. The temporal power of the Popes, which had lasted more than a thousand years, ended that day. Pius IX withdrew to the Vatican and declared himself a prisoner. The families of the Black Nobility —those who owed their position and their titles to the papacy— had to choose between loyalty to the Pope or accommodation to the new Kingdom of Italy.

The Theodoli, like the Massimo, the Colonna, the Orsini, the Sacchetti, and the majority of the families of the Black Nobility, closed the doors of their palace as a sign of mourning and solidarity with the Pope. The gesture of closing the doors was more than symbolic: it meant rejecting the legitimacy of the Kingdom of Italy, refusing to participate in the political life of the new state, and maintaining the legal fiction that the Pope was still the legitimate sovereign of Rome.

The historian David Kertzer, in _The Pope and Mussolini: The Secret History of Pius XI and the Rise of Fascism in Europe_ (Random House, 2014), documents how the families of the Black Nobility maintained the boycott of the Italian state for fifty-nine years —from 1870 to the signing of the Lateran Pacts in 1929—, rejecting public offices, not voting in national elections, and limiting their marriages to other families of the same loyalty. The Theodoli formed part of that boycott, as attested by the social chronicles of the Rome of the last third of the 19th century.

---

## 20th Century — From the Roman Question to the Lateran Pacts

After the Lateran Pacts of 1929, which resolved the Roman Question and created the State of the Vatican City, the Black Nobility lost its political reason for being. There was no longer a prisoner Pope to be loyal to, nor an Italian state to reject. The families who for almost sixty years had closed their doors to the Kingdom of Italy progressively integrated into the life of the new regime —the fascist monarchy of the Savoys, and later the Italian Republic—.

The Theodoli, like so many other families of the Roman aristocracy, adapted to the new reality. They continued using the title of Marquess of San Vito in the circles of the Roman nobility, maintained the Palazzo Theodoli in Rome, and preserved the agricultural properties in Latium. But the world that had sustained the Black Nobility for centuries —the temporal power of the Popes, the pontifical court as the center of the social and political life of Rome, the system of fiefs and papal titles— had disappeared. What remained was the memory, the stone, and the name.

---

## 21st Century — The Theodoli Today: The Palace That Survives

In 2026, the Theodoli family continues to exist. The title of **Marquess of San Vito** and **Count of Ciciliano** continues to be used in the circles of the Roman aristocracy —although without legal recognition by the Italian Republic, which does not recognize noble titles—. The family preserves properties in Rome and in Latium.

The **Palazzo Theodoli** in Rome —located in the area of Campo Marzio, not far from the Italian Parliament— still bears the name of the family, although it has been partially converted into residential apartments. Like so many palaces of the Black Nobility, its history reflects the trajectory of the pontifical aristocracy in the 20th and 21st centuries: from a single-family residence of a family to an apartment building, from a symbol of power to a silent witness of a vanished world.

But the name on the facade is still there. And in the Black Nobility, to survive —with the name, with the title, with the palace— is already a form of victory.

---

## The Second-Tier Pattern: The Theodoli and Nobility Without a Pope

The Theodoli represent, in an almost archetypal way, the **second-tier pattern** in the Black Nobility. It is a pattern they share with the Sacchetti, the Ruspoli, the Pallavicini, and other families of the pontifical aristocracy: documentation since the Late Middle Ages, feudal titles acquired in the Modern Age, one or several cardinals over the centuries, constant presence at the papal court as chamberlains and functionaries of the Curia, closing of doors in 1870, and survival into the 21st century without producing a Pope.

This pattern is distinct from that of the great papal families —Borghese, Barberini, Chigi, Pamphili, Boncompagni— who reached the summit of pontifical power during one or two pontificates, accumulated immense wealth, built monumental villas and palaces, and then became extinct or diluted. The families of the second tier did not have a century of glory: they had centuries of modest but constant presence. They did not build the Trevi Fountain or Saint Peter's Square, but they maintained their palaces, their titles, and their place in Roman society for six hundred or seven hundred years.

The historian Claudio Rendina, in _Le grandi famiglie di Roma_ (Newton Compton, 2006), describes the Black Nobility as "a constellation of families of different ranks and influences, from the princes who rivaled kings to the simple marquesses who orbited around the papal court without ever aspiring to the throne." The Theodoli occupied that second circle of the constellation, and they occupied it with the wisdom of those who know that the most durable power is not that which shines, but that which does not go out.
