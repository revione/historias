# The Conti — Innocent III, the Man Who Ruled Kings

---

## 12th Century — Origins in Segni: A Family of the Roman Countryside

The Conti family owes its name to the comital title —_comites_, counts— they held from their origins in the city of Segni, an ancient citadel of the Lepini Mountains, about sixty kilometers southeast of Rome. Segni —the ancient Roman _Signia_, founded by Tarquin the Proud according to legend— was a stronghold controlling the pass between the Sacco valley and the Pontine countryside. The _comites Signiae_ —the counts of Segni— appear in notarial documentation from the 11th century as territorial lords of the region, direct vassals of the Apostolic See.

The first documented member of the family with the surname "Conti" is **Trasimondo dei Conti di Segni**, count of Segni at the end of the 11th century, father of several sons who would occupy key positions in the Roman Church. One of them, Lotario, would change the history of Europe.

The British historian Jane Sayers, in her monograph _Innocent III: Leader of Europe, 1198-1216_ (Longman, 1994), describes the Conti as "a family of the rural aristocracy of Latium with deep connections in the Roman Curia but without the territorial ambition of the Colonna or the military ferocity of the Orsini." Their power did not emanate from castles or private armies: it emanated from canon law, from theology, and from a conception of the papacy that was about to reach its zenith.

---

## 1198 — Innocent III: The Most Powerful Pope of the Middle Ages

On January 8, 1198, Cardinal Lotario dei Conti di Segni was elected Pope under the name Innocent III. He was thirty-seven years old —the youngest Pope in history up to that point—. He held a doctorate in theology from the University of Paris and a doctorate in canon law from the University of Bologna, the two great intellectual factories of medieval Christendom. His treatise _De miseria humanae conditionis_ —"On the Misery of the Human Condition," written before he was Pope— was one of the most widely read ascetic texts of 12th-century Europe.

Innocent III governed the Church for eighteen years (1198-1216) and, in that time, raised papal power to a height that no predecessor had reached —and that no successor would equal—. The German historian Helene Tillmann, in her monumental _Papst Innocenz III._ (Bonn, 1954, translated into English in 1980), maintains that "Innocent III was the first Pope who effectively governed Latin Christendom as a universal sovereign."

His concept of the papacy was formulated in his own correspondence. In a letter to the patriarch of Constantinople, he wrote: "The Lord has set me over nations and kingdoms, to root up and destroy, to build and to plant." The phrase was a quote from the prophet Jeremiah, but Innocent III applied it to himself without reservation. The Pope was not one bishop among bishops: he was the vicar of Christ on earth, the supreme judge of kings and emperors, the arbiter of political legitimacy throughout Christendom.

---

## 1198–1216 — The Pope Who Deposed Kings: England, France, and the Empire

Innocent III did not limit himself to proclaiming his supremacy: he exercised it. During his pontificate, he intervened in the dynastic successions of all the great monarchies of Europe.

**England.** In 1205, after the death of the archbishop of Canterbury, the cathedral chapter and King John Lackland proposed different candidates for the primatial see of England. Innocent III rejected both candidates and imposed his own man: Stephen Langton, an English cardinal trained in Paris. John refused to accept him. Innocent III responded in 1208 with an interdict on all of England —churches closed, bells silenced, sacraments suspended— and, in 1209, with the personal excommunication of the king. Finally, in 1213, John capitulated: he handed over England and its domains to the Pope as a fief of the Holy See, pledging to pay an annual tribute of a thousand marks. The King of England was, legally, a vassal of the Pope. The historian Jane Sayers documents this episode as "the greatest affirmation of papal sovereignty over a Christian kingdom in the Middle Ages."

**The Holy Roman Empire.** When Emperor Henry VI died in 1197, the imperial throne was disputed between Philip of Swabia —of the house of Hohenstaufen— and Otto of Brunswick —of the house of Welf—. Innocent III set himself up as arbiter of the dispute and, in 1202, issued the decretal _Venerabilem_, in which he affirmed the Pope's right to "examine, approve, anoint, and consecrate" the emperor-elect —and, therefore, to reject a candidate he considered unworthy—. Innocent III first supported Otto IV and, when he broke his promises, excommunicated him and transferred his support to Frederick II —the future emperor and nemesis of the papacy—. The Pope decided who was emperor.

**France.** In 1199, King Philip II Augustus of France repudiated his wife Ingeborg of Denmark to marry Agnes of Merania. Innocent III declared the second marriage illegitimate and, in 1200, imposed an interdict on France —the same collective punishment he would later apply to England—. Philip II, one of the most powerful monarchs in Europe, submitted: he readmitted Ingeborg to the court and broke with Agnes.

In the words of the historian John C. Moore, in _Pope Innocent III: To Root Up and to Plant_ (Brill, 2003): "No Pope before or after exercised power so directly over the thrones of Europe. Innocent III was not a spiritual sovereign: he was a universal sovereign, and the kings knew it."

## 1202–1204 — The Fourth Crusade: The Diversion Innocent III Could Not Control

The Fourth Crusade was the greatest paradox of Innocent III's pontificate. Summoned by the Pope in 1198 with the aim of reconquering Jerusalem, the crusade was diverted to Constantinople —the capital of the Christian Byzantine Empire—, which was sacked by the crusaders on April 13, 1204.

Innocent III had explicitly forbidden attacking Christian cities. But the crusaders, indebted to Venice —which had financed the fleet—, accepted the proposal of Doge Enrico Dandolo: to attack Zara (in Dalmatia, a Christian city) and then Constantinople to restore a deposed Byzantine emperor who promised to finance the continuation of the crusade. The plan failed, the Greeks did not pay, and the crusaders sacked Constantinople for three days —they burned the city, desecrated Hagia Sophia, stole relics—.

Innocent III, upon learning the news, wrote a furious letter to the crusaders excommunicating them for having attacked Zara. But, once the sack of Constantinople was consummated, he opted for ecclesiastical realpolitik: he accepted the appointment of a Latin patriarch in Constantinople and proclaimed that God had used the crusade —despite the sins of the crusaders— to reunify the Greek Church with the Roman one. The union lasted until 1261, when the Byzantines recovered Constantinople, but the fracture between East and West became irreversible —much more so than after the schism of 1054—.

The historian Donald E. Queller, in _The Fourth Crusade: The Conquest of Constantinople_ (University of Pennsylvania Press, 1977), documents that Innocent III "was at once the promoter of the crusade and its political victim: he created a machine he could not control, and the result was the greatest diplomatic disaster in the relations between Rome and Constantinople."

## 1209–1216 — Franciscans and Dominicans: The Pope Who Approved the Two Orders That Saved the Church

Innocent III was the Pope who approved —or initiated the process of approval of— the two great mendicant orders of the Middle Ages: the Franciscans and the Dominicans. The coincidence was not accidental: the Pope understood that the Cathar and Waldensian heresy —which ravaged southern France and northern Italy— could not be fought only with crusades and excommunications, but with preachers who lived what they preached.

**Francis of Assisi.** In 1209 or 1210, a young penitent from Assisi named Francis —son of a rich cloth merchant, who had renounced his inheritance to live in radical poverty— arrived in Rome with a small group of followers to request papal approval of their way of life. Innocent III received them, listened to their proposal and —after some resistance from the cardinals, who considered their rule too extreme— granted oral approval. The Franciscan biographer Thomas of Celano, in his _Vita Prima_ (1228), narrates that Innocent III had a dream in which he saw a poor man holding up the basilica of Saint John Lateran —the Pope's cathedral— to prevent it from collapsing. That man was Francis.

The formal approval —the bull _Solet annuere_— would come in 1223, under Honorius III Savelli. But it was Innocent III who opened the door.

**Dominic de Guzmán.** In 1215, during the IV Lateran Council, the Castilian canon Dominic de Guzmán asked Innocent III for the approval of an order of preachers dedicated specifically to combating heresy through preaching and study. Innocent III urged him to adopt an already existing rule —that of Saint Augustine— and, on December 22, 1216, his successor Honorius III confirmed the Order of Preachers. But once again it was Innocent III who conceived and blessed the project.

The historian Lester K. Little, in _Religious Poverty and the Profit Economy in Medieval Europe_ (Cornell University Press, 1978), argues that the approval of the Franciscans and Dominicans by Innocent III was "the most brilliant act of pastoral government of the medieval papacy" —because it integrated radical poverty movements within the Church instead of letting them drift toward heresy—.

## 1215 — The IV Lateran Council: The Church Reorganizes

In November 1215, Innocent III inaugurated the IV Lateran Council —the twelfth ecumenical council of the Catholic Church— in the basilica of Saint John Lateran in Rome. More than four hundred bishops, eight hundred abbots and priors, and representatives of all Christian monarchies attended. It was the largest ecclesiastical assembly of the Middle Ages and defined Catholicism for the following three hundred years.

The seventy canons of the IV Lateran Council —personally drafted by Innocent III, according to historian Anne J. Duggan in _The Fourth Lateran Council_ (Brepols, 2008)— established:

- The annual obligation of confession and Easter communion for all the faithful —the canon _Omnis utriusque sexus_— which created sacramental control over the Christian population and gave the Church unprecedented power over individual conscience.
- The dogmatic definition of transubstantiation —the term appears for the first time in a conciliar document—, affirming that bread and wine really become the body and blood of Christ.
- The prohibition of new religious orders, to control the proliferation of movements that could drift into heresy.
- Norms on episcopal election, clergy discipline, and the prohibition against clergy participating in trials involving bloodshed.
- The obligation for Jews and Muslims in Christian lands to wear visible badges on their clothing —the origin of the yellow star and other discriminatory marks that lasted for centuries—.

The IV Lateran Council transformed the Church from a loose federation of dioceses into a centralized pontifical monarchy. Innocent III, in the opening session, delivered a sermon on the text of Luke 22:38 —"Lord, here are two swords"— in which he reaffirmed that both swords —the spiritual and the temporal— belonged to the Church and were at the service of the Pope.

## 1227 — Gregory IX: The Pope Who Created the Inquisition

On March 19, 1227, Cardinal Ugolino di Conti was elected Pope under the name Gregory IX. He was the nephew —or perhaps cousin— of Innocent III and had been his trusted man in the Curia for almost three decades. He was over eighty years old —the exact age is uncertain— but his pontificate, which lasted fourteen years (1227-1241), was one of the most energetic and transformative of the 13th century.

Gregory IX passed into history for three acts that defined medieval Catholicism:

**The Papal Inquisition.** In 1231, Gregory IX promulgated the bull _Excommunicamus_, establishing the pontifical Inquisition —a permanent tribunal charged with prosecuting heresy, dependent directly on the Pope, not on local bishops—. In 1233, he entrusted its execution to the Dominicans, who became the _Domini canes_ —the "dogs of the Lord"—. The historian Edward Peters, in _Inquisition_ (University of California Press, 1989), documents that Gregory IX "transformed the prosecution of heresy from a sporadic episcopal task into a centralized institutional system operating throughout Christendom."

**The Decretals of Gregory IX.** In 1234, Gregory IX promulgated the _Liber Extra_ —known as the Decretals of Gregory IX—, a codification of canon law compiled by the Dominican jurist Saint Raymond of Peñafort. The Decretals were the legal code of the Catholic Church until 1917 —nearly seven hundred years— and established the legal framework for the governance of Christendom. No Pope legislated so exhaustively on so many aspects of ecclesiastical and civil life.

**The War with Frederick II.** Gregory IX was the most tenacious adversary of Emperor Frederick II Hohenstaufen —the _stupor mundi_, the most brilliant and dangerous man of his time—. Frederick II was king of Sicily, emperor of the Holy Roman Empire and —in theory— vassal of the Pope, but he governed his domains with an autonomy that frontally challenged papal theocracy. Gregory IX excommunicated him in 1227 for failing to keep his promise to lead a crusade; absolved him in 1230 after a precarious agreement; and excommunicated him again in 1239, launching a conflict that consumed the last years of his pontificate.

Gregory IX died on August 22, 1241, while Frederick II was besieging Rome. He was buried in Saint Peter's Basilica. His pontificate had consolidated the institutional power of the papacy —Inquisition, Decretals, dominion over the Empire—, but it had also exacerbated the contradiction between theocratic pretension and the reality of imperial military power.

---

## 1228 — The Canonization of Francis of Assisi

Gregory IX, before being Pope, had been the cardinal protector of the Franciscan Order and a personal friend of Francis of Assisi. As Pope, on July 16, 1228, barely a year after his election, he canonized Francis in the basilica of San Giorgio in Assisi —the fastest canonization in history—. In 1230, he commissioned the construction of the basilica of Saint Francis in Assisi, which would become one of the great spiritual and artistic centers of Christendom —with frescoes by Giotto and Cimabue—.

The Conti-Franciscan connection was intimate. Gregory IX took from Francis not only his spirituality but his network of friars, whom he used as preachers of Roman orthodoxy against heresy. The same Pope who created the Inquisition canonized the saint of poverty. The apparent contradiction is, in reality, the key to the political genius of the Conti: integrating the most radical spiritual movements into the structure of papal power.

---

## 1254–1261 — Alexander IV: The Third Conti Pope

On December 12, 1254, Cardinal Rinaldo dei Conti di Segni —nephew of Gregory IX— was elected Pope under the name Alexander IV. He was the third Pope of the Conti family in less than sixty years. His pontificate, which lasted until 1261, was less spectacular than those of Innocent III and Gregory IX, but consolidated two family legacies: the alliance with the Franciscans —he confirmed the order of the Poor Clares— and the defense of the papacy against the Hohenstaufen, continuing the war of his predecessor against Manfred, illegitimate son of Frederick II.

Alexander IV also created cardinals from his family —including his nephew Annibaldo—, perpetuating another Conti pattern: the continuous presence of at least one cardinal of the blood in the Sacred College during the 13th century.

---

## 13th–18th Centuries — The Conti di Segni: From Popes to Dukes

After the death of Alexander IV in 1261, the Conti family no longer produced Popes. But it did not disappear. Throughout the late medieval and modern centuries, the Conti transformed from feudal counts into dukes and princes of the Roman aristocracy, integrated into the Black Nobility and linked by marriage to the great baronial houses —Orsini, Colonna, Savelli—.

**The Duchy of Poli.** In the 16th century, the Conti acquired the fief of Poli, in the hills east of Rome, and turned it into a duchy. The Palazzo Conti of Poli —today Palazzo Conti di Segni— was the main residence of the family for centuries and still stands as one of the best-preserved noble palaces of Latium.

**The Valmontone Branch.** Another branch of the Conti established itself in Valmontone, south of Rome, where they built the Palazzo Conti —also known as Palazzo Doria Pamphili—, an imposing baroque palace that today houses the Museum of Valmontone.

Unlike the Caetani —who built a territorial empire in the Pontine Marshes— or the Colonna —who dominated southern Latium—, the Conti maintained a modest territorial profile but a sustained influence in the Curia and in Roman nobility for seven hundred years.

The historian Gaetano Moroni, in his _Dizionario di erudizione storico-ecclesiastica_ (1840–1879), documents the Conti di Segni as "one of the oldest and most respected families of Roman nobility, whose principal glory resides in the three great pontiffs it gave to the Church in the 13th century."

---

## 21st Century — The Conti Today

In 2026, the Conti di Segni continue to exist as a family, although without the power they had in the medieval centuries. They hold the title of Duke of Segni —recognized by the Holy See— and maintain properties in Rome and in the Latium countryside. No Cardinal Conti today occupies a place in the Curia, but the family name remains in the historical memory of the papacy as that of the dynasty that, in the 13th century, governed Christendom with an ambition and efficacy that no other equaled.

Innocent III, Gregory IX, and Alexander IV —three Popes in sixty years— represent the zenith of medieval pontifical power. The Conti did not build a territorial empire like the Farnese or a machine of nepotism like the Borghese: they built the very juridical and doctrinal architecture of papal power. Canon law, the Inquisition, supremacy over kings, conciliar centralization: all of that came from the mind of the Conti di Segni.
