# The Orsini — Three Popes, 34 Cardinals and the Throne That Never Fell

---

## 10th Century — Legendary origins and rise in medieval Rome

Rome around the year 1000 was a city of factions. The papacy was not yet the centralised spiritual monarchy it would later become: it was a bishopric disputed by the great families of the city —Crescenzi, Teofilatti, Tuscolani— who succeeded one another on the throne of St Peter as if it were a fiefdom. In this fertile ground emerged the Orsini, whose name —"orsino", that which belongs to the bear— appears in the oldest Roman documents with the family coat of arms they still bear: a rampant bear.

The first Orsini documented with certainty is **Orso di Bobone**, lord of the castle of Monte Giordano —today Palazzo Orsini Taverna, in the rione Ponte of Rome— around the year 1000. The Bobone were a branch of the Roman landowning aristocracy with properties in the Tiber valley and the countryside north of the city. The English chronicler Matthew Paris, who visited Rome in the 13th century, called them _"a race of bears"_ for their political ferocity. It was not a compliment, but it was a recognition: the Orsini already inspired fear.

Pope Innocent II (1130-1143), of the Papareschi family —related to the Bobone— favoured the rise of his Orsini relatives. He granted them castles on the Via Flaminia, rights over Tiber bridges, and custody of the city gates. By the mid-12th century, the Orsini controlled the northern access to Rome and a network of fortifications that allowed them to block the city's supplies if necessary. The pattern would repeat itself for eight hundred years: every time an Orsini reached the papacy —or the cardinalate—, the family properties expanded.

## 1191 — Celestine III: the first Orsini Pope and the birth of family papal power

On 30 March 1191, Giacinto Bobone Orsini was elected Pope. He took the name Celestine III. He was eighty-five years old. The Curia considered him a transitional pope due to his age —they were wrong. He governed for seven years, until 1198, and in that time he laid the foundations of what would be Orsini power for the next eight centuries.

Celestine III crowned Emperor Henry VI of Hohenstaufen on 15 April 1191, in a ceremony where, according to tradition, the old pope used his feet to remove the crown from the bowed emperor's head and then replace it —a gesture of papal superiority that enraged the imperial court. The symbolic humiliation was the message: the Pope grants temporal power, and can withdraw it.

But Celestine III did not limit himself to imperial politics. During his pontificate, he placed two nephews in the College of Cardinals —creating the first Orsini bloc in the Curia— and transferred Church properties to the family under the pretext of "protecting" them from rival factions. The castle of Vicovaro, the fortress of Nerola, the lands of Monte Rotondo: each of these fiefs, originally property of the Apostolic See, passed into Orsini hands during his papacy. The boundary between ecclesiastical patrimony and family patrimony dissolved.

When he died in 1198, the Orsini were no longer just another family of the Roman nobility. They were the papal family par excellence, with a model they would repeat meticulously: use the throne of Peter to enrich the kinship, place cardinals as a guarantee of future influence, and accumulate territory in the Papal States as a base of temporal power.

## 13th Century — Guelphs versus Ghibellines: the perpetual war with the Colonna

The 13th century consolidated the Orsini as leaders of the Guelph faction in Rome: the Pope's party against the Emperor. Their nemesis were the Colonna, leaders of the Ghibelline —pro-imperial— camp. The Orsini-Colonna rivalry became the axis of Roman politics for three hundred years.

The division was not purely ideological. It was territorial, economic, and bloody. The Colonna controlled the countryside south of Rome, southern Latium, and the Alban Hills with their formidable castles —Palestrina, Genazzano, Paliano. The Orsini dominated the north, with fortresses on the Via Flaminia, the Via Cassia, and the Tiber valley. Rome was a city divided between two families that detested each other, and every papal election was a pitched battle —literally— in which the cardinals of both factions negotiated, threatened, and sometimes killed each other.

The historian Ferdinand Gregorovius, in his monumental _Geschichte der Stadt Rom im Mittelalter_ (1859-1872), described the Orsini-Colonna struggle as "the chronic fever of Rome." During the papal interregnum of 1268-1271 —the longest in history, three years without a Pope because the cardinals could not agree— the Orsini and the Colonna turned Viterbo into a battlefield. The city magistrates locked the cardinals in the papal palace and, according to legend, removed the roof to hasten the decision. Even so, it took nearly three years. The chosen one, Gregory X, was neither Orsini nor Colonna.

## 1277 — Nicholas III: the Orsini who built a territorial empire

On 25 November 1277, Giovanni Gaetano Orsini was elected Pope as Nicholas III. He was a man of forty-seven years, a cardinal since the age of twenty-eight, trained at the pontifical court of Innocent IV. Unlike Celestine III, who reached the throne in his old age, Nicholas III arrived in his prime and with a plan.

In three years of pontificate (1277-1280), Nicholas III executed the greatest expansion of family power in Orsini history. Contemporary chroniclers nicknamed him "the cardinal of the bear" and later "the builder pope."

**Massive land purchases.** Nicholas III used the pontifical treasury to acquire castles, villas, and lands in the name of the family. In Rome, he bought Monte Giordano —already an Orsini possession but now consolidated as an urban fortress— and the area of the future Palazzo Orsini in Campo de' Fiori. In the countryside, he acquired the fiefs of Galera, Bracciano, Anguillara, Cerveteri, and Formello. At his death, the Orsini possessed more than sixty castles and fortifications in Latium and Umbria.

**Systematic nepotism.** Nicholas III created three Orsini cardinals in a single consistory: his brother Giordano, his nephew Latino Malabranca —son of his sister Mabilia Orsini— and another nephew, Matteo Rosso Orsini, who would become one of the most powerful figures in the College of Cardinals over the following century. In addition, he appointed Orsini relatives as podestà —governors— of strategic cities of the Papal States: Bologna, Perugia, Viterbo, Orvieto. The papal administration became, in practice, an Orsini administration.

**Constitutional reform.** In 1278, Nicholas III promulgated the bull _Fundamenta militantis ecclesiae_, which prohibited foreigners —meaning the French and Angevins— from holding offices in the Senate of Rome and in the city administration. The measure, presented as a defence of Romanitas, had a practical purpose: to ensure that no external power could challenge the dominion of the Roman families —that is, the Orsini— over the city. He also forced Charles of Anjou, King of Sicily and lifelong Senator of Rome, to resign his senatorial office.

**The Treaty with Rudolf of Habsburg.** In May 1278, Nicholas III negotiated with Rudolf I of Habsburg —the first emperor of the dynasty that would govern Central Europe for six centuries— the recognition of papal sovereignty over the territories of Romagna and the Pentapolis, which the German emperors had claimed since Charlemagne. In exchange, Nicholas III recognised Rudolf as King of the Romans and future Emperor. The treaty was a masterpiece of diplomacy: the Orsini secured the eastern borders of the Papal States while expanding their fiefs within them.

Nicholas III died on 22 August 1280 at the castle of Soriano nel Cimino, one of the Orsini fortresses. Dante condemned him to the eighth circle of Hell —among the simoniacs— in Canto XIX of the _Divine Comedy_, accusing him of nepotism and of having prostituted the papacy for gold. But Dante, a Ghibelline, was a political enemy of the Orsini. What he called corruption was, seen from Rome, pure dynastic politics.

## 14th Century — The cardinal network: 34 purple robes in eight centuries

The Orsini produced thirty-four cardinals between the 12th and 20th centuries. It is a figure surpassed only by the Colonna —their eternal rival— and, in the entire history of the Church, by very few other families.

The first documented Orsini cardinal was **Pietro Orsini**, created by Pope Alexander III in 1165, even before the pontificate of Celestine III. But it was from Nicholas III onwards that the Orsini cardinalate became a system. Between 1278 and 1378 —exactly one century— eleven Orsini received the cardinal's hat. In the College of Cardinals there were, on average, between one and three Orsini cardinals in each conclave. No papal election for two hundred years took place without the Orsini having at least one guaranteed vote —and almost always more.

Cardinal **Napoleone Orsini** (1263-1342) was the most influential figure of the family in the 14th century. Created cardinal deacon of Sant'Adriano in 1288 by Nicholas IV, he survived nine popes, participated in seven conclaves, and accumulated the archdeaconry of Liège, the abbey of Saint-Martin in Tournai, and the diocese of Palermo as personal benefices —all of which produced revenues that flowed into the Orsini treasury. By 1305, Napoleone Orsini was one of the richest men in Europe. He wrote to King Philip IV of France offering him advice on how to handle popes —because he knew them all.

Cardinal **Giovanni Battista Orsini** (1453-1503) was another notable case: created cardinal by Sixtus IV at the age of twenty-eight, he was one of the most active diplomats of the Renaissance, with missions in France, Hungary, and the Italian states. In 1503, Pope Alexander VI Borgia —mortal enemy of the Orsini— imprisoned him in the Castel Sant'Angelo and probably poisoned him, along with other family members who fell in the Borgian purge.

The Orsini cardinal strategy was always the same: place at least one family member in each generation of the College of Cardinals to ensure that no Pope could govern without them. It worked for eight hundred years.

## 14th Century — The marriage network: from the German Anhalt to the Julio-Claudians

Orsini marriages were not romantic: they were geopolitical contracts. The family wove alliances with the reigning houses of Germany, Austria, and Poland, creating a kinship network that transcended Italian borders.

**German connection: Anhalt, Baden, Rosenberg.** In the 14th century, the Orsini established matrimonial ties with the House of Anhalt —one of the oldest dynasties of the Holy Roman Empire, originating from the castle of Anhalt in Saxony-Anhalt. Also with the Margraves of Baden, who governed territories in the Black Forest, and with the Rosenbergs of Bohemia. These marriages had a precise function: the Orsini needed allies at the German imperial court to counter the influence of the Colonna —who had always been pro-imperial— and to ensure that the Emperor did not support their enemies in the endless wars of the papacy.

**The Julio-Claudian connection.** One of the most controversial genealogical claims —but one documentarily defended by the family itself— is the link of the Orsini with the Julio-Claudian dynasty of imperial Rome, through the _gens Ursina_. The thesis, defended by the 17th-century genealogist Francesco Sansovino in his _Origine e fatti delle famiglie illustri d'Italia_ (Venice, 1582), holds that the Orsini descend from the _gens Ursina_, a branch of the imperial Roman nobility related to the family of Julius Caesar. Most modern historians consider this lineage legendary —constructed during the Renaissance to endow the Orsini with an antiquity comparable to that of the Colonna, who claimed descent from the Counts of Tusculum. But the fact that the family actively promoted this narrative for centuries is itself a historical datum: it reveals to what extent the Orsini conceived their power as a continuation of imperial Roman power.

**Italian alliances.** Within Italy, the Orsini intermarried with the Savelli —who produced two popes—, the Caetani —the family of Boniface VIII—, the Ordelaffi of Forlì, the da Varano of Camerino, and the Este of Ferrara. Each marriage sealed a truce or a military alliance. The Orsini did not marry: they annexed.

## 1724 — Benedict XIII: the third Orsini Pope

On 29 May 1724, Cardinal Pietro Francesco Orsini was elected Pope under the name Benedict XIII. He was seventy-five years old. He was a Dominican, a preaching friar before being a prince, and his pontificate (1724-1730) was ascetic, devout, and disastrous for the temporal power of the family.

Benedict XIII was an atypical Orsini. He had entered the Order of Preachers against his family's will —which expected him to be a prince of the Church in the traditional style, not a friar— and maintained his vows of poverty even as Pope. He slept on a straw mattress, celebrated Mass daily, visited hospitals, and refused to enrich his nephews. By the standards of the Black Nobility, he was an anomaly.

But his pontificate was marked by scandal. Benedict XIII entrusted the administration of the Papal States to Cardinal Niccolò Coscia, a Neapolitan cleric of dubious reputation who plundered the papal coffers, sold ecclesiastical offices, and turned Benevento —his native diocese— into a personal fief. When Benedict XIII died in February 1730, the College of Cardinals prosecuted Coscia for corruption, excommunicated him, and imprisoned him in the Castel Sant'Angelo. But the damage to the Orsini reputation was done: the family's third Pope had left a legacy of mismanagement, not territorial expansion.

Despite everything, Benedict XIII canonised St John of the Cross, confirmed the cult of St Vincent Ferrer, and celebrated the Roman Synod of 1725 which reinforced clerical discipline. He was, paradoxically, the holiest and the least effective of the Orsini popes.

## 1560-1868 — The Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne: a throne beside the throne

The Orsini hold, together with the Colonna, the hereditary dignity of **Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne** (_Princeps Assistens Solio_). It is one of the oldest offices of the papal court and one of the few noble titles that the Vatican still formally recognises.

The dignity was created by Pope Pius IV in 1560 for the Orsini and the Colonna jointly —an ingenious attempt to force peace between the two families by compelling them to share an honour. The Prince Assistant stands to the right of the papal throne during pontifical ceremonies, physically closer to the Pope than any other layperson in the world. In photographs of 20th-century papal audiences, the Orsini prince invariably appears beside the Pope, standing, in full dress uniform.

For centuries, the office was not ceremonial: it was political. The Prince Assistant was the intermediary between the Pope and the Roman nobility, responsible for managing the Vatican's relations with the Catholic powers of Europe, and the keeper of the conclave door —literally: during papal elections, the Prince Assistant controlled who entered and left the cardinals' enclosure.

The reform of the papal court by Paul VI in 1968 abolished most of the Vatican's noble offices, but kept that of Prince Assistant as a symbolic dignity. Today, the prince assistant of the Colonna branch is Don Prospero Colonna, Prince of Avella. For the Orsini, the title falls to the line of the Dukes of Gravina.

## 16th Century — The Borgias, the Orsini, and the Jesuit connection

Relations between the Orsini and the Borgias —the Valencian family that produced two popes: Callixtus III and Alexander VI— were anything but peaceful. Alexander VI Borgia (1492-1503) attempted to exterminate the Orsini to consolidate the power of his children —Cesare and Lucrezia— in central Italy. In December 1499, Cesare Borgia attacked the Orsini castles of Imola and Forlì. In January 1503, Pope Alexander VI imprisoned and probably poisoned Cardinal Giovanni Battista Orsini, as has been documented. The war between the two families was total.

But from that war was born a deeper connection: the Society of Jesus.

**Francisco de Borja y Acevedo** (St Francis Borgia, 1510-1572), third Superior General of the Society of Jesus (1565-1572) and co-founder of the order in practice —he was the one who consolidated its institutional structure—, was a Borgia. Great-grandson of Pope Alexander VI, grandson of Giovanni Borgia —Duke of Gandía, murdered in 1497 in a crime legend attributes to his brother Cesare—, Francis Borgia broke with the military tradition of his lineage to become a Jesuit. But his path to the Society of Jesus was, in part, influenced by the political environment that the Orsini had forged in Rome.

After the death of his wife Leonor de Castro in 1546, Francis Borgia renounced his titles —he was Duke of Gandía and Marquis of Lombay—, distributed his goods among his children, and entered the Society of Jesus. His entry was approved by Ignatius of Loyola personally. But what the chronicles omit is that the network of contacts that Borgia needed to navigate the papal court —and for the Jesuits to be accepted as an order— included the Orsini. The Roman family, partially reconciled with the Borgias after the death of Alexander VI, saw in the Society of Jesus a useful counterweight to the Dominicans —traditional enemies of the Jesuits— and an instrument of influence in the Catholic courts of Europe.

The Orsini-Jesuit bond was not one of subordination but of convergence of interests. The Orsini supported the Society in Rome; the Society, with its global network of colleges, missions, and confessors to kings, extended Orsini influence to places where the family had no direct presence. When Francis Borgia was elected third General of the Jesuits in July 1565, several Orsini cardinals voted in favour of his papal confirmation.

## 12th-18th Centuries — The Orsini castle network: fortresses of Latium and Umbria

The Orsini built, bought, or inherited more than a hundred castles and fortifications between the 12th and 18th centuries. Many still stand. Some remain in the hands of the family.

**Bracciano.** The Orsini-Odescalchi castle of Bracciano, on the shores of the lake of the same name north of Rome, was built by Napoleone Orsini in the 15th century over a pre-existing fortification. Its current form dates from 1470-1490, when Gentil Virginio Orsini —condottiero in the service of Ferdinand of Aragon— enlarged it with five cylindrical towers. The fortress resisted a siege by the papal troops of Sixtus IV and another by those of Alexander VI. In 1696, the Odescalchi family —which had intermarried with the Orsini— inherited the castle, which today is the property of the Odescalchi branch but retains the compound name Orsini-Odescalchi. It is one of the best-preserved castles in Italy.

**Monterotondo.** The Orsini castle of Monterotondo, twenty kilometres northeast of Rome, was the family's principal fortress in the Roman countryside during the 14th and 15th centuries. From there, the Orsini controlled the Via Salaria —the salt route— and blocked any army attempting to approach Rome from the north. The castle was stormed by the troops of the Duke of Alba in 1556 and partially destroyed, but the Orsini rebuilt it.

**Nerola.** The fortress of Nerola, in the province of Rieti, was one of the original fiefs transferred to the Orsini during the pontificate of Celestine III. Enlarged in the 15th century, the fortress dominates the valley of the river Farfa and controlled the border between the Papal States and the Kingdom of Naples. More recently, the castle gives its name to "neroli," the essence of orange blossom extracted from the bitter oranges that grew in its gardens and which Anna Maria Orsini, Princess of Nerola, popularised at the European courts of the 17th century.

**Vicovaro.** The castle of Vicovaro, at the confluence of the Aniene river with the Licenza stream, east of Rome, was the key to the Via Valeria —the route to the Abruzzi. The Orsini received it in 1191 as compensation from Celestine III and kept it for four centuries, until they sold it in the 16th century. Today it is an Italian national monument.

**Galera.** The fortress of Galera, north of Rome, was the castle that controlled the Via Clodia. Built on an Etruscan settlement, it was acquired by the Orsini in the 13th century and became one of the agricultural centres of the family estate. It was abandoned in the 19th century and is today a ruin, but the surrounding lands remain registered in the name of Orsini heirs.

**Soriano nel Cimino.** The castle of Soriano, where Nicholas III died in 1280, dominates the southern slope of the Cimini Mountains. The fortress, built by the Orsini pope over an earlier structure, was impregnable and controlled the Via Ciminia —the alternative route towards Tuscany. It passed to the Albani in the 18th century and today houses municipal offices.

The Orsini castle network was not decorative: it was an integrated system of defence and territorial control. Each fortress watched over a route, a bridge, a mountain pass. Together, they allowed the Orsini to block access to Rome from any direction. Whoever wanted to enter the holy city —whether a German emperor, a French king, or an enemy pope— had to negotiate with an Orsini.

## 20th Century — The Black Nobility in the Italian Republic

The signing of the Lateran Pacts on 11 February 1929 between the Holy See and Mussolini's fascist Italy resolved the "Roman Question" —the conflict between the Vatican and the Kingdom of Italy since the capture of Rome in 1870. For the Black Nobility —the aristocratic families that had remained loyal to the Pope and had refused to recognise the Kingdom of Italy— the Pacts were a bittersweet moment. On the one hand, the Pope recovered his territorial sovereignty. On the other, the Italian monarchy of the House of Savoy was now a legitimate interlocutor of the Vatican, and the Black Nobility lost its historical raison d'être.

The Orsini adapted. During the fascist period (1922-1943), they maintained pragmatic relations with the regime —like most of the Italian aristocracy— without overcommitting. After the fall of the monarchy in the 1946 referendum, the Italian Republic abolished noble titles. The republican constitution of 1948, in its fourteenth transitional provision, declared that noble titles were not recognised and that the predicates of titles existing before 1922 counted as part of the name —and nothing more. But the republican abolition did not touch the true wealth of the Orsini: the land, the Roman palaces, and influence in the Vatican.

Today, the descendants of the Orsini continue to own significant properties in the historic centre of Rome and in the Latium countryside. The **Palazzo Orsini Taverna**, in the rione Ponte, remains in the hands of the family —albeit adapted to modern times, with luxury apartments and offices. The **Palazzo Orsini** on Monte Savello, next to the Theatre of Marcellus, is the property of the Dukes of Gravina and houses private apartments. The **Villa Orsini** on Via della Camilluccia, north of Rome, is one of the family's historic residences in the capital's residential area.

## 2026 — Contemporary power: between the Curia, the IOR, and Roman concrete

In 2026, asking "who is the head of the Orsini?" is asking the wrong question. The Orsini do not function as a family monarchy with a visible leader —unlike other noble houses. They function as a network. A network that runs through the Vatican Curia, the Institute for the Works of Religion (IOR, the Vatican Bank), the Roman real estate market, and the historical connections with the European aristocracy.

**In the Curia.** Although no cardinal today bears the surname Orsini —the last was Cardinal Domenico Orsini d'Aragona, who died in 1789— the family's influence in the Vatican is not measured by the number of purple robes with their name. It is measured by the presence of ecclesiastics trained in institutions historically linked to the Orsini, by the network of relationships the family maintains in the Secretariat of State and in the dicasteries of the Curia, and by the capacity for intermediation given by their unique position: noble —belonging to the Black Nobility— but perfectly integrated into the post-conciliar Church.

**In the IOR.** The Institute for the Works of Religion has been, since its founding in 1942, a territory of influence for the Roman nobility —including the Orsini— through the laypeople who have occupied its supervisory board, its financial committees, and its client networks. The management of noble estates —with needs for discretion, international transfers, and tax exemptions— finds a natural channel in the IOR. There is no public evidence of irregularities: there is an institutional logic that makes the Pope's bank the natural bank of the papal aristocracy.

**In the Roman real estate market.** Rome is a city where land is worth more than gold and where real estate ownership is the true power of the Black Nobility. The Orsini, through asset-holding companies —some registered in Italy, others in more discreet jurisdictions— control a significant part of the luxury rental market in the historic centre. Palaces converted into apartments for diplomats, embassies, investment funds: the old territorial empire of the Orsini has been transformed into one of the most valuable real estate portfolios in Rome.

**Prince Fabrizio Massimo and the visible line.** Prince Fabrizio Massimo —heir of the Massimo family, another great house of the Black Nobility— is often cited as the visible head of contemporary Roman aristocracy. But the Massimo are not the Orsini. The Orsini network operates with a lower profile, without a single visible head, distributed among the surviving branches of the family —the Dukes of Gravina, the Princes of Roccagorga, the Counts of Pitigliano— and their curial connections. Visibility is weakness in this game.

## The "Grey Pope" hypothesis: what the sources say and what they do not say

There is a current of analysis —mainly in alternative research circles and in traditionalist Catholic media— that maintains the existence of a "Grey Pope" (_Grey Pope_): a figure situated above both the visible Pope and the Superior General of the Society of Jesus, who would direct the affairs of the Church from the shadows. Some versions of this theory identify this "Grey Pope" as an Orsini.

There is no documentary evidence to support the existence of an operative "Grey Pope" in the sense these theories describe. What is documented —and historically indisputable— is that the Orsini have exercised a sustained influence over the papacy for more than eight hundred years, that they have placed three of their own on the throne of Peter, that they have produced thirty-four cardinals, that they possess a unique hereditary dignity —Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne—, and that their network of properties, curial contacts, and financial influence continues to operate in 21st-century Rome. The "Grey Pope" theory may be a conspiratorial exaggeration. But the question that inspires it —"who really commands in the Vatican?"— has a partial and uncomfortable answer: the same families that commanded five hundred years ago.

In the words of the British historian Peter Partner, author of _The Lands of St. Peter: The Papal State in the Middle Ages and the Early Renaissance_ (1972): _"The papacy has never been merely a spiritual institution. It has been, from its origins, an institution governed by families. And among those families, none has lasted so long or gone so far as the Orsini."_
