# The Boncompagni-Ludovisi — The Pope of the Calendar and the Pope of the Secret Conclave

---

## 15th Century — The Boncompagni: jurists from Bologna in the city of law

The Boncompagni were neither Romans nor aristocrats. They were Bolognese.

The surname Boncompagni —"good companion"— appears in the notarial registers of Bologna from the 12th century, associated with artisans and small merchants of the San Pietro quarter. But throughout the 14th and 15th centuries, the family experienced upward social mobility through law. Bologna was the seat of the oldest and most prestigious university in Europe, and the Boncompagni —who had understood that the key to power in the Italy of city-states was jurisprudence, not the sword— placed themselves in the lower and middle rungs of the pontifical administration. They produced notaries, consistorial lawyers, auditors of the Rota, and a bishop —Cristoforo Boncompagni, Archbishop of Ravenna in the 15th century.

The historian Peter Partner, in *The Lands of St. Peter* (1972), documents that the Boncompagni were, around 1500, an eminent family of the Bolognese civic elite, but without noble titles, without great fiefs, and without cardinals. The family's wealth came from their law offices and from the purchase of lands in the Emilian countryside. Their ascent was due exclusively to the meritocracy of law —a characteristic of the Papal States, where legal training was the main avenue of access to the Curia for families without feudal blood.

The man who would change the destiny of the Boncompagni was born in Bologna on 1 January 1502. His name was Ugo.

---

## 1572 — Ugo Boncompagni becomes Gregory XIII: the jurist Pope

On 13 May 1572, the conclave gathered after the death of Pius V elected Cardinal **Ugo Boncompagni** as Pope. He was seventy years old. He was a doctor of canon and civil law from the University of Bologna, where he himself had been a professor before moving to Rome. He had participated in the Council of Trent as an expert canonist, had served as judge of the Rota Tribunal, and had been sent as papal legate to the court of Philip II in Spain. He was a jurist of the first magnitude, a rigorous administrator, and a cosmopolitan European —he had lived in Bologna, Rome, Trent, and Madrid. He took the name **Gregory XIII**.

The pontificate of Gregory XIII (1572–1585) is remembered for two great achievements: the reform of the calendar —which brought him universal fame— and the construction of a system of colleges and seminaries that transformed the formation of the Catholic clergy.

Gregory XIII was a product of the Tridentine Counter-Reformation. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) had decreed the reform of the Church at all levels —doctrinal, disciplinary, administrative— and Gregory XIII was the Pope who set in motion the institutional apparatus to apply those reforms. He founded or reformed twenty-three colleges and seminaries in Rome, including the Collegio Romano —which would later bear his name: Pontifical Gregorian University—, the Collegio Greco, the Collegio Inglese, and the Collegio Ungherese. The network of national colleges created by Gregory XIII trained generations of priests who returned to their countries —England, Germany, Hungary, Greece— as agents of Catholic renewal. The historian Ludwig von Pastor, in volume XIX of his *History of the Popes*, calls Gregory XIII "the Pope who made Rome the training centre for all the Catholic clergy of the world."

But what would define his legacy forever was not a college. It was the calendar.

---

## 1582 — The calendar reform: the universal legacy of Gregory XIII

The Julian calendar, instituted by Julius Caesar in 46 BCE, contained a small error: it assumed that the solar year lasted 365.25 days, when in reality it lasts approximately 365.2422 days. The difference —about eleven minutes a year— had accumulated over more than sixteen hundred years. By 1582, the spring equinox —which determined the date of Easter— had shifted ten days from its actual astronomical position. The liturgy had become misaligned with the cosmos.

Gregory XIII, faithful to his legal training, tackled the problem with the rigour of a canonist. He assembled a commission of astronomers, mathematicians, and canonists —among them the German Jesuit Christopher Clavius and the Calabrian astronomer Aloysius Lilius— and charged them with the reform. The result was the bull **Inter gravissimas**, promulgated on 24 February 1582, which established:

- The elimination of ten days from the calendar: Thursday 4 October 1582 would be followed by Friday 15 October 1582.
- The reform of leap years: centurial years —1700, 1800, 1900— would only be leap years if divisible by 400 —which is why 1600 and 2000 were leap years, but 1700, 1800, and 1900 were not.
- The adoption of 1 January as the start of the year —in many places, the year began on 25 March or on movable dates.

The reform was implemented immediately in the Papal States, Spain, Portugal, and most Italian kingdoms. France adopted it that same year. The Protestant and Orthodox countries resisted for decades and centuries —Great Britain did not adopt the Gregorian calendar until 1752; Russia waited until 1918; Greece until 1923—, but today the Gregorian calendar is the universal civil standard. All countries in the world —including China, Japan, Saudi Arabia, and North Korea— use the calendar that bears the name of Gregory XIII.

The historian John L. Heilbron, in *The Sun in the Church: Cathedrals as Solar Observatories* (1999), reconstructs the scientific process of the reform and stresses that the precision of the Gregorian calendar is such that it only accumulates one day of error every three thousand years. The calendar that governs the dates of the global economy, international travel, contracts, and school calendars of the entire world is, in essence, the work of a Bolognese Pope of the 16th century who had studied law at the oldest university in Europe.

## 17th Century — The Ludovisi: from Bologna to Rome, from war to the throne

The Ludovisi were, like the Boncompagni, a Bolognese family of jurists and soldiers. The first documented Ludovisi is **Bertrando Ludovisi**, a 13th-century Bolognese magistrate. During the 14th and 15th centuries, the Ludovisi consolidated as a family of the civic elite of Bologna, with members in the city senate and in the pontifical administration. But the true leap of the Ludovisi occurred when a family member —**Alessandro Ludovisi**— ascended the papal throne.

Alessandro Ludovisi was born in Bologna on 9 January 1554. He was a doctor of canon and civil law from the University of Bologna —like Ugo Boncompagni— and embarked on a brilliant curial career. He was auditor of the Rota Tribunal, apostolic nuncio in Switzerland, and from 1616, cardinal archbishop of Bologna. His career was discreet and efficient: he was a competent jurist, a prudent diplomat, and a reliable administrator. When Pope Paul V died in January 1621, Alessandro Ludovisi was elected in a conclave of only three days —a speed that indicated consensus among the cardinalatial factions. He took the name **Gregory XV**.

The pontificate of Gregory XV was brief —barely two and a half years, until his death in July 1623— but produced a reform that changed the history of the Church: the regulation of the conclave.

---

## 1621–1623 — Gregory XV and the conclave reform: the bull Aeterni Patris

On 15 November 1621, Gregory XV promulgated the bull **Aeterni Patris Filius**, which established the norms of the papal conclave as they —with subsequent modifications— function to this day. The bull codified three fundamental principles:

- **The secret written vote.** Each cardinal had to write the name of his candidate on a ballot, fold it, and deposit it in a chalice placed on the altar of the Sistine Chapel. The vote was secret even within the College of Cardinals —no cardinal could see what another wrote.
- **The two-thirds majority.** Canonical election required the vote of at least two-thirds of the cardinals present, a rule that remains in force —with variations— in 2026.
- **The scrutiny and the accessus.** The bull introduced post-vote control procedures to verify that no cardinal had voted for himself.

The conclave reform responded to a practical need: to avoid the disputes, external pressures, and irregularities that had plagued papal elections during the Renaissance and the Counter-Reformation. The conclaves of the 15th and 16th centuries had often been episodes of intense political pressure —European monarchs vetoing candidates through the *ius exclusivae*, cardinals buying votes, factions prolonging conclaves for months to extract concessions from the future Pope. Gregory XV, a jurist of Bolognese training, applied the rigour of law to a process that, for centuries, had depended more on politics than on norms.

The historian Frederic J. Baumgartner, in *Behind Locked Doors: A History of the Papal Elections* (2003), argues that the bull of 1621 was "the founding moment of the modern conclave": it transformed the papal election from an opaque political process into a legally codified procedure. Although later generations added modifications —Pius X abolished the monarchs' veto in 1904; John Paul II introduced a simple majority in case of prolonged deadlock in 1996—, the basic structure of the conclave remains that which Gregory XV designed in 1621.

Gregory XV was also the Pope who established the **Congregation of Propaganda Fide** (1622), the Vatican dicastery responsible for coordinating Catholic missions in Asia, Africa, and America —the germ of modern global Catholicism. And he canonised Ignatius of Loyola, Francis Xavier, Teresa of Ávila, and Philip Neri —four of the most important saints of the Counter-Reformation— in a single ceremony in March 1622.

But his pontificate was brief. Gregory XV died on 8 July 1623, at the age of sixty-nine, of kidney disease. His nephew, Cardinal Ludovico Ludovisi —who had served as cardinal nephew during the pontificate—, inherited the task of consolidating the family's power. And he did so with the same vigour as the cardinal nephews of other papal families: accumulating land, building palaces, and, above all, creating the Villa Ludovisi.

---

## 1621–1633 — Ludovico Ludovisi and the Villa Ludovisi: paradise on the Quirinal

Cardinal **Ludovico Ludovisi** (1595–1632), nephew of Gregory XV, was created cardinal three days after his uncle's election —he was twenty-six years old— and served as cardinal nephew with formidable efficiency. In the two and a half years of Gregory XV's pontificate, Ludovico Ludovisi accumulated immense riches, bought lands in the Roman countryside, and acquired the Duchy of Zagarolo —which the Ludovisi bought from the Colonna. But his great work was not political but urbanistic and artistic.

In 1621, Ludovico bought the first plots on Monte Quirinal, northeast of the historic centre of Rome, in what were then vineyards and orchards. On those plots he built the **Villa Ludovisi**, a complex of gardens, small palaces, fountains, statues, and woods extending over an area of some thirty hectares —the equivalent of forty football pitches— between today's Via Veneto, Via di Porta Pinciana, and Via Francesco Crispi.

The villa included:

- The **Casino dell'Aurora**, decorated with a fresco by **Guercino** —Giovanni Francesco Barbieri— representing Aurora in a chariot drawn by horses, one of the most celebrated works of the Italian Baroque.
- The **Casino del Cappuccino**, decorated by **Domenichino**.
- The **Palazzo Grande**, the main building of the villa, which housed the Ludovisi art collection.
- Formal gardens with fountains, ponds, grottoes, and an amphitheatre.
- A collection of ancient statues that included the celebrated *Ludovisi Throne* —a 5th-century BCE Greek relief representing the birth of Aphrodite—, the *Ares Ludovisi*, and the *Dying Gaul*, all masterpieces of classical sculpture that are today preserved in the Museo Nazionale Romano of Palazzo Altemps.

The Ludovisi art collection, assembled by Ludovico in barely a decade, was one of the most important in Rome in ancient sculpture and Baroque painting. The cardinal bought works by Guercino, Domenichino, Guido Reni, and other artists of the Bolognese school —the same city from which the Ludovisi and the Boncompagni came—, creating a nucleus of Emilian art in the heart of Rome.

Ludovico died in 1632, at the age of thirty-seven. The Villa Ludovisi passed to his heirs, who maintained it as a residence and expanded it during the 17th and 18th centuries.

---

## 1681 — The Boncompagni-Ludovisi fusion: two dynasties unite

In 1681, **Girolamo Boncompagni**, heir of the Boncompagni, married **Ippolita Ludovisi**, heiress of the Ludovisi. The marriage fused two papal dynasties and their respective patrimonies.

The Boncompagni contributed the legacy of Gregory XIII —the calendar pope—, the Duchy of Sora, the Principality of Piombino, and various lands in Emilia-Romagna and Latium. The Ludovisi contributed the legacy of Gregory XV —the conclave pope—, the Duchy of Zagarolo, the Villa Ludovisi, and the collection of ancient and Baroque art. The fusion created the house of **Boncompagni-Ludovisi**, which became one of the principal families of the Black Nobility.

The marriage agreement stipulated, as was customary in fusions of the high papal aristocracy, that the heirs would bear both surnames and that the patrimony would be kept united through the *fidecommisso*. The Boncompagni-Ludovisi thus combined the Bolognese legal tradition —both families came from Bologna and both had produced jurist popes— with the territorial and artistic wealth accumulated in Rome.

---

## 18th Century — The Boncompagni-Ludovisi in the Roman aristocracy

During the 18th century, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi consolidated their position in the high Roman aristocracy. They participated in the pontifical court, intermarried with other families of the Black Nobility, and expanded their properties in Rome and in the Papal States.

The Villa Ludovisi continued to be one of the great lordly residences of Rome, with its gardens, its art collections, and its festivities. Grand Tour travellers —such as Goethe and Stendhal— visited the villa and admired the collection of ancient statues. But by the end of the 18th and beginning of the 19th centuries, the family began to experience financial difficulties —a common pattern for many houses of the Black Nobility, whose agricultural revenues could no longer sustain the aristocratic lifestyle.

---

## 19th Century — The end of the Villa Ludovisi and the birth of Via Veneto

In the 1880s, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi made a decision that would transform the geography of Rome: they sold the Villa Ludovisi.

The city of Rome, converted into the capital of the Kingdom of Italy in 1871, was experiencing an unprecedented real estate boom. The population was growing, the administration of the new State needed offices and residences, and the plots near the historic centre were rapidly increasing in value. The Boncompagni-Ludovisi, who owned the villa on the Quirinal —an area adjacent to the historic centre—, decided to sell the plots for urban development.

The operation was one of the greatest urban transformations of modern Rome. The gardens of the Villa Ludovisi were parcelled out and sold to property developers. On the former Baroque paradise of the Ludovisi were built palaces, hotels, embassies, and residences of the new Roman bourgeoisie. The main street that crossed the former gardens became **Via Vittorio Veneto**, which in the following decades would transform into the symbol of the Roman *dolce vita* —the cafés, the luxury hotels such as the Excelsior and the Palace, the paparazzi, and the film stars that Federico Fellini immortalised in his film *La Dolce Vita* (1960).

The Boncompagni-Ludovisi retained the **Casino dell'Aurora**, the small villa with the Guercino fresco, which remained the property of the family. And with the money from the sale —an immense sum for the era—, the family restored its finances and adapted to the new economic order of unified Italy.

The sale of the Villa Ludovisi is, in a sense, a parable of the Black Nobility in the 19th century: families that for centuries had based their wealth on land and feudal privileges found themselves forced to sell part of their patrimony in order to survive in a capitalist economy. But, unlike other families that were completely ruined, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi survived the transition and preserved a nucleus of real estate and artistic patrimony that guaranteed their continuity.

---

## 20th Century — Adaptation in republican Italy

During the 20th century, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi followed the same adaptation pattern as the rest of the Black Nobility. The abolition of noble titles by the Italian Republic in 1946 did not affect their real patrimony: the Casino dell'Aurora —with the Guercino fresco, one of the most singular artistic treasures of Rome— remained family property, as did other real estate properties in Rome and in Latium.

The family participated in Roman cultural and aristocratic life with a discreet profile. Unlike the Colonna or the Borghese —whose palaces are museums open to the public—, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi maintained a more reserved profile. The Casino dell'Aurora is not open to the public on a regular basis —only on special occasions—, which has contributed to surrounding it with an aura of mystery and exclusivity.

The Guercino fresco that decorates the ceiling of the Casino dell'Aurora —the goddess Aurora crossing the sky in her chariot— remains one of the most admired works of the Roman Baroque. And the irony is notable: of the thirty hectares of the Villa Ludovisi, only the small casino decorated by Guercino remains in the hands of the family. But that casino, with that fresco, is worth more —in terms of cultural capital— than many entire palaces.

## 2026 — The Boncompagni-Ludovisi today: the calendar, the conclave, and the Casino dell'Aurora

In 2026, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi remain one of the most singular families of the Black Nobility. Their singularity lies not in the number of popes they gave the Church —only two— nor in the extent of their territories —they lost most of the Villa Ludovisi—, but in the nature of the two popes they produced.

Gregory XIII reformed the calendar that today governs the daily life of all humanity. Gregory XV reformed the conclave that today determines the election of each Pope. Both legacies are of universal scale. The Gregorian calendar is the measure of time for the eight billion human beings who inhabit the planet —regardless of their religion, culture, or nationality. The rules of the conclave, established by the bull *Aeterni Patris Filius* of 1621, determine the election of the spiritual leader of the one billion three hundred million Catholics of the world.

Few families —perhaps none— can claim such a lasting and quotidian impact on the life of humanity. Every time someone looks at a calendar, they are using the reform of Gregory XIII. Every time white smoke announces a new Pope, the reform of Gregory XV is operating. The Boncompagni-Ludovisi do not own the largest palace in Rome nor the most visited art collection. But they own something that no other family of the Black Nobility can claim: two popes whose works structure time and the power of the Catholic Church.

The **Casino dell'Aurora**, on Via Lombardia, in the heart of the Via Veneto neighbourhood, remains the property of the family. The Guercino fresco remains on its ceiling. And the story of how a family from Bologna, which began as anonymous jurists in the courts of the university city, ended up measuring the time of the world and regulating the election of the Vicar of Christ, continues to be told —or should be told— every time a traveller pauses before the Hotel Excelsior and steps, unknowingly, on what were once the gardens of the Ludovisi.

---

## The Boncompagni-Ludovisi pattern: fusion as a survival strategy

The Boncompagni-Ludovisi represent the most extreme version of the fusion pattern that characterises the Black Nobility. If the Doria Pamphili united the sea and the throne, and the Borghese united papal nepotism with imperial marriage, the Boncompagni-Ludovisi united the two most reformist popes of the early Baroque.

Both pontificates were brief —Gregory XIII governed thirteen years; Gregory XV, two and a half years. Both popes came from Bologna, not Rome. Both were jurists trained at the same university. Both left an institutional legacy —the calendar and the conclave— that did not depend on the continuity of their family, but on the continuity of the Church. And both, ironically, belonged to families whose later fusion —the Boncompagni-Ludovisi marriage of 1681— guaranteed the survival of both lineages.

The history of the Boncompagni-Ludovisi demonstrates that in the Black Nobility, power is not always measured in hectares or palaces. Sometimes it is measured in the intangible: in the measure of time that the world uses every day and in the secret rules that elect the man who occupies the throne of St Peter.
