# The Massimo — From Hannibal's General to the Pope's Throne

---

## 3rd Century BCE — The legendary origin: the Fabii and the Cunctator

The boldest genealogical claim in European aristocracy belongs to the Massimo. The family maintains —and has maintained for centuries— that it descends in direct line from Quintus Fabius Maximus Verrucosus, called *Cunctator* —"the Delayer"—, the Roman general who saved the Republic from Hannibal. Fabius Maximus, five times consul and twice dictator between 233 and 209 BCE, devised the strategy that broke the Carthaginian campaign in Italy: avoid pitched battle, wear down the enemy, deny him the great confrontation that Hannibal needed to destroy Rome in a single blow. The *gens Fabia* was one of the oldest patrician families of the Republic, with records going back to the 5th century BCE. Fabius Maximus was its most celebrated member and, according to the Massimo, their direct ancestor.

There is no documentary evidence that allows an unbroken genealogical line to be traced from the 3rd century BCE to the Middle Ages. Modern historians —from Pompeo Litta in the 19th century to Sandro Carocci in our own— treat the claim as legendary. But the legend is not irrelevant. For more than a thousand years, the Massimo have inscribed "MAXIMVS" on their monuments, commissioned genealogies linking them to the Fabii, and invoked that antiquity as the foundation of their pre-eminence over all other Roman families —including those that produced popes. In the words of historian Claudio Rendina, the Fabian claim of the Massimo "is not verifiable, but it is constitutive of their identity: the Massimo do not need to prove that they descend from the Fabii; they need Rome to believe that they descend from them." The legend is part of the power.

---

## 10th Century — The first documented Massimo

The documented history of the Massimo begins in the Rome of the year 900, when the city was a shell of its imperial past —some 30,000 inhabitants crowded into the bend of the Tiber, surrounded by ruins that no one knew how to maintain— and the papacy was a bishopric disputed among local factions. In this context, the *de Maximis* —"the Greatest", "the Largest"— appear in Roman notarial registers as owners of lands and houses in the rione Sant'Eustachio, in the heart of the ancient city, over what had been the Stadium of Domitian —today Piazza Navona.

The first Massimo documented with certainty is **Leone de Maximis**, who appears as a witness in a deed of the year 1012 relating to the church of San Lorenzo in Damaso. In 1023, another Massimo —Stefano— appears as *consul Romanorum*, one of the magistrates of the medieval commune that governed Rome in the name of the Pope and the people. The Massimo were already, in the 11th century, part of the city's governing elite, alongside other families that would shape Roman history for centuries: the Crescenzi, the Teofilatti, the Pierleoni.

Unlike the Orsini and the Colonna —who built their power on castles, armies, and feudal alliances in the Roman countryside—, the Massimo specialised in urban power. They were magistrates, notaries, judges, and administrators of the city. They did not possess large rural fiefs or armies of their own: they possessed control of Rome's municipal institutions. That specialisation —urban power, not territorial power— defined the Massimo throughout the Middle Ages and explains many of their later decisions.

## 12th–13th Centuries — Magistrates, notaries and the art of surviving without an army

While the Orsini and the Colonna bled themselves dry in feudal wars for control of Latium, the Massimo enriched themselves in the administration of Rome. The communal records of the 12th and 13th centuries show Massimo after Massimo occupying the offices of *consul*, *senator*, and *iudex* of the city. They were not warriors: they were jurists. They did not conquer castles: they drafted contracts. They did not lead armies: they administered taxes, public works, and litigation.

This position gave them a unique advantage. The Orsini and the Colonna died out in battles, lost fiefs with each change of papacy, and were perpetually indebted by the costs of feudal warfare. The Massimo, by contrast, accumulated urban capital —properties in the centre of Rome— and institutional capital —knowledge of law, administration, and municipal politics. They did not depend on a favourable Pope or a military victory. They depended on the continuity of the city itself. And Rome, with all its crises, never ceased to exist.

In the 13th century, the Massimo were already one of the wealthiest families in Rome —not in land, but in urban real estate and loans. They possessed the *hospitale Sancti Andreae*, a complex of dwellings and workshops in the ancient Stadium of Domitian, and multiple houses in the rioni Parione and Sant'Eustachio. Their wealth was not ostentatious: it was dense, compact, urban. It could not be seen from afar. One had to enter Rome to find it.

## 1532–1536 — Palazzo Massimo alle Colonne: Peruzzi's masterpiece

On 14 May 1527, a fire destroyed the ancient Massimo palace on Via Papalis —today Corso Vittorio Emanuele. The cause was not an accident: it was the Sack of Rome. The troops of Charles V, who had been sacking the city for months, set fire to the Massimo residence during the chaotic days of the imperial assault. Pietro Massimo, head of the family at that time, died shortly afterwards.

His heir, **Angelo Massimo**, decided to rebuild. He hired the Sienese architect **Baldassarre Peruzzi** (1481–1536), one of the greats of the High Roman Renaissance, a disciple of Bramante and rival of Raphael. Peruzzi designed a palace that broke with all the conventions of Roman noble architecture.

The Palazzo Massimo alle Colonne —so called for the columns of its curved façade— was built between 1532 and 1536. Its most astonishing feature is the convex façade, which follows the curvature of the ancient Via Papalis instead of imposing a straight line. It is an architectural decision without precedent in 16th-century Rome: instead of straightening the street —which would have required demolishing neighbouring buildings—, Peruzzi made the palace adapt to the street. The curvature creates an effect of movement, as if the building were leaning towards the passer-by. The inner courtyard, with its portico of Doric columns, is one of the masterpieces of Renaissance classicism. The façade, with its rustic rustication on the ground floor and its elegantly framed windows on the piano nobile, already heralds the Mannerism that would dominate Roman architecture in the following decades.

The Palazzo Massimo alle Colonne remains the property of the Massimo family in 2026. It is one of the few great Renaissance palaces of Rome that has never changed hands. The architectural historian James S. Ackerman, in his study of the palace, described it as "the most original work of domestic architecture of the Roman Renaissance."

## 16th Century — Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne: the curial consecration

In 1560, Pope Pius IV created the dignity of **Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne** —*Princeps Assistens Solio*— and granted it jointly to the Massimo and the Colonna. Later, the Orsini joined the privilege. The office, as documented in the text on the Orsini, placed the prince assistant physically beside the papal throne during pontifical ceremonies —the layperson closest to the Pope in the entire Catholic world.

For the Massimo, the office had a special meaning. Unlike the Orsini and the Colonna, who had produced popes, cardinals, and generals for the Church, the Massimo never produced a Pope. Their power was not ecclesiastical but civil. The title of Prince Assistant recognised that lay antiquity and incorporated it into the symbolic architecture of the papacy. The Massimo did not serve the Pope as ecclesiastics: they served him as laypeople, from a position of precedence that not even the cardinals questioned.

The office carries another historical privilege: the custody of the **Conclave Key**. During papal elections, the Massimo prince was —and symbolically still is— the keeper of one of the keys that seal the enclosure where the cardinals elect the new Pope. It is a ceremonial vestige, but revealing: the family that claims descent from the Fabii is also symbolic guardian of the door to the throne of St Peter.

## 17th–19th Centuries — The family that survived everything

The Massimo produced no popes. They commanded no armies. They accumulated no duchies. And yet, they survived every regime that Rome knew between the Renaissance and Italian unification. The Roman Republic of 1798 —established by Napoleonic troops— confiscated goods of the Church and of papal families, but the Massimo, with their low profile and urban roots, preserved their palace. The Restoration of 1814 returned their position at the pontifical court. The ephemeral and revolutionary Roman Republic of 1849 did not touch them.

On 20 September 1870, when the troops of the Kingdom of Italy entered through Porta Pia and Pius IX declared himself a prisoner in the Vatican, the Massimo closed the doors of the Palazzo Massimo alle Colonne, like all families of the Black Nobility. But their mourning was less dramatic than that of the Colonna or the Orsini. The Massimo had never depended on the Papal States —which were disappearing— but on the city of Rome —which remained. The palace was on the same street, with the same urban revenues. The fall of the Pope's temporal power was a political trauma, not a financial disaster for the Massimo.

---

## 1929–1968 — The Lateran Treaty and the abolition of the papal court

The Lateran Pacts of 1929 resolved the "Roman Question" and recognised the sovereignty of the Holy See over the Vatican. For the Massimo, it was a new equilibrium: Vatican citizens, residents in Rome, with a palace on Corso Vittorio Emanuele —Italian soil— and diplomatic status.

The apostolic constitution *Pontificalis Domus* of Paul VI in 1968 abolished the pontifical court and the hereditary offices that composed it. The title of Prince Assistant to the Pontifical Throne ceased to have official functions. But, as in the case of the Colonna and the Orsini, the abolition was more ritual than real. The Massimo retain the title on an honorary basis. The Conclave Key remains a tradition that the Vatican respects. And the palace, as always, still stands.

---

## 2026 — Prince Fabrizio Massimo-Brancaccio and discretion as strategy

In 2026, the head of the Massimo family is **Prince Fabrizio Massimo-Brancaccio**, who resides with his family in the piano nobile of the Palazzo Massimo alle Colonne. The palace opens some of its halls to the public on special occasions —Culture Week, some Saturday mornings— but remains, fundamentally, a private residence. It is one of the few Renaissance palaces in Rome that has not been converted into a museum, embassy, or hotel. The family inhabits it.

The contemporary Massimo embody the paradox of the Black Nobility in the 21st century. They have no formal political power. They do not control Vatican dicasteries. They do not sit on the boards of large corporations. But they own one of the most valuable properties in the historic centre of Rome, maintain a network of relationships encompassing the Curia, international diplomacy, and the European aristocracy, and operate with a discretion that is, in itself, a form of power.

In the words of British historian David Gilmour, author of *The Pursuit of Italy: A History of a Land, Its Regions and Their Peoples* (2011), the Massimo represent "the purest form of urban aristocracy: without armies, without duchies, without popes —only a palace on the oldest street in Rome and the unshakeable conviction that they were there before everyone else."

---

## The Massimo position: between legend and permanence

The Massimo occupy a unique position among the families of the Black Nobility. The Colonna produced a Pope and dozens of cardinals. The Orsini produced three Popes and thirty-four cardinals. The Borghese produced a Pope, a patron cardinal, and an art collection that defines the Baroque. The Farnese created the Jesuits, the Council of Trent, and an imperial duchy. The Massimo did none of that.

And yet, none of those families can claim what the Massimo claim: the antiquity of the Fabii, continuity from Republican Rome, pre-existence to the papacy itself. The Massimo claim to have been in Rome before the Pope existed, before St Peter existed, before Christianity set foot in the eternal city. That claim, verifiable or not, gives them a prestige that neither Popes nor cardinals can match. The Pope is the Vicar of Christ. The Massimo are the memory of the consuls.

That position —ancient, but not ecclesiastical; noble, but not papal; powerful, but discreet— is probably the reason for their longevity. The Massimo were never threatening enough to be exterminated, nor irrelevant enough to be ignored. They were exactly where they needed to be: in the centre of Rome, in Peruzzi's curved palace, with the conclave key in their pocket and two thousand years of history —real or claimed— behind them.
