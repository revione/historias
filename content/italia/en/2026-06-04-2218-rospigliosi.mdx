# The Rospigliosi — Two Years of Pope, Centuries of Wealth

---

## 12th–16th Centuries — Origins in Pistoia: Tuscan Wool Merchants

The Rospigliosi hail from Pistoia, the Tuscan city situated at the foot of the Apennines, halfway between Florence and Lucca. Pistoia was, during the Late Middle Ages and the Renaissance, a center of textile production: its workshops produced high-quality woolen cloth exported throughout Europe, and its merchants competed —on a smaller scale— with those of Florence and Lucca for control of the wool trade of Tuscany.

The surname Rospigliosi appears for the first time in the notarial records of Pistoia in the 12th century, linked to commercial activities. During the 13th to 15th centuries, the Rospigliosi consolidated themselves as a family of the Pistoian mercantile bourgeoisie: they traded in wool, cloth, and dyes, owned textile workshops in the city and lands in the Tuscan countryside, and participated in the municipal government of Pistoia —one of the city-republics of medieval Tuscany, though eclipsed by the hegemonic power of Florence—. They were a prosperous but second-rank family, without noble titles, without great palaces, and without a presence in the Roman Curia.

In the 16th century, the Rospigliosi began to ascend socially through service to the Medici —the dukes of Tuscany, who governed the region from Florence— and through the Church. Some members of the family entered the Medicean administration; others, the Tuscan clergy. The definitive leap occurred in the generation of the man who would carry the surname Rospigliosi to the throne of Peter.

---

## 1600–1667 — Giulio Rospigliosi: The Poet Who Became Pope

**Giulio Rospigliosi** was born in Pistoia on January 28, 1600. He was the son of a textile merchant who had prospered enough to send him to study at the best institutions of Tuscany. Giulio was educated at the seminary of Pistoia and later at the University of Pisa, where he earned a doctorate in theology and canon law. He was a man of letters: he wrote poetry in Latin and Italian, composed librettos for operas —he was the author of the first comic opera libretto in history, *Chi soffre speri*, performed in 1637— and frequented the literary circles of Baroque Rome.

The ecclesiastical career of Giulio Rospigliosi was, for several decades, that of a competent diplomat and administrator in the service of the Barberini popes. Urban VIII —the humanist Pope who loved poetry as much as power— took him under his protection, appointed him secretary of the Congregation of Rites, and sent him as apostolic nuncio to the court of Philip IV in Madrid between 1644 and 1653. The nunciature in Spain —the most important and difficult in pontifical diplomacy— was a trial by fire for any prelate, and Rospigliosi passed it successfully: he maintained stable relations with the Spanish monarchy during the turbulent years of the Thirty Years' War, the Peace of Westphalia, and the crisis of the Spanish monarchy.

In 1657, Alexander VII Chigi created him a cardinal and appointed him Secretary of State —the highest office of the Curia after the Pope—. Rospigliosi served as Secretary of State during the last ten years of the pontificate of Alexander VII, managing Vatican diplomacy in the final phase of the war between Spain and France that culminated in the Peace of the Pyrenees (1659). He was an experienced diplomat, a refined humanist, and a career ecclesiastic. But, unlike other papabile cardinals —the Barberini, the Chigi, the Altieri—, Rospigliosi was not the head of a great papal dynasty. He was, simply, a Rospigliosi from Pistoia.

On June 20, 1667, after the long pontificate of Alexander VII —twelve years— the conclave elected Giulio Rospigliosi Pope. He was sixty-seven years old. He took the name **Clement IX**. His pontificate would be one of the briefest in history —barely two years, until his death on December 9, 1669—. But in those two years, the Rospigliosi transformed themselves from textile merchants of Pistoia into princes of the Black Nobility.

## 1667–1669 — Two Years of Pontificate: The Rospigliosi Pattern of Accelerated Extraction

The pontificate of Clement IX lasted barely two years, five months, and nineteen days. But the brevity of the pontificate was not an obstacle to the enrichment of the Rospigliosi family. On the contrary: the imminence of the Pope's death —who was sixty-seven years old and did not enjoy good health— accelerated the extraction operations. The Rospigliosi pattern is, in a sense, the opposite of the Barberini pattern: while the Barberini had twenty-one years to accumulate wealth under Urban VIII, the Rospigliosi had only two. And in those two years, they acted with remarkable efficiency.

Clement IX's principal nephew, **Giacomo Rospigliosi**, was made a cardinal immediately after the Pope's election —a practice still common in 1667, before the anti-nepotistic reforms of Innocent XI and Innocent XII—. Giacomo assumed the office of Cardinal Nephew and began managing the mechanisms of family enrichment: accumulation of ecclesiastical benefices —rents from dioceses, abbeys, and monasteries—, control of curial appointments, and acquisition of properties in Rome and in the Roman countryside.

Another nephew, **Camillo Rospigliosi**, received the title of Prince and was appointed commander of the Pontifical Guard —the military post that was traditionally granted to the Pope's lay nephew—. Camillo was responsible for consolidating the family's territorial patrimony in Latium and Tuscany.

In two years, the Rospigliosi brothers —Giacomo, the cardinal nephew, and Camillo, the lay prince— managed to accumulate enough capital to build the **Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini** on the Quirinal, one of the most beautiful Baroque palaces in Rome, and to buy lands and fiefs in Latium that guaranteed the economic continuity of the family during the following centuries.

The pontificate of Clement IX, despite its brevity, was not devoid of political relevance. Clement IX attempted to mediate in the war between France and Spain that culminated in the Peace of Aachen (1668) —the treaty that ended Louis XIV's War of Devolution against the Spanish Netherlands—. But his role in international politics was limited: a two-year pontificate does not allow time to unfold a coherent foreign policy.

The most lasting legacy of Clement IX, however, was not diplomatic but artistic. And that legacy materialized in a bridge and nine angels.

## 1667–1669 — Clement IX and Bernini: The Angels of the Ponte Sant'Angelo

Despite the brevity of his pontificate, Clement IX left an indelible artistic mark on Rome. His most famous commission was the ten marble angels that decorate the **Ponte Sant'Angelo**, the bridge leading to the Castel Sant'Angelo, over the Tiber River, by which pilgrims approached Saint Peter's Basilica.

Clement IX commissioned **Gian Lorenzo Bernini** —the greatest artist of the Baroque, who had already served Urban VIII, Innocent X, and Alexander VII— to execute the statues of angels holding the instruments of the Passion of Christ: the cross, the crown of thorns, the nails, the lance, the column of the flagellation. The ten angels line up on both sides of the bridge, creating a processional and symbolic journey that transforms a 2nd-century Roman bridge —built by the emperor Hadrian— into a spiritual voyage toward the tomb of Saint Peter.

Bernini personally sculpted two of the angels —the *Angel with the Crown of Thorns* and the *Angel with the Inscription*—, which were so beautiful that Clement IX ordered them not to be placed on the bridge but to be preserved in a church and copies installed in their place. Bernini's originals are preserved today in the church of Sant'Andrea delle Fratte, in Rome. The other eight angels were executed by Bernini's disciples —Ercole Ferrata, Antonio Raggi, Cosimo Fancelli, among others— following the master's designs.

The Ponte Sant'Angelo with its angels is today one of the most photographed places in Rome and one of the most visited works of public art in the world. Every tourist who crosses the bridge toward the Castel Sant'Angelo walks among the statues commissioned by a Rospigliosi Pope. And that is, in a way, the paradox of Clement IX: a very brief pontificate, almost forgotten in the history manuals of the Church, but a bridge that millions of people cross every year.

Clement IX was also a Pope who loved music and theater. He wrote opera librettos, protected musicians and composers, and sponsored theatrical performances at the pontifical court. He was, in that sense, a product of the Roman Baroque: a Pope who understood that artistic beauty was a form of power and that marble angels preached as effectively as sermons.

## 1670–1676 — The Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini: The Palace of the Aurora

The **Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini**, located on the Quirinal —the highest of Rome's hills—, is the most important architectural legacy of the Rospigliosi. Built during and after the pontificate of Clement IX, on a preexisting building, the palace was conceived as the family's Roman residence and as an architectural manifesto of their new status: the textile merchants of Pistoia were now Roman princes, and they needed a palace to prove it.

The palace was designed by the architects **Flaminio Ponzio** and **Giovanni Vasanzio**, with later expansions. But the most famous element of the Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini is not the exterior architecture, but the interior decoration —and, specifically, a single painting—.

On the ceiling of the *Casino dell'Aurora*, the pleasure pavilion built in the palace gardens —which originally formed part of the grounds of the neighboring Villa Ludovisi, before its urbanization—, is the fresco of the ***Aurora*** painted by **Guido Reni** in 1614. The work, considered one of the absolute masterpieces of Italian Baroque painting, represents the goddess Aurora —the personification of dawn— driving a chariot drawn by winged horses, preceded by the morning star and followed by the dancing Hours. The fresco is painted with an illusionistic technique —the *quadro riportato*— that simulates a framed canvas on the ceiling, and its silvery and golden chromaticism produces an effect of morning light that has fascinated visitors for more than four centuries.

Guido Reni's *Aurora* was not commissioned by the Rospigliosi —it was painted in 1614 for Cardinal Scipione Borghese, who then owned the grounds—. But when the Rospigliosi bought the palace in the 17th century, the Aurora became their most precious jewel. The fresco is today the most famous work of art of the Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini and one of the most admired paintings in Rome —it competes in beauty with Guercino's *Aurora* fresco in the Casino Ludovisi, and with Pietro da Cortona's *Triumph of Divine Providence* in the Palazzo Barberini—.

The palace later passed —by inheritance and marriage— to the **Pallavicini** family, which added its surname to it. In 2026, the Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini remains private property and is partially open to the public through guided tours that allow visitors to admire Guido Reni's fresco. The tourists who visit the Quirinal to see the Baroque frescoes tread, unknowingly, the palace built by the nephews of a Pope whose pontificate lasted only two years.

## 18th–19th Centuries — The Rospigliosi in the Black Nobility: Centuries of Consolidated Aristocracy

The Rospigliosi did not produce another Pope —no subsequent conclave ever elected a family member again— but they consolidated themselves as a house of the Roman Black Nobility through marriages with the great papal families: the Pallavicini, the Colonna, the Doria Pamphili, the Orsini. The surname Rospigliosi-Pallavicini reflects one of these dynastic fusions, typical of the papal aristocracy, which guaranteed the continuity of the lineage and of the patrimony.

The family maintained the Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini, rural properties in Latium and in Tuscany —in the area of Pistoia, their place of origin— and noble titles granted by the popes. During the 18th and 19th centuries, the Rospigliosi participated in the social and cultural life of the Roman aristocracy, occupied honorary positions at the pontifical court, and cultivated artistic patronage —following the tradition of Clement IX, the poet Pope and lover of art—.

The family produced a few more cardinals —although none reached the papacy—, such as **Felice Rospigliosi**, created cardinal in 1773, and **Giacomo Rospigliosi**, cardinal in the 19th century. But the principal legacy of the Rospigliosi is not curial: it is artistic. The angels of the Ponte Sant'Angelo and Guido Reni's *Aurora* are their true monuments. Every visitor who crosses the bridge over the Tiber or who admires the ceiling of the Casino Rospigliosi is, unknowingly, before the legacy of a family of merchants from Pistoia who had a Pope for two years.

## 20th–21st Centuries — The Rospigliosi Today: The Palace, the Fresco, and the Angels

In 2026, the Rospigliosi-Pallavicini remain a family of the high Roman aristocracy. They maintain ownership of the Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini on the Quirinal —with Guido Reni's fresco— and manage the guided tours that allow the public to admire the *Aurora*. Their public profile is discreet —as befits most families of the contemporary Black Nobility— but their mark on Rome is visible to anyone who crosses the Ponte Sant'Angelo.

Bernini's angels on the bridge are probably the most democratic legacy of a papal family: they are not in a closed museum nor do they require admission. They are on a public bridge, under the sky of Rome, available to any passerby twenty-four hours a day. Clement IX, the poet Pope, chose to decorate a bridge —not a church, nor a palace, nor a private chapel— because he knew that bridges are crossed, inhabited, photographed, and remembered. And he was right: his angels are today one of the symbols of Rome.

The Rospigliosi embody, better than any other papal family, the speed of nepotistic enrichment and the durability of cultural capital. Two years of Pope. Two centuries —and counting— of wealth, palaces, and frescoes. That is, ultimately, what it means to be a family of the Black Nobility: to convert the ephemeral power of the pontificate into marble, painting, bronze, and titles that outlive the Pope who created them.

---

## The Rospigliosi Pattern: Short, Intense, and Profitable

The Rospigliosi represent the most extreme version of the accelerated extraction pattern of the Black Nobility. Other papal families —the Barberini, the Borghese, the Chigi— had years or decades to accumulate wealth. The Rospigliosi had only two. And in those two years, their nephews —Giacomo, the cardinal, and Camillo, the prince— accumulated enough to build a palace on the Quirinal, buy fiefs in Latium, and consolidate the family in the Roman aristocracy.

The Rospigliosi pattern is, in a way, the purest of the Black Nobility because it lays bare the mechanism: a Pope who governs for a short time means a nephew who extracts much in a short time. Clement IX was not a corrupt Pope —he did not enrich himself personally, he did not strip monuments like Urban VIII with the Pantheon, he did not empty the pontifical treasury in a private war—. But his family did take advantage of the window of opportunity. And the Palazzo Rospigliosi-Pallavicini, with its *Aurora* by Guido Reni, is the testimony that, in Baroque Rome, two years of Pope were enough for a family of textile merchants from Pistoia to become princes.
