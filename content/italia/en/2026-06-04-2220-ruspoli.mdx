# The Ruspoli — Princes by the Emperor, Not the Pope

---

## 13th–15th Centuries — Origins in Florence and Siena: Between the Two Tuscan Republics

The Ruspoli hail from inner Tuscany, from that strip of hills and valleys between Florence and Siena that during the Late Middle Ages was one of the richest and most conflictive territories in Europe. The surname Ruspoli appears in Florentine and Sienese documents from the 13th century as a family of the mercantile bourgeoisie —wool producers, merchants, bankers— orbiting between the two rival city-republics.

The first Ruspoli to leave documentary traces was a family member registered in Florence at the beginning of the 14th century, linked to the Arte della Lana —the wool guild, the most powerful of republican Florence—. Like so many families of pre-Medicean Tuscany, the Ruspoli participated in the city government, occupied positions in the magistracies, and accumulated capital through trade. They were not aristocrats by blood nor feudal lords. They were burghers of the *popolo grasso*, the class that governed the Tuscan city-republics before the Medici monopolized power.

Toward the end of the 15th century, one branch of the Ruspoli moved to Siena, where the family prospered in the wool and cloth trade. Siena was then an independent republic, rival of Florence, and its banking system —which gave rise to Monte dei Paschi in 1472— was one of the most sophisticated in Italy. The Sienese Ruspoli accumulated wealth and established connections that, two centuries later, would open the doors of the Roman nobility to them.

The historian Claudio Donati, in *L'idea di nobiltà in Italia: secoli XIV–XVIII* (Laterza, 1988), places the Ruspoli among the families of the Tuscan mercantile elite who carried out a successful transition from communal bourgeoisie to titled nobility during the 16th and 17th centuries.

---

## 16th Century — Military Service: The Ruspoli Take Up the Sword

What distinguishes the Ruspoli from other Black Nobility families is the route they chose to ascend. The Orsini and Colonna were medieval barons with their own armies. The Borghese, Barberini, and Aldobrandini reached power through the Papacy and nepotism. The Chigi and Sacchetti arrived through papal banking. The Ruspoli arrived through the sword —but not the sword of the feudal baron, but the sword of the officer in the service of the Emperor.

During the 16th century, the Ruspoli progressively abandoned commerce and embraced the military career in the service of the Holy Roman Empire. Serving the Emperor was a perfectly codified path of social ascent in Habsburg Europe: the imperial wars in Italy, Flanders, Hungary, and the Mediterranean demanded officers, and the military service nobility could ascend through the ranks of the imperial army, receive lands, titles, and pensions from the Emperor, and —ultimately— integrate into the European aristocracy.

The military service of the Ruspoli is inscribed in a broader context: that of the Tuscan nobility that, after losing political power in their city-republics under Medicean dominion, sought horizons of ascent in the Habsburg empires. The British historian John Hale, in *War and Society in Renaissance Europe* (1985), documents how 16th-century Italy exported officers to all European armies.

---

## 1598–1682 — Francesco Ruspoli, the Emperor's Soldier

The man who transformed the Ruspoli from Tuscan burghers to imperial princes was **Francesco Ruspoli** (1598–1682). Francesco was a career soldier: he served in the armies of the Holy Roman Empire during the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), fought in the campaigns of Italy and Flanders, and reached the rank of colonel in the service of Emperor Ferdinand III of Habsburg.

Francesco Ruspoli was not a mercenary. He was a Tuscan nobleman who had chosen the career of arms as his path of social ascent, and who rendered diplomatic and military services to the Emperor for decades. His loyalty to the House of Habsburg was rewarded with honors, pensions, and, above all, the imperial consideration that would allow his son to make the definitive leap to titled nobility.

The Italian military historian Piero Pieri, in *Il Rinascimento e la crisi militare italiana* (1952), mentions the Tuscan officers in the service of the Empire as a characteristic phenomenon of the crisis of the Italian communal nobility: families that no longer had power in their republics became servants of the great European empires. Francesco Ruspoli is an example of this process.

Francesco invested the fortune obtained in imperial service in properties in Latium and Rome, laying the territorial foundations of the future princely family. He bought lands, built relations with the Curia, and placed his sons in positions that would guarantee the continuity of the family.

---

## 1709 — Princes of the Holy Empire: The Title That Distinguishes Them

In 1709, Emperor **Joseph I of Habsburg** granted the Ruspoli the title of **Princes of the Holy Roman Empire**. The beneficiary was **Alessandro Ruspoli**, son of Francesco, who had continued the tradition of military and diplomatic service to the House of Habsburg. The title was hereditary and made the Ruspoli members of the high nobility of the Reich, with precedence over most families of the Roman Black Nobility —whose titles came from the Pope, not from the Emperor—.

The concession of the imperial title to the Ruspoli in 1709 is significant for several reasons. First, it demonstrates that there existed an alternative route to the Papacy for accessing the high nobility in Italy. Second, it places the Ruspoli in a lineage of Italian families that served the Empire —like the Pallavicini or the Gonzaga— and received titles in exchange. Third, it establishes an internal hierarchy within the Black Nobility: imperial princes like the Ruspoli could claim a rank superior to that of pontifical princes like the Borghese or Barberini.

The Austrian historian Johann Rainer, in *Der Adel im Heiligen Römischen Reich* (1979), documents the concessions of imperial titles to Italian families as a Habsburg strategy to maintain influence in Italy after the loss of Spanish hegemony. The Ruspoli were beneficiaries of that strategy.

---

## 1709–1806 — Princes of Cerveteri: The Etruscans, Rome, and the Imperial Fief

Along with the princely title, the Ruspoli received the feudal investiture of the **Principality of Cerveteri**, a territory on the coast of Latium, northwest of Rome, which had been one of the most important cities of the Etruscan civilization —the ancient Caere— and which preserved monuments, necropolises, and archaeological treasures of pre-Roman Italy.

Cerveteri was an imperial fief, not a papal one. That distinction is crucial: the Ruspoli were not vassals of the Holy See, but of the Emperor, which gave them a different legal status from the traditional Roman barons. As princes of Cerveteri, the Ruspoli governed a territory with seigneurial rights —administration of justice, collection of taxes, military levies— that depended directly on the Emperor.

The fief of Cerveteri included agricultural lands, forests, hunting grounds, and, above all, the Etruscan ruins that from the 18th century began to attract archaeologists, antiquarians, and travelers of the European Grand Tour. The Ruspoli became custodians —sometimes unwilling— of one of the most important archaeological sites in Italy, a position that linked them to European scholarship and the intellectual circles of the Enlightenment.

---

## 17th–18th Centuries — The Palazzo Ruspoli on Via del Corso: The Facade of the Imperial Princes

The **Palazzo Ruspoli** is one of the most singular buildings of Baroque Rome. Situated at number 418 of the Via del Corso —the main artery of the historic center of Rome—, the palace has one of the longest facades in the city: over one hundred meters in length, with a three-story structure, stone-framed windows, a large central portal, and a monumental marble staircase designed by the architect **Martino Longhi the Younger**.

The palace was originally built in the 16th century for the Jacobilli family, remodeled by the Rucellai, and finally acquired by the Ruspoli in 1713, shortly after receiving the princely title. The Ruspoli commissioned Longhi to renovate the building, which became the main residence of the family in Rome and one of the architectural landmarks of the Via del Corso.

Unlike other palaces of the Black Nobility —the Palazzo Borghese, the Palazzo Barberini, the Palazzo Doria Pamphili—, the Palazzo Ruspoli was not built by a cardinal nephew nor financed with ecclesiastical rents. It was bought and remodeled with money earned from imperial service. Longhi's marble staircase —one of the most spectacular in Rome— is the symbol of a family that rose socially without passing through the conclave.

The palace still belongs to the Ruspoli in 2026. It houses private apartments of the family, rented spaces, and, occasionally, cultural events and exhibitions.

---

## 18th–19th Centuries — The Ruspoli in the Black Nobility: Between Two Loyalties

The Ruspoli pose a singular case within the Black Nobility: how could one be loyal to the Pope when the princely title came from the Emperor? During the 18th and 19th centuries, the Ruspoli resolved this ambiguity through a strategy of balance: they maintained their historical loyalty to the House of Habsburg —which, after all, remained the preeminent Catholic dynasty— but integrated fully into the pontifical court and the society of the Roman Black Nobility.

The Ruspoli participated in the ceremonies of the papal court, occupied honorary positions in the Vatican —such as that of *Camerieri Segreti di Spada e Cappa*, the Pope's lay chamberlains—, and connected matrimonially with other families of the Black Nobility. The family joined the Orsini, the Colonna, the Doria, and the Pallavicini through a marriage policy that cemented their position in the Roman aristocracy.

During the Risorgimento, the Ruspoli —as imperial princes— found themselves in a complex situation. Italian unification meant the annexation of Rome and the abolition of the Papal States, affecting the Black Nobility as a whole. But the Ruspoli also had ties to the Habsburgs, defeated by the Kingdom of Italy in the wars of unification. The family opted for discretion: they maintained loyalty to the Pope as Roman princes and avoided the political activism that characterized other families.

---

## 20th–21st Centuries — The Ruspoli in Republican Italy

In the 20th century, the Ruspoli followed the trajectory of the Black Nobility: loss of political power, preservation of artistic and architectural heritage, and adaptation to the Italian Republic. The title of Prince Ruspoli was recognized by the Kingdom of Italy and, after the proclamation of the republic in 1946, became a private noble title without official recognition but fully valid in the social and cultural sphere of the Italian aristocracy.

In 2026, the Ruspoli continue to exist as a princely family. The title of **Prince Ruspoli** —Prince of the Holy Empire and Prince of Cerveteri— is transmitted hereditarily. The family preserves the Palazzo Ruspoli on the Via del Corso and properties in Latium, including lands tied to the ancient fief of Cerveteri. The Necropolis of Banditaccia —the main Etruscan site of Cerveteri— is today a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the most visited archaeological sites in Italy. Although the Ruspoli no longer own the site —it has been property of the Italian state since the 20th century—, their name remains associated with ancient Caere.

The contemporary Ruspoli maintain a discreet public profile, centered on the administration of their heritage and participation in Roman cultural life. They are not a political power as three centuries ago, but their surname remains recognizable in the circles of Italian and European aristocracy.

---

## The Imperial Route: The Sword, Not the Pope

The Ruspoli represent a virtuous anomaly in the history of the Black Nobility: they ascended to the Roman aristocracy without passing through the conclave, without a cardinal nephew, and without ecclesiastical rents. Their ascent was military, not curial. They served the Emperor on the battlefields of the Thirty Years' War, received a princely title from the hands of the Habsburgs, and built a palace on the Corso with money earned fighting for the Empire.

This trajectory distinguishes them from all other families of the Roman Black Nobility. The Orsini and Colonna were barons of Latium since the Middle Ages. The Borghese, Barberini, Aldobrandini, Pamphili, and Chigi are direct products of papal nepotism. The Pallavicini were medieval imperial princes who later became bankers. Only the Ruspoli reached the summit of the Roman aristocracy by entering through the door of military service to the Emperor.

In a city where all power emanated from the Pope, the Ruspoli demonstrated that one could also be a prince by serving the Emperor. And their marble palace on the Via del Corso, with the longest facade in Rome, is the proof that the sword also bought stone.
