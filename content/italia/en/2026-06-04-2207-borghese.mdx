# The Borghese — From Papal Nephew to Napoleon's Brother-in-Law

---

## 16th Century — From Siena to Rome: the origins of a family of jurists

The Borghese were not Romans. They were Sienese.

The family came from the city of Siena, where they had served as magistrates, notaries, and jurists for generations. The first Borghese documented with certainty is **Tiezzo da Monticiano**, a 13th-century Sienese jurist whose descendants adopted the surname Borghese —possibly derived from *borghese*, "bourgeois", indicating their urban and not feudal origin. During the 14th and 15th centuries, the Borghese of Siena produced lawyers, judges, diplomats, and a bishop, but nothing that foreshadowed what was to come.

The man who changed the family's destiny was **Marcantonio Borghese** (1504–1574), a renowned jurist who moved to Rome in 1537. Marcantonio placed himself at the service of Pope Paul III Farnese as a consistorial lawyer —a position of high legal responsibility in the Curia— and later served Popes Julius III, Marcellus II, and Paul IV. His legal competence was exceptional: he drafted opinions on canon law, represented the Holy See in international litigation, and earned the trust of cardinals and popes alike. He was not a noble: he was a legal technician. But in Renaissance Rome, a legal technician with access to the Curia was more valuable than a count with a castle.

Marcantonio bought the family's first palace in Rome, in the rione Campo Marzio, and placed his sons in positions of the pontifical administration. He had seven sons. Two of them changed the history of Rome. One was Camillo. The other, Giovanni Battista. The first would become Pope. The second would sire the cardinal who built the gallery.

---

## 1605 — Camillo Borghese becomes Paul V

On 16 May 1605, the conclave gathered after the death of Leo XI elected Cardinal **Camillo Borghese** as Pope. He was fifty-two years old. He was a doctor of canon and civil law from the University of Padua, had served as auditor of the Apostolic Chamber, vice-legate in Bologna, and cardinal priest of San Crisogono. He was a jurist, an administrator, a man of the curial apparatus —not a saint nor a warrior. He took the name **Paul V**.

The historian Ludwig von Pastor, in his monumental *History of the Popes*, describes Paul V as a pontiff of "sincere but not mystical piety, formidable administrative energy, and an unshakeable conviction in papal authority." He governed the Church for sixteen years —until his death in January 1621— in one of the most turbulent periods of post-Tridentine Europe: the Thirty Years' War had just erupted, the dispute between Catholics and Protestants was tearing apart the Holy Roman Empire, and the Pope's authority was being challenged by emerging absolute monarchies —France, Spain, England.

Paul V responded with a policy of uncompromising institutional affirmation. He completed the façade of St Peter's —the most visible part of the most important temple in Christendom— and added the longitudinal nave designed by Carlo Maderno. He built the Borghese Chapel —or Pauline Chapel— in Santa Maria Maggiore, the largest Marian basilica in Rome, where he placed the icon of the *Salus Populi Romani* and reserved the right to bury his own. He reformed the College of Cardinals, strengthened the Congregation of the Holy Office, and published the Roman Ritual of 1614 which would unify the liturgy for centuries.

But what would define his legacy was not theology or diplomacy. It was what he did with his family.

---

## 1605 — The cardinal nephew: Scipione Borghese and the machine of nepotism

Scarcely elected Pope, Paul V appointed his nephew **Scipione Borghese** (1577–1633) as cardinal. Scipione was twenty-eight years old. He was the son of Francesco Caffarelli and Ortensia Borghese —the Pope's sister— and had been adopted by Camillo Borghese years earlier, taking his uncle's surname. Paul V's decision was not original —all Renaissance and Baroque popes appointed cardinal nephews—, but few cardinal nephews accumulated as much power as Scipione Borghese.

The office of **Cardinal Nephew** —*Cardinalis Nepos*— was an informal institution but perfectly codified in curial practice. The cardinal nephew served as de facto Secretary of State, controlled access to the Pope, managed diplomatic correspondence, supervised pontifical finances, and negotiated the political alliances of the Holy See. He was, in essence, the Pope's prime minister. And like all papal prime ministers, he used his position to enrich his family.

Scipione Borghese received from his uncle a cascade of ecclesiastical benefices: the abbey of San Gregorio al Celio, the diocese of Rieti, the legation of Avignon, the abbey of Subiaco, the commendam of Santa Maria in Trastevere. The combined revenues from these benefices exceeded 140,000 scudi per year —a colossal fortune for the era, equivalent to the income of some small European kingdoms. With that money, Scipione did three things: he accumulated art, built a villa, and cemented the Borghese fortune as one of the most solid of the Roman aristocracy.

---

## 1606–1621 — The Borghese collection: how a cardinal nephew created a museum

Cardinal Scipione Borghese was one of the greatest art collectors in history. His method was not that of a patient patron who commissions works from young artists: it was that of a voracious accumulator who used papal power to confiscate, buy at forced prices, and appropriate entire collections.

In 1607, Scipione confiscated the collection of the painter Giuseppe Cesari —called the *Cavaliere d'Arpino*— on the pretext that Cesari possessed weapons illegally. Among the seized works were two paintings by a young Michelangelo Merisi, better known as **Caravaggio**: the *Boy with a Basket of Fruit* and the *Sick Bacchus*. Both works remain today in the Galleria Borghese.

That same year, Scipione commissioned from Caravaggio the *Madonna dei Palafrenieri*, which he rejected as indecorous, but the painter's other works —*St Jerome Writing*, *David with the Head of Goliath*— entered his collection through the usual channels: purchases, forced donations, transfers from churches to the palace.

But Caravaggio was only one part. Scipione bought works by Raphael —*Lady with a Unicorn*, *The Deposition*—, by Titian —*Sacred and Profane Love*, the most famous painting in the collection—, by Correggio —the *Danaë*—, by Antonello da Messina, by Botticelli, by Giovanni Bellini. In sculpture, he commissioned from a young **Gian Lorenzo Bernini** —who was not yet the Bernini of St Peter's and the colonnade— his first masterpieces: the *Aeneas, Anchises and Ascanius* (1619), the *Rape of Proserpina* (1622), the *David* (1623) and, above all, the *Apollo and Daphne* (1625), which remains, according to the art historian Rudolf Wittkower, "the most astonishing work of Baroque marble sculpture."

Scipione was not an aesthete who bought for the love of art. He was a prince of the Church who understood —before almost anyone else— that art was power. The Borghese collection was not intended for the public: it was intended to impress the ambassadors, cardinals, princes, and kings who visited the villa. Each painting, each sculpture, each room was a message: the Borghese did not just govern the Church. They governed beauty.

---

## 1608–1633 — Villa Borghese: the construction of the private paradise

In 1606, Scipione Borghese bought the first plots on the Pincio Hill, north of the historic centre of Rome, in what were then vineyards and orchards on the outskirts of the city. Between 1608 and 1633, the cardinal built there his crowning work: the **Villa Borghese Pinciana**.

The villa was not a residence: it was a palatial complex surrounded by formal gardens, hunting woods, fountains, statues, artificial grottoes, and pavilions. The main building, known as the Casino Borghese, was designed by the Flemish architect **Giovanni Vasanzio** —Jan van Santen—, a disciple of Flaminio Ponzio. The Casino was not an urban palace like the Palazzo Massimo or the Palazzo Farnese: it was a suburban pleasure villa, a place for parties, banquets, concerts, and —above all— for exhibiting the art collection.

The gardens of Villa Borghese extended over more than eighty hectares and included an artificial lake, a secret garden, an open-air theatre, a hippodrome, and a deer park. The cardinal commissioned fountains from the best sculptors in Rome and ancient statues —bought or plundered— to populate the paths. The villa was, according to the English traveller John Evelyn who visited it in 1644, "an Elysium of delights, where nature and art compete to delight the senses."

When Scipione died in 1633, the villa passed to his heir Marcantonio II Borghese, who expanded it. Over the following centuries, Villa Borghese became one of the obligatory destinations of the Grand Tour —the educational journeys that young European aristocrats made through Italy. Goethe visited it. Stendhal described it. Byron strolled through its gardens. The villa was not just a family residence: it was the image of what a papal family could build when it combined ecclesiastical power, unlimited money, and exquisite taste.

## 17th–18th Centuries — The Borghese, landowners of the Papal States

The art collection and the villa were the visible face of Borghese wealth. But the economic base was deeper: the land.

During the pontificate of Paul V, the Borghese acquired vast expanses of land in the Roman countryside and in the Papal States. The Pope transferred numerous fiefs to his family —Montefortino, Olevano, Monte Compatri, Rocca Priora— and Cardinal Scipione bought even more. By 1620, the Borghese possessed more than 50,000 hectares in Latium and had become one of the largest landowners of the Papal States, comparable only to the Colonna and the Orsini in extent of property.

The Borghese did not sell. They reinvested. Agricultural revenues —wheat, oil, wine, livestock— flowed into the family coffers, financed new art acquisitions, and maintained the villa. During the 17th and 18th centuries, the Borghese entails —the *fidecommisso* that prevented the division of the patrimony among heirs— guaranteed that the fortune remained intact generation after generation.

In 1634, the Borghese bought the Principality of Rossano, in Calabria. In 1769, they bought the Principality of Sulmona. By the end of the 18th century, the head of the family held the titles of Prince of Sulmona, Prince of Rossano, Duke of Palombara, Duke of Poggio Nativo, and Marquis of Mentana, among others. The collection of titles was as vast as the art collection.

---

## 1803 — Camillo Borghese marries Paulina Bonaparte: Napoleon's brother-in-law

On 14 August 1803, **Camillo Borghese** (1775–1832), Prince of Sulmona and head of the family, married **Paulina Bonaparte** (1780–1825), younger sister of Napoleon Bonaparte, in Paris. The wedding was a diplomatic event of the first order: it united the richest papal family in Rome with the man who was reshaping the map of Europe.

The marriage was orchestrated by Napoleon himself, who needed to root his family in traditional European aristocracy to legitimise his dynasty. Camillo Borghese was chosen not for his person —he was described by contemporaries as handsome but of modest intelligence— but for his surname, his palace, his villa, his art collection, and his titles. The Bonapartes were upstarts. The Borghese were princes. Napoleon bought lineage with marriages, and Paulina was the bargaining chip.

For the Borghese, the marriage was a masterstroke of survival. In 1808, Rome was occupied by Napoleonic troops and the Papal States were annexed to the French Empire. Pope Pius VII was arrested and deported to Savona. Papal families that lacked imperial protection lost properties, titles, and position. The Borghese, by contrast, were the family of the Emperor's brother-in-law. While other Roman palaces were sacked, Villa Borghese remained intact.

But the marriage was a personal disaster. Paulina was one of the most beautiful and scandalous women in Europe —Antonio Canova sculpted her as *Venus Victrix* in a statue that is today in the Galleria Borghese, showing her semi-nude in a gesture that scandalised papal Rome. Rumours of infidelity, including an alleged relationship with the violinist Niccolò Paganini, circulated throughout Europe. Camillo and Paulina lived apart for most of the marriage. She died in Florence in 1825; he survived her by seven years. They had no children.

But the Bonaparte connection had fulfilled its function. When the Napoleonic order collapsed in 1814–1815, the Borghese emerged from the revolutionary period with their fortune intact, their palace standing, and their art collection incomplete —Paulina had persuaded Camillo to sell part of the ancient sculptures to Napoleon in 1807, and those pieces ended up in the Louvre— but still formidable. They had been the only great papal family that not only survived Napoleon, but intermarried with him.

---

## 1902 — The sale of the Galleria Borghese to the Italian State

On 7 November 1902, the Italian State bought from the Borghese family the Villa Borghese Pinciana —the Casino and the gardens— and the art collection it housed for 3.6 million lire for the villa and 4.5 million lire for the collection. In total, 8.1 million lire —approximately 35 million euros in today's money.

The sale was a complex operation. The Borghese family was experiencing financial difficulties at the end of the 19th century —not ruin, but a liquidity pressure that made maintaining the villa and the collection unsustainable. The Italian State, in turn, was building the national cultural heritage of the young unified Italy and saw in the Borghese collection one of the jewels that legitimised Rome as the cultural capital of Europe.

The Galleria Borghese opened as a public museum in 1903 and remains today one of the most visited museums in Italy. The collection, which includes masterpieces by Bernini —*Apollo and Daphne*, *The Rape of Proserpina*, *David*—, Caravaggio —six paintings, including *David with the Head of Goliath* and *St Jerome Writing*—, Titian —*Sacred and Profane Love*—, Raphael —*The Deposition*, *Lady with a Unicorn*—, Correggio, Antonello da Messina, and Domenichino, is considered one of the most extraordinary private art collections ever assembled.

But the family did not lose everything. The Borghese retained ownership of the Villa Borghese of Artena, an agricultural estate south of Rome, and maintained an honorary link with the gallery. The Borghese name remained associated with the institution, and the family retained —and retains— consultative rights over the museum's management. Moreover, the gardens of Villa Borghese —bought by the State in 1902 and converted into a public park— remain the largest green space in the centre of Rome and bear the name of the family that created them.

## 20th Century — From the Black Nobility to heritage management

During the 20th century, the Borghese followed the same pattern as the rest of the Black Nobility: loss of formal political power, conservation of real estate and artistic assets, and silent transformation from a courtly aristocracy into a heritage management network.

The main branch of the Borghese maintained its position at the pontifical court during the first decades of the century —Prince Scipione Borghese (1871–1927) was a prominent figure of pre-fascist Rome— but the abolition of the court by Paul VI in 1968, the agrarian reform of the 1950s, and the abolition of noble titles by the Italian Republic in 1946 eroded the institutional bases of their power. Like the Colonna, the Orsini, and the Massimo, the Borghese adapted: they diversified their investments, created foundations to protect their artistic legacy, and maintained a discreet but effective presence in the interstices of Roman society.

One episode that marked the public perception of the Borghese in the 20th century was the figure of Prince **Junio Valerio Borghese** (1906–1974), commander of the famous X Flotilla MAS during the Second World War —an elite unit of the Italian navy that carried out daring sabotage operations against British ships in the Mediterranean. After the war, Junio Valerio Borghese joined the Italian Social Movement —the neo-fascist party— and in 1970 was accused of leading an attempted coup d'état known as the *Borghese Coup*, a far-right plan that foresaw occupying the Ministry of the Interior, the media, and the Quirinal. The coup was never executed, but the scandal associated the Borghese surname with the Italian far right for decades.

The main branch of the family, however, distanced itself politically from Junio Valerio and maintained the institutional profile that had characterised the Borghese since Paul V: closeness to the Vatican, public discretion, and management of their historical heritage.

## 2026 — Villa Borghese, the gallery, and cultural capital

In 2026, the Borghese remain one of the most recognisable families of the Black Nobility, although their power is no longer political or territorial in the traditional sense. Their main asset is not money or land: it is name.

The **Galleria Borghese** is one of the most visited museums in Rome —with figures exceeding half a million visitors annually— and its collection is valued, according to art market estimates, at several billion euros. The family is not the owner of the works —they belong to the Italian State—, but their name is indelibly associated with them. In any tourist guide, in any art history book, in any documentary about Bernini or Caravaggio, the word "Borghese" appears.

The **Villa Borghese of Artena**, south of Rome, remains the property of the family and functions as a private residence and agricultural enterprise. Other real estate properties in Rome and in Tuscany complete a discreet but solid heritage.

The descendants of the Borghese participate in Roman cultural life —foundations, boards of trustees, museum councils— but rarely appear in the media. They do not need visibility: the name does everything. In Rome, saying "Borghese" immediately evokes Bernini, Caravaggio, the gardens of the Pincio, the cardinal who plundered collections to build a museum, the Pope who completed the façade of St Peter's, and the prince who married Napoleon's sister. That constellation of meanings is, in itself, an asset that no financial crisis can devalue and that no political regime can confiscate.

---

## The Borghese irony: the nepotism that became universal heritage

No family of the Black Nobility illustrates better than the Borghese the paradox of papal nepotism. Cardinal Scipione Borghese used his uncle the Pope's power to confiscate collections, buy land with Church money, and build a pleasure villa at the expense of the pontifical treasury. Everything he did was, by the standards of any contemporary morality, a form of institutional corruption.

But the result of that corruption is the Galleria Borghese. The marbles of Bernini, the canvases of Caravaggio, the oils of Titian and Raphael that Scipione accumulated with dubious methods are today the heritage of humanity, accessible to any visitor who pays an entrance fee. The villa he built for his private enjoyment is today the most frequented public park in Rome. The collection he formed to impress ambassadors is today the object of study by art historians from around the world.

The Borghese did not plan this. They did not imagine that their collection would end up in a public museum. They acted as all papal families acted: they accumulated, exhibited, and transmitted. But time transformed their nepotism into heritage. What was plunder became legacy. What was ostentation became culture. The Borghese no longer own the gallery, but they own something more valuable: the story of how it was made. And that story bears their name.
