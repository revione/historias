# The Sacchetti — Bankers of the Papal Depositary

---

## 13th–16th Centuries — Florentine Origins: From Merchants to Papal Bankers

The Sacchetti hail from Florence, the capital of European banking during the Late Middle Ages and the Renaissance. The surname Sacchetti appears documented in Florence from the 13th century, linked to the wool and cloth trade —the industry that sustained the Florentine economy— and to the banking activities that flourished in the city on the Arno.

The Sacchetti belonged to the Florentine *popolo grasso*, that class of enriched burghers who governed the Republic through the major guilds and competed with the feudal nobility for control of the city. Like so many other families of the Tuscan mercantile elite —the Barberini, the Corsini, the Strozzi, the Gondi—, the Sacchetti combined the merchandise trade with credit operations, currency exchange, and loans to governments and princes.

The writer **Franco Sacchetti** (1332–1400), one of the most celebrated members of the family, was a Florentine poet and storyteller, author of the *Trecentonovelle*, a collection of tales portraying the daily life of 14th-century Florence with a realism that makes him one of the precursors of Italian narrative. His work is testimony that the Sacchetti were already, in the 14th century, a family with the cultural sophistication characteristic of the Florentine mercantile elite.

During the 14th and 15th centuries, the Sacchetti followed the usual trajectory of Tuscan banking families: they accumulated capital in Florence, established branches in Rome and other European capitals, and lent money to the Curia. The historian Richard A. Goldthwaite, in *The Economy of Renaissance Florence* (2009), studies the dense fabric of Florentine family banks that financed the Papacy and mentions the Sacchetti among the families that migrated from Florence to Rome throughout the 16th century.

---

## Mid-16th Century — The Move to Rome: The Sacchetti Seek Fortune in the Curia

In the mid-16th century, one branch of the Sacchetti did what many ambitious Florentine families did: they moved to Rome. The Rome of Paul III Farnese (1534–1549) and his successors was a capital in full transformation —the Counter-Reformation, the Council of Trent, the construction of the new Saint Peter's— that demanded financial, administrative, and diplomatic services. The Tuscan banking families found in Rome an expanding market and a state —the Papal States— that needed bankers.

The man who consolidated the Sacchetti in Rome was **Giovanni Battista Sacchetti**, banker and merchant who established the Roman branch of the family bank in the second half of the 16th century. Giovanni Battista lent money to the Apostolic Chamber —the pontifical treasury—, financed operations of the Curia, and cultivated relations with cardinals and popes. The business model was the same as that of the Chigi or the Altoviti: lend money to the Holy See in exchange for supply contracts, monopoly concessions, and access to privileged information on pontifical finances.

Giovanni Battista laid the foundations for what would be the true qualitative leap of the Sacchetti: control of the General Depositary. But that would come with the next generation.

---

## 17th Century — The General Depositary: The Most Lucrative Post in Rome

The **General Depositary** of the Holy See was the most important and most lucrative financial office in the entire Papal State. The depositary managed the pontifical treasury: he received the revenues from taxes, feudal tributes, and ecclesiastical rents; he paid the salaries of the pontifical bureaucracy and the expenses of the papal court; and —this was decisive— he advanced money to the Holy See when revenues did not arrive on time, charging interest on those advances. The depositary was, in practice, the Pope's banker, and the office generated commissions, interest, and business opportunities that turned its holders into some of the richest men in Europe.

In the 17th century, the Sacchetti accessed the General Depositary almost hereditarily. **Marcello Sacchetti** (1575–1629) was appointed General Depositary under Urban VIII Barberini, and his descendants held the office —or preferential access to it— for decades. The Sacchetti were not the only papal bankers —the Chigi, the Altoviti, the Falconieri, and other families also competed— but they achieved a particularly stable position thanks to their network of contacts in the Curia and their closeness to the Barberini.

The historian Massimo Petrocchi, in *La politica finanziaria del papato nel Seicento*, describes the General Depositary as "the nerve center of pontifical finances" and details how the banking families that controlled this office —among them the Sacchetti— accumulated wealth at a pace that was only comparable to that of the most aggressive papal nepotism. The money did not come from ecclesiastical benefices or curial offices: it came from the interest, commissions, and commercial margins that the office generated.

Control of the General Depositary allowed the Sacchetti to finance the purchase of lands in Latium, the construction of a palace in Rome, and the acquisition of noble titles. From bankers they became landowners. From landowners, aristocrats.

---

## 1626–1629 — Pietro da Cortona and the Villa Sacchetti at Castel Fusano

The most visible symbol of the Sacchetti's wealth is the **Villa Sacchetti** at **Castel Fusano**, on the coast of Latium, about twenty kilometers southwest of Rome. The villa was commissioned by the brothers **Marcello** and **Giulio Sacchetti** —the General Depositary and his brother, who would become a cardinal— from the Baroque painter and architect **Pietro da Cortona** (1597–1669) between 1626 and 1629.

Pietro da Cortona —author of the frescoes of the *Triumph of Divine Providence* in the Palazzo Barberini— designed the Villa Sacchetti as a country residence in the manner of 16th-century Tuscan villas, adapted to the landscape of the Latium coast. The villa was surrounded by pine forests —the famous pine grove of Castel Fusano— and geometric gardens, with a symmetrical facade, a central loggia, and agricultural outbuildings. It was a place of rest and hunting for the papal bankers, but also a showcase of their wealth.

The villa at Castel Fusano had additional importance for Pietro da Cortona: the frescoes he painted in its interior —cycles of classical mythology and allegories of rural life— were his first great commission as a painter and the springboard that allowed him access to larger commissions, including the fresco of the Palazzo Barberini. In that sense, the Sacchetti acted as talent scouts. Their patronage launched the career of one of the great painters of the Roman Baroque.

The art historian Giuliano Briganti, in *Pietro da Cortona o della pittura barocca* (1962), devotes an entire chapter to the Villa Sacchetti at Castel Fusano and describes it as "the first essay of great Baroque decorative painting in a civil residence."

---

## 1626–1648 — Cardinal Giulio Sacchetti: The Sacchetti in the Curia

Marcello's younger brother, **Giulio Sacchetti** (1587–1663), followed the ecclesiastical career and was made a cardinal in 1626 by Urban VIII Barberini. Giulio was a jurist trained at the University of Pisa —the same profile as so many papal cardinals—, had served as governor of several cities of the Papal States and as apostolic nuncio in Spain.

Cardinal Giulio Sacchetti was one of the men closest to Urban VIII and formed part of the Barberinian faction that dominated the Curia during the two decades of the Barberini pontificate. He participated in the financial, diplomatic, and political decisions of the papacy, and was one of the candidates favorable to the Barberini in the conclaves of 1644 —which elected Innocent X Pamphili— and 1655 —which elected Alexander VII Chigi—. Although he did not become Pope —no Sacchetti ever was—, his weight in the College of Cardinals cemented the family's position in the Black Nobility.

Giulio Sacchetti was also a patron of the arts, sponsoring the construction of the church of San Giovanni dei Fiorentini in Rome —the national church of the Florentines— and commissioning works from some of the most important Baroque artists.

## 17th–18th Centuries — From Bankers to Marquesses: The Aristocracy of Money

Without a Pope of their own —no Sacchetti was elected Pope—, the Sacchetti consolidated themselves in the Black Nobility through a less spectacular but equally effective route: the accumulation of financial wealth, the acquisition of lands and titles, and marriages with the great papal families.

The Sacchetti bought the title of **Marquess** and accumulated properties in Rome —the Palazzo Sacchetti on the Via Giulia, which they still preserve, originally built by Antonio da Sangallo the Younger in the 16th century— and in Latium, including Castel Fusano and other agricultural estates. The family managed its patrimony with banking prudence: reinvestment of profits, diversification of assets, protection of capital through entails. The Sacchetti were not the richest, nor the most powerful, nor the oldest. But they were the bankers par excellence of the Black Nobility.

The historian Paolo Coen, in *Le magnifiche sorti e progressive*, studies the integration of banking families —including the Sacchetti— into the 18th-century Roman aristocracy and describes how the nobility of money merged with the nobility of blood through the system of titles and marriages.

---

## 18th–19th Centuries — Survival and Discretion: The Sacchetti in the Black Nobility

During the 18th and 19th centuries, the Sacchetti followed the usual pattern of the Black Nobility: loyalty to the Pope, rejection of the Kingdom of Italy after unification, preservation of the patrimony. The family maintained the Palazzo Sacchetti on the Via Giulia —one of the most emblematic streets of Renaissance Rome— and the agricultural properties in Latium, and remained present in the pontifical court and in the social life of the Roman aristocracy.

They were not protagonists of military feats or decisive conclaves. They did not build plazas or colonnades. They did not produce popes. But they survived. And in the history of the Black Nobility, to survive is already an achievement.

---

## 20th–21st Centuries — The Sacchetti Today: The Bank That Became Stone

In 2026, the Sacchetti continue to exist as an aristocratic family. The title of **Marquess Sacchetti** is hereditary. The family preserves the **Palazzo Sacchetti** on the Via Giulia —a Renaissance building of great architectural value, designed by Sangallo and decorated with frescoes by Francesco Salviati— and maintains properties in Latium. The palace is partially open for visits and cultural events, managed with the discretion that has characterized the family for centuries.

The Sacchetti do not have the public fame of the Borghese —whose museum millions of people visit— nor of the Chigi —whose palace is the seat of the Italian government— nor of the Colonna —whose dynasty goes back to the 11th century—. But their story is, in a way, more representative of the Black Nobility than that of those exceptionally famous families. The majority of the families of the Black Nobility were like the Sacchetti: they accumulated wealth through the financial administration of the Papal State, bought titles and palaces, connected with the most powerful families, and survived for centuries without producing a Pope or winning a war. The silent elite. The managers of money.

The Palazzo Sacchetti on the Via Giulia is the testimony of this trajectory. It was not built by a cardinal nephew. It was bought with money earned lending to the Pope. And it still stands, four centuries later, with the same surname on the door.

---

## The Papal Banker Pattern: The Sacchetti and the Depositary

The Sacchetti represent a specific pattern in the history of the Black Nobility: that of the family of Tuscan banker-merchants that transforms itself into Roman aristocracy by controlling a key financial post of the pontifical administration. They did not need a Pope. They needed the General Depositary. Through that office, they converted the revenue stream of the Holy See into family capital: lands, palaces, titles, and art collections.

This pattern is distinct from that of the Chigi —who did produce a Pope— and closer to that of the Altoviti or Falconieri: money buys nobility, but money is not enough to occupy the throne of Peter. The Sacchetti arrived as far as money could take them without a Pope in the family. And that limit —the marquisate, the palace, the villa of Castel Fusano— was not little. It was a life of princes in everything except the title.
