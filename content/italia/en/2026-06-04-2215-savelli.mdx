# The Savelli — Guardians of the Conclave

---

## 10th Century — Origins in the Rome of the Theophylacts

The Savelli emerged in 10th-century Rome, in the midst of the _saeculum obscurum_ —the "dark age" of the papacy—, when the Theophylact family —the counts of Tusculum— controlled the throne of Saint Peter as if it were a hereditary fief. The Savelli were a branch of the landowning Roman aristocracy with properties on the Aventine, the southern hill of Rome, and in the countryside of Latium. The name derives from the castle of Sabellum —near present-day Albano Laziale—, although the oldest sources cannot establish with certainty whether the castle gave its name to the family or the family to the castle.

The first Savelli documented with certainty is **Giovanni Savelli**, who appears in a deed of the monastery of Sant'Alessio on the Aventine around the year 1000 as a donor of lands in the region of Albano. By the 11th century, the Savelli were already lords of several castles in the Alban Hills and in the Roman countryside, allies of the counts of Tusculum —the future Colonna— and occasional rivals of the emerging Orsini.

The historian Sandro Carocci, in _Baroni di Roma_ (École Française de Rome, 1993), places the Savelli among the four great baronial families of medieval Rome —alongside Colonna, Orsini, and Annibaldi— who controlled the territory and papal elections during the 12th and 13th centuries.

---

## 12th Century — The Curial Ascent: The Savelli Enter the Sacred College

Unlike the Colonna —who built their power from rural fiefs—, the Savelli ascended through the Curia. The first documented Savelli cardinal is **Aimerico Savelli**, created cardinal by Pope Honorius II in 1126. From that moment, the family maintained an almost uninterrupted presence in the Sacred College of Cardinals for two hundred years.

The Savelli strategy was different from that of the Orsini and Colonna. They sought not the territorial control of entire regions of Latium —like the Colonna with Palestrina or the Orsini with Bracciano— but a network of castles and palaces that guaranteed control of the Aventine and, above all, the curial offices that gave them direct access to the machinery of papal power. It was in this context that the office that would define the family for seven hundred years emerged: the Marshalate of the Holy Roman Church.

---

## Before 1150 — The Office of Marshal of the Holy Roman Church

The Savelli held the hereditary office of **Marshal of the Holy Roman Church** (_Marescalcus Sanctae Romanae Ecclesiae_). The exact date of the concession is uncertain —probably in the 11th or early 12th century—, but it is documented as a hereditary privilege of the Savelli from at least 1150.

The Marshal had two main functions:

- **The custody of the conclave.** During papal elections, the Savelli Marshal was responsible for the physical security of the enclosure where the cardinals gathered to elect the Pope. He controlled who entered and who left, supervised the seals on the doors, and ensured that the conclave was not interrupted by external pressures. In practice, the Marshal could influence the papal election simply by deciding which messages reached the cardinals and which did not.

- **The guard of the papal palaces.** As head of the Pope's physical security, the Marshal commanded the troops that protected the papal residences in Rome and in the cities of the Papal States.

The historian Gaetano Moroni, in his _Dizionario di erudizione storico-ecclesiastica_ (1840-1879), documents that the office of Marshal conferred upon the Savelli "a unique position in the hierarchy of the pontifical court: neither ecclesiastical nor purely military, but of armed custody of the very institution of the papacy." The Savelli did not govern the Church: they protected it. And in protection there was power.

Later, in the modern era, the title of Marshal passed to the Chigi —who held it until the reforms of Paul VI in 1968—, but the origin of the office and its configuration as a hereditary office were the work of the Savelli.

## 1216 — Honorius III: The Pope Who Approved the Franciscan Rule and Launched the Fifth Crusade

On July 18, 1216, two days after the death of Innocent III, Cardinal Cencio Savelli was elected Pope in Perugia. He took the name Honorius III. He was a man of advanced age —probably around sixty-five—, trained in the pontifical Chancery, where he had served as chamberlain. His most enduring work before the pontificate was the _Liber Censuum_ (1192), the fiscal cadastre of the Roman Church, a meticulous compilation of all the rents, tributes, and properties of the Apostolic See. Historians consider it the first systematic budget in papal history.

Honorius III governed the Church for eleven years (1216-1227). His pontificate was, in many respects, the administrative consolidation of the conquests of Innocent III. But it had two acts of its own that marked the history of Christianity.

**The approval of the Franciscan rule.** On November 29, 1223, Honorius III promulgated the bull _Solet annuere_, which definitively approved the rule of the Order of Friars Minor —the Franciscans— founded by Francis of Assisi. Innocent III had given oral approval in 1209 or 1210; Honorius III turned it into written canon law binding on the entire Church. The bull _Solet annuere_ —preserved in the Vatican Apostolic Archive— is one of the most important foundational documents in religious history: it transformed a movement of lay penitents into a formal order with structure, hierarchy, and legal status.

The Franciscan historian Cuthbert Hess, in _The Friars Minor: Their Rule and Constitutions_ (1930), maintains that Honorius III "understood the potential of the Franciscans as an instrument of pastoral reform within the Church far better than the cardinals who opposed their approval." Honorius III was the Pope who gave the Franciscans their charter of canonical existence, and with it, the institutional cover that allowed the order to expand throughout Christendom in less than a generation.

**The Fifth Crusade.** Honorius III inherited from Innocent III the project of a new crusade to reconquer Jerusalem. The Fifth Crusade (1217-1221) was the first planned directly by a Pope —Innocent III had convoked it at the IV Lateran Council but died before launching it—. Honorius III's plan was strategically ambitious: attack Egypt —the base of Saladin's Ayyubid power— to force the return of Jerusalem. The crusaders took the port city of Damietta in 1219, but the campaign stalled and, in 1221, they were defeated at the battle of al-Mansura. The crusade failed in its final objective, but Honorius III did not abandon the project: he dedicated the last years of his pontificate to organizing the Sixth Crusade, which his successor Gregory IX would launch under the command of Frederick II.

Honorius III died on March 18, 1227. He was buried in Santa Maria Maggiore. The historian Ferdinand Gregorovius, in volume V of his _Geschichte der Stadt Rom im Mittelalter_ (1862), describes him as "an administrator Pope, not a conqueror Pope: his pontificate was that of institutional consolidation, not of ideological expansion."

## 1285 — Honorius IV: The Pope of the Aventine

On April 2, 1285, Cardinal Giacomo Savelli was elected Pope under the name Honorius IV. He was a man of advanced age —probably around seventy-five—, severely affected by gout: contemporary chronicles state that he could not move his hands and that he celebrated Mass with his arms supported on a mechanical support. But his physical frailty did not prevent his pontificate, though brief —barely two years, until his death in 1287—, from being remarkably active.

Honorius IV was a Roman of the Aventine. His family owned its main fortress on that hill —the Rocca Savelli— and he himself had been born and raised in the shadow of its walls. As Pope, Honorius IV moved his residence to the Aventine —instead of the Lateran palace, which he found in poor condition— and governed the Church from Monte Savelli, surrounded by his kinsfolk.

His pontificate, documented by Gregorovius and by the historian Peter Partner in _The Lands of St. Peter_ (1972), focused on two fronts:

- **The Sicilian question.** After the Sicilian Vespers (1282) —the rebellion that expelled the Angevins from Sicily—, the island had fallen under the dominion of Peter III of Aragon, challenging papal sovereignty. Honorius IV excommunicated Peter III and supported the attempts of Charles of Anjou to recover Sicily, but the situation remained unresolved at his death. The Sicilian problem would poison papal politics for decades.

- **University policy.** Honorius IV was the Pope who granted formal privileges to the University of Paris —the great factory of theologians of Christendom— and supported the study of Arabic and Hebrew in European universities, with the aim of training missionaries capable of preaching in the languages of the infidels. He was an intellectual Pope, trained in law and sensitive to the importance of academic knowledge for the defense of the faith.

Honorius IV died on April 3, 1287, and was buried in the basilica of Santa Maria in Aracoeli —next to the Campidoglio—, in a funerary monument designed by Arnolfo di Cambio, one of the great sculptors of the Italian _Duecento_. His tomb, destroyed in later renovations of the basilica, was a testimony to the artistic wealth that the Savelli knew how to mobilize in their moment of maximum splendor.

## 13th–15th Centuries — The Rocca Savelli: The Fortress of the Aventine

The **Rocca Savelli** —the Savelli fortress on the Aventine— was the main residence of the family in Rome during most of its history. Probably built in the 12th century on the remains of an earlier fortification, the Rocca dominated the eastern side of the Aventine Hill, with views of the Circus Maximus and the Palatine. From its walls, the Savelli controlled the southern access to Rome via the Via Ostiense —the route to the port— and the Via Appia —the route to Naples—.

The fortress was expanded by Honorius IV in 1285, when the Savelli Pope made it his pontifical residence. During his pontificate, the Rocca Savelli was, for two years, the center of the government of Christendom —the only moment in history when the Aventine, and not the Vatican or the Lateran, was the seat of papal power—.

Over the centuries, the Rocca Savelli passed through various hands. In the 15th century, the Savelli sold part of the fortress to the knights of the Order of Malta, who incorporated it into their Roman priory. Today, the Rocca —known as the Priory of Malta— still stands in Piazza dei Cavalieri di Malta, on the Aventine. The famous "keyhole" of the priory —through which one sees the dome of Saint Peter's framed by a garden of cypresses— is one of the most visited secrets of Rome. Behind that keyhole is the fortress of the Savelli.

## 13th–17th Centuries — The Savelli Castle Network in Latium

The Savelli, like all the great Roman baronial families, built a network of castles and fortifications that guaranteed their territorial power in Latium. Among the main ones:

**Palombara Sabina.** The castle of Palombara, northeast of Rome, was one of the main fiefs of the Savelli in the Sabina. The fortress, built on a pre-Roman settlement, dominates the valley of the Farfa River and controlled the Via Salaria —the salt route—. The Savelli owned it from the 13th century until their extinction in the 18th century. Today, the castle of Palombara is municipal property and houses a museum.

**Castel Gandolfo.** Although today it is world-famous as the summer residence of the Popes —nestled in the Alban Hills, dominating Lake Albano—, the original castle of Castel Gandolfo was owned by the Savelli from the 13th century. The family sold it to the Apostolic Chamber in the 17th century, and Urban VIII —of the Barberini family— turned it into the papal residence we know today.

**Albano Laziale.** The Savelli were lords of Albano, the city by the lake, for several centuries. The family owned lands, vineyards, and feudal rights in the entire region of the Alban Hills, which they shared —and disputed— with the Colonna.

**Rocca di Papa.** The fortress of Rocca di Papa, on the summit of Monte Cavo —the ancient _Mons Albanus_, the sacred mountain of the Latins—, was one of the strategic fortifications of the Savelli in the Alban Hills, controlling access to the sanctuary of Jupiter Latiaris since prehistoric times.

Unlike the Orsini —who controlled a territorial empire of sixty castles— or the Colonna —who dominated eight hundred square kilometers—, the Savelli maintained a more compact lordship, centered on the Aventine, the Sabina, and the Alban Hills. Their power lay not in the extent of territory but in the quality of their strategic positions —and in the possession of the office of Marshal of the Conclave—.

## 16th–18th Centuries — The Gradual Decline and Extinction in 1712

From the 16th century, the Savelli —like other medieval baronial families— entered into a slow decline. The Papal States were being centralized, the Pope's authority over rural barons was being reinforced, and the great families of the Middle Ages —Savelli, Annibaldi, Conti— were being eclipsed by the new papal dynasties of the Renaissance and Baroque —Farnese, Borghese, Barberini—.

The Savelli maintained their presence in the Curia through cardinals —such as **Giulio Savelli** (1574-1644), created cardinal by Urban VIII— but they no longer produced Popes after Honorius IV. Their power was gradually reduced to the castle of Palombara and the properties on the Aventine.

On March 5, 1712, **Bernardino Savelli**, last Duke of Castel Gandolfo and last male of the direct line of the family, died without male descendants. With him, the agnatic line of the Savelli became extinct. The surname, however, did not disappear: it passed through the women of the family. Bernardino's daughter, **Maria Virginia Savelli**, married Duke Francesco Sforza Cesarini, and their descendants incorporated the surname Savelli into their own. The Sforza Cesarini Savelli —who still exist— are the genealogical heirs of the ancient Savelli, although the original male line became extinct over three hundred years ago.

The historian Gaetano Moroni, in the volume dedicated to the Savelli of his _Dizionario_, closes the entry with a laconic phrase: _"The Savelli family became extinct in 1712, leaving the memory of two pontiffs, a fortress on the Aventine, and the office of Marshal of the Holy Roman Church which survived them for two more centuries."_

---

## Legacy — What the Savelli Left Behind

The Savelli did not build a territorial empire like the Colonna or a machine of nepotism like the Borghese. Nor did they produce a Pope of the theocratic caliber of Innocent III or a literary figure like Vittoria Colonna. But their legacy is, in a way, deeper: they were the institutional guardians of the papacy in the crucial period from the 12th to the 14th century —the moment when the Church went from being a federation of feudal bishoprics to a centralized pontifical monarchy—.

The Marshalate of the Holy Roman Church —the office of physically guarding the conclave— was the institutional expression of that function: the Savelli did not decide who was Pope, but they controlled who could influence the decision. The Rocca Savelli on the Aventine still stands, converted into the Priory of Malta. And the name Savelli, though extinct in the male line since 1712, survives fused in the Sforza Cesarini Savelli —a reminder that, in the Roman nobility, not even biological extinction means total disappearance—.

In the words of the British historian Peter Partner: _"The Savelli belong to that category of Roman families that, without protagonizing the great scandals or the great glories of the papacy, constituted the institutional fabric that made possible the survival of the Holy See during the most turbulent centuries of its history."_
