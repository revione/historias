# The Pamphili — The Pope of Velázquez and the Woman Who Governed

---

## 15th Century — Modest Origins in Gubbio

The Pamphili were not Romans, nor Tuscans, nor Genoese. They came from **Gubbio**, a small city in Umbria, in the foothills of the central Apennines, famous for its Eugubine Tables —some of the oldest documents in the Umbrian language— but irrelevant in the geography of Italian power during the Renaissance. The original surname may have been "Pamfilio" or "Panfili," Latinized as "Pamphilius" in later ecclesiastical documentation.

The Pamphili appear in the documentation of Gubbio in the 15th century as a family of the local petty nobility: landowners, linked to the municipal administration, without connections to great Italian politics. They were provincial notables. Nothing in their origin foreshadowed that a century later they would dominate Rome.

The historian Gaetano Moroni, in his *Dizionario*, traces the origin of the Pamphili from Gubbio to Rome through the emigration of several family members in the 16th century, attracted by the opportunities that the Roman Curia offered to Umbrian jurists and administrators.

---

## 16th Century — Move to Rome: The Pontifical Bureaucracy as a Social Elevator

The true founder of the Pamphili fortune was **Giovanni Battista Pamphili** (d. 1639), who emigrated from Gubbio to Rome at the end of the 16th century, studied law, and integrated into the pontifical bureaucracy. Giovanni Battista was not a banker like the Chigi, nor a cloth merchant like the Barberini, nor a military captain like the Ruspoli. He was a jurist. A technician of law in the service of the papal monarchy.

He served as consistorial advocate in the Roman Rota —the supreme tribunal of the Church— and as auditor of the causes of the Apostolic Chamber. He married **Maria Cancellieri del Bufalo**, from a family of the lesser Roman nobility, and built the economic base of the Pamphili: urban properties in Rome, lands in Latium, and a small family palace in the Piazza Navona —where the great Palazzo Pamphili would later be built—.

Giovanni Battista had several children. One of them was named **Giovanni Battista Pamphili** —like his father— and was born in Rome in 1574. That son would change the history of the family and of the Church.

---

## 1644 — Giovanni Battista Pamphili Becomes Innocent X: The Man of the Portrait

On September 15, 1644, after a 37-day conclave dominated by the struggle between the pro-French faction —led by the Barberini— and the pro-Spanish faction —led by the cardinals of the Habsburg party—, Cardinal Giovanni Battista Pamphili was elected Pope. He took the name **Innocent X**. He was 70 years old.

Giovanni Battista Pamphili was then a cardinal of long trajectory, educated at the Collegio Romano of the Jesuits, doctor of law, apostolic nuncio in Naples and Madrid —where he had established a close relationship with the court of Philip IV of Spain— and a prominent member of the Spanish party in the Sacred College. His election was a defeat for the Barberini —the nephews of Urban VIII— and a victory for the faction that demanded the end of excessive nepotism and the restoration of financial discipline in the Holy See.

The historian Leopold von Ranke, in his classic study on the popes, describes Innocent X as "a man of severe appearance and brusque manners, of weak character but enormous susceptibility to wounded pride." His pontificate lasted eleven years —until January 1655— and was marked by the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended the Thirty Years' War and marginalized the Papacy from the great European decisions. Innocent X formally protested against Westphalia through the bull *Zelo Domus Dei* (1650), but his protest was ignored by the European powers. The papacy was no longer an international arbiter. It was an Italian regional power.

But Innocent X is not remembered for Westphalia. He is remembered for the portrait.

---

## 1650 — Velázquez and the Most Famous Portrait in the History of the Papacy

In 1650, during the Roman Jubilee, **Diego Velázquez** (1599–1660), the court painter of Philip IV of Spain, traveled to Rome to acquire works of art for the Spanish royal collection and to portray Innocent X. The result was the **Portrait of Innocent X**, painted in oil on canvas, which today is preserved in the Galleria Doria Pamphili in Rome.

The portrait of Innocent X is unanimously considered one of the absolute masterpieces of the history of Western painting. Velázquez captured the seventy-six-year-old Pope with merciless realism: the penetrating gaze, the folds of the double chin, the rictus of the mouth, the bejeweled hands gripping a letter, the red cape and the papal zucchetto, the golden chair. It is a portrait that does not idealize: it shows the power and the vulnerability, the cunning and the weariness, the authority and the fragility of an old man who governs Christendom.

The legend —probably apocryphal but revealing— says that Innocent X himself, upon seeing the finished portrait, exclaimed: *"Troppo vero"* —"Too true"—. True or not, the comment captures the effect that the portrait produces on the viewer: the sensation of being before a real man, not before an icon.

The art historian Jonathan Brown, in *Velázquez: Painter and Courtier* (1986), analyzes the diplomatic context of the portrait: Velázquez was not just a painter, he was a cultural ambassador of Philip IV, and the portrait of Innocent X was a political act —a gesture of deference from the Hispanic Monarchy toward the pro-Spanish Pope—. But the result was art, not diplomacy. Francis Bacon, the 20th-century British painter, was obsessed with the portrait and recreated it in dozens of versions —the series of the *Screaming Popes*—, transforming Velázquez's work into an icon of modern art.

The portrait of Innocent X is still in Rome, in the Galleria Doria Pamphili, in the palace of the Via del Corso that the Pamphili —and later the Doria Pamphili— inhabited for centuries (see post 2210 on the Doria Pamphili for the history of the fusion of the two families and of the palace).

## 1644–1655 — Olimpia Maidalchini: "La Papessa" Who Governed Rome

Innocent X did not govern alone. He governed —or was governed— by his sister-in-law, **Olimpia Maidalchini** (1591–1657). Olimpia was the sister of the wife of the Pope's brother —an indirect but decisive kinship relationship— and became the most powerful woman in Rome during the pontificate of Innocent X.

Olimpia Maidalchini had been born in Viterbo into a family of the local petty nobility. She married first a rich landowner, became a widow, and inherited his fortune. In second nuptials, she married **Pamphilio Pamphili** (1564–1639), the older brother of the future Innocent X, and established herself in Rome. From the moment her brother-in-law was elected Pope, Olimpia assumed a political role that no woman had played in the papal court since the famous Marozia of the 10th century —the *senatrix* who dominated the papacy during the period known as the "pornocracy" in Protestant historiography—.

Olimpia managed the papal audiences, controlled access to the Pope, negotiated with cardinals and ambassadors, administered the finances of the Pamphili family, and accumulated a personal fortune that scandalized Rome. The Romans called her **"la papessa"** —the popess— and pasquinades circulated —the satirical lampoons that the Romans nailed to the talking statues of the city— mocking her greed and her dominion over the pontiff.

The German historian Ferdinand Gregorovius, in his monumental *Geschichte der Stadt Rom im Mittelalter* but also in his works on modern Rome, dedicates pages to Olimpia Maidalchini as an example of the systemic corruption of Baroque nepotism. The Italian historian Claudio Rendina, in *I papi: Storia e segreti*, describes her as "the most hated woman in Rome" during the pontificate of Innocent X.

The most persistent legend about Olimpia —recorded by contemporary chroniclers— is that, when Innocent X died on January 7, 1655, Olimpia removed from the Pope's corpse all the money and valuables that were in the mortuary chamber and refused to pay for the funeral, alleging that she "was a widow" and could not assume the expense. The body of Innocent X remained abandoned for three days in Saint Peter's Basilica, without a proper coffin and covered by a sheet, until a steward paid out of his own pocket for a wooden box for the burial. The anecdote, although possibly magnified by Olimpia's enemies, reflects the reputation of greed that pursued the Pamphili family until its merger with the Doria.

Olimpia was, in sum, the true power behind the Pamphili throne. Innocent X signed. Olimpia governed.

## 1644–1655 — The Transformation of the Piazza Navona: Sant'Agnese in Agone and the Palazzo Pamphili

The urbanistic legacy of the Pamphili was in the **Piazza Navona**, the ancient arena of the Circus of Domitian turned into a Baroque plaza. The Pamphili had their family palace in this square since the early 17th century, and it was Innocent X who transformed it into one of the most spectacular urban ensembles in Rome.

The Pope commissioned the construction of the church of **Sant'Agnese in Agone** —dedicated to Saint Agnes, martyred according to tradition in the brothel that occupied that site— on the west side of the square. The initial project was by Girolamo Rainaldi and his son Carlo Rainaldi, but in 1653 Innocent X transferred the direction of the work to **Francesco Borromini**, Bernini's great rival. Borromini designed the concave facade of Sant'Agnese, one of the paradigmatic masterpieces of Roman Baroque, and added the two bell towers flanking the dome.

In parallel, the Pope commissioned **Gian Lorenzo Bernini** to create the **Fontana dei Quattro Fiumi** —the Fountain of the Four Rivers— in the center of the square. The fountain, completed in 1651, is an allegory of the four great rivers of the known continents —the Nile for Africa, the Ganges for Asia, the Danube for Europe, the Río de la Plata for America—, with an Egyptian obelisk at the center brought from the Circus of Maxentius and topped by the Pamphili dove —the family emblem—. The fountain is a political message: the Pope dominates the rivers of the world, even those that do not obey the Church.

The rivalry between Bernini and Borromini in the Piazza Navona is legendary. Bernini's fountain was financed with taxes on wine and bread —Innocent X had no qualms about taxing the poor to embellish Rome—, and its construction was the object of criticism for the excessive cost. But the result is one of the most beautiful urban ensembles in the world.

---

## 1655 — The Death of Innocent X and the Brief Splendor of the Pamphili

The death of Innocent X in January 1655, followed by the flight of Olimpia Maidalchini from Rome —pursued by creditors and popular fury—, marked the beginning of the decline of the Pamphili as an autonomous papal dynasty. The family had accumulated an immense fortune during the eleven years of the pontificate, but it lacked the Roman roots of the Orsini, the cardinal machinery of the Colonna, or the banking network of the Chigi. Without a second Pope —and there wasn't one—, the Pamphili were condemned to extinction in the male line.

In the 18th century, the Pamphili family became extinct in the direct male line. The universal heiress of the Pamphili patrimony, Anna Pamphili, married the Genoese prince Giovanni Andrea Doria in 1671, uniting the two dynasties. The merger gave rise to the **Doria Pamphili Landi** family, which inherited the palace on the Via del Corso, the villa on the Via Aurelia, the art collection —including the portrait of Innocent X by Velázquez— and all the titles and properties of the Pamphili (see post 2210 on the Doria Pamphili for the full story of the merger and of the palace).

---

## Brief Conclusion: The Dynasty That Lasted One Pope

The original Pamphili —those from Gubbio, those of the jurist who emigrated to Rome, those of Olimpia Maidalchini and the portrait of Velázquez— represent one of the most extreme and brief cases of rise and decline in the Black Nobility. In two generations, they went from being Umbrian notables to dominating Rome. They had a Pope —Innocent X, 1644–1655—, a woman who governed in his name, a palace in the Piazza Navona, a fountain by Bernini, a church by Borromini, and a portrait by Velázquez. And afterward, the male line became extinct.

The Pamphili are the proof that papal nepotism —even in its most concentrated and intense form— did not guarantee long-term dynastic survival. Without a second pontificate to renew the political capital and without a network of marriages to consolidate the lineage, the wealth accumulated in eleven years passed to another family. The surname Pamphili survives today fused with that of the Doria —Doria Pamphili—, but the original family from Gubbio died out in the 18th century. What remains is the art: the portrait by Velázquez, the fountain by Bernini, the church by Borromini. And the legend of Olimpia, the woman who governed Rome.
