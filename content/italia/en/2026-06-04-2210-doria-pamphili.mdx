# The Doria Pamphili — From Genoese Admirals to the 'Papessa' of Rome

---

## 1466 — Andrea Doria: the admiral who was "father of the fatherland" of Genoa

The Doria were one of the oldest and most powerful families of the Republic of Genoa. Their name appears in the city's annals from the 12th century, when they participated in Genoese naval expeditions to the eastern Mediterranean and the Black Sea. They were shipowners, merchants, and above all, warriors of the sea. But no Doria reached the historical stature of **Andrea Doria** (1466–1560).

Andrea Doria was born in Oneglia, on the Ligurian Riviera di Ponente, on 30 November 1466, into a cadet branch of the family. Orphaned of his father at an early age, he was trained as a condottiero and mercenary sailor, serving successively Pope Innocent VIII —himself a Genoese—, Ferdinand II of Naples, the Republic of Genoa, and the King of France. As commander of the Genoese galleys, he fought the Barbary pirates, the Ottoman Turks, and the rival fleets of Venice and Spain in the turbulent Mediterranean of the early 16th century.

The naval historian Roger Crowley, in *Empires of the Sea* (2008), describes Andrea Doria as "the last great naval condottiero: an admiral without a fixed homeland who sold his sword to the highest bidder until he found a cause to which he could commit himself." That cause was the liberation of Genoa.

In 1528, Andrea Doria executed the masterstroke of his career. Genoa had been subjected to the French orbit for decades, governed by factions that answered to the King of France. Doria, who until then had served Francis I, switched sides. He allied with Emperor Charles V and, at the head of his personal fleet, liberated Genoa from French dominion. He restored the Republic of Genoa with a new constitution that stabilised the city's oligarchic government for the following two and a half centuries. Charles V granted him the title of Prince of Melfi and the Order of the Golden Fleece. Genoa acclaimed him *Pater Patriae* —"father of the fatherland"— and erected to him a statue that still today presides over Piazza del Principe, in front of his palace.

The Swiss historian Jacob Burckhardt, in *The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy* (1860), saw in Andrea Doria the archetype of the Renaissance individual who forges his own destiny: "he inherited neither a throne nor a fleet; he built both with his talent and his sword."

## 1644 — Giovanni Battista Pamphili becomes Innocent X

The Pamphili did not come from any ancient greatness. They were a family of jurists and administrators originating from Gubbio, in Umbria, who had moved to Rome at the end of the 15th century. The first to stand out was **Giovanni Antonio Pamphili**, who served as auditor of the Apostolic Chamber and bought land in the Roman countryside. His son, **Camillo Pamphili**, was created cardinal by Gregory XIII in 1599. But the definitive leap was made by his brother's son: Giovanni Battista Pamphili.

**Giovanni Battista Pamphili** was born in Rome on 6 May 1574. He earned his doctorate in canon and civil law at the Collegio Romano —the Jesuits' university— and embarked on the curial career. He served as consistorial lawyer, auditor of the Tribunal of the Rota —the highest court of appeal of the Church—, apostolic nuncio in Naples, and from 1625, nuncio at the court of Philip IV in Madrid. In Spain, Pamphili acquired a deep knowledge of the politics of the Austrian Habsburgs and forged relations with the most powerful court in Europe —connections that would prove decisive for his election.

On 15 September 1644, after a conclave of thirty-seven days —the longest of the 17th century—, Giovanni Battista Pamphili was elected Pope. He took the name **Innocent X**. He was seventy years old. He was an experienced jurist, a shrewd diplomat, and a man of reserved and distrustful character, very different from the exuberant Urban VIII Barberini who had preceded him.

The historian Leopold von Ranke, in volume III of *Die römischen Päpste* (1836), describes Innocent X as "a man of clear understanding but weak will, dominated by the women of his family and by distrust towards those around him." That woman was Olimpia.

## 1644–1655 — Olimpia Maidalchini: "la papessa" who governed Rome

If there is one figure who defines the pontificate of Innocent X, it is not the Pope. It is his sister-in-law: **Olimpia Maidalchini** (1591–1657), called in Rome *la papessa* —"the popess"—, the woman who controlled access to the Pope, amassed a colossal fortune, and governed the Papal States for a decade without holding any official office.

Olimpia Maidalchini was the daughter of a modest tax collector from Viterbo. She had married in first nuptials a wealthy landowner from Viterbo, of whom she was soon widowed, and in second nuptials **Pamphilio Pamphili**, older brother of the future Innocent X. When Pamphilio died in 1639, Olimpia was widowed for the second time, but with a son —Camillo Pamphili— who was the heir of the family. And when her brother-in-law became Pope in 1644, Olimpia Maidalchini became the most powerful woman in Rome.

The historian Ludwig von Pastor, in volume XXX of his *History of the Popes*, dedicates pages of barely concealed irony to the figure of Olimpia: "no woman in the history of the papacy, not even Lucrezia Borgia or the Countess Matilda, exercised such direct and visible power over the person of the Pope." Olimpia controlled the papal audiences: no cardinal, no ambassador, no prince could see the Pope without her consent. She managed curial appointments and, according to the chroniclers of the era, collected commissions for every ecclesiastical and civil office that was granted. She accumulated properties, palaces, and fiefs in Rome and in the countryside. Her son, Camillo Pamphili, was appointed cardinal nephew —the traditional power office of the papal nephew— but renounced the cardinal's hat in 1647 to marry Olimpia Aldobrandini, the immensely rich heiress of the Aldobrandini. Camillo's renunciation —which infuriated his mother— left the field free for Olimpia to exercise power without intermediaries.

The chronicles of the era —the diary of Giacinto Gigli, the letters of the Venetian ambassadors, the Roman *avvisi*— portray Olimpia as a woman of formidable intelligence, insatiable greed, and absolute psychological dominance over the Pope. Innocent X, according to the Venetian ambassador Giovanni Giustiniani, "makes no decision without consulting her, and when she is absent, the government of the Church is paralysed."

When Innocent X died on 7 January 1655, Olimpia Maidalchini was the protagonist of the episode that would define her black legend. According to the account transmitted by the chroniclers, the Pope's corpse lay abandoned for three days in a room of the Apostolic Palace because no one wanted to pay for the funeral. Olimpia, who had accumulated millions of scudi, refused to cover the expenses, alleging that she was "a poor widow." Finally, the canons of St Peter's paid for a modest burial. The historian Eleanor Herman, in *Mistress of the Vatican* (2008), nuances the anecdote —funeral delays were common in papal Rome— but confirms that Olimpia's avarice was legendary and her unpopularity was total. She died in October 1657, on her fief of San Martino al Cimino, having survived the Pope by only two years.

## 1650 — Velázquez and the portrait that immortalised the Pamphili

In 1650, during the Jubilee, the Spanish painter **Diego Velázquez** —the greatest artist of the Spanish Golden Age— travelled to Rome sent by Philip IV. His official mission was to acquire works of art for the Spanish royal collection. But his artistic mission was another: to paint the Pope.

Velázquez was received by Innocent X in the Apostolic Palace. The Pope, who had been nuncio in Madrid and knew Spain well, granted the painter several sessions. The result was the *Portrait of Innocent X*, preserved today in the Galleria Doria Pamphili in Rome, which is considered by many art historians —from Francis Bacon, who reinterpreted it obsessively in his series of "Screaming Popes," to the critic Robert Hughes— as the best portrait ever painted.

The art historian Jonathan Brown, in *Velázquez: Painter and Courtier* (1986), analyses the portrait as "a work without concessions": Velázquez did not idealise the Pope. He showed a seventy-six-year-old man with a penetrating gaze, tight lips, and an expression of cunning distrust that seemed to confirm everything the Romans said about Innocent X. When the Pope saw the finished painting, according to tradition, he exclaimed: *Troppo vero* —"Too true."

Velázquez also painted the Pope's family, including a portrait of Camillo Pamphili and possibly one of Olimpia Maidalchini —although the latter has been lost. The Velázquez-Pamphili connection sealed the family's entry into universal art history: the Pope's portrait was not just a portrait, it was visual immortality.

## 1644–1660 — The construction of Pamphili power: Palazzo Doria Pamphili and Villa Pamphili

During the pontificate of Innocent X, the Pamphili executed a programme of construction and artistic acquisition that placed them at the level of the great papal families.

The **Palazzo Pamphili** on Via del Corso —today Palazzo Doria Pamphili— began to be built in 1644 on a pre-existing nucleus of medieval buildings. The architect **Girolamo Rainaldi**, one of the most active in Baroque Rome, designed the main façade and the sequence of halls that would constitute the core of the palace. Later, **Carlo Fontana** —a disciple of Bernini— added the great gallery and completed the wing facing Piazza del Collegio Romano. The palace is today the largest private palace in Rome: more than a thousand rooms, a surface area occupying an entire block between Via del Corso, Via della Gatta, Piazza Grazioli, and Via Lata.

The **Villa Pamphili** was built on the Janiculum Hill, west of Trastevere, on land bought by Innocent X. The architect **Alessandro Algardi** —Bernini's great rival— designed the villa and the gardens, which extended over more than one hundred and fifty hectares. The villa, known as the Casino del Bel Respiro, is one of the masterpieces of Roman Baroque, with façades decorated with classical reliefs and stuccoes celebrating the glory of the Pamphili family. The gardens of Villa Pamphili, opened today as a public park —the largest in Rome—, were designed with fountains, ponds, grottoes, and a theatre of greenery by the landscape architect Francesco Grimaldi.

The Pamphili construction programme had an explicit political function: the family, of recent origin and without deep feudal roots, needed an architecture that could compete with that of the Colonna, the Orsini, and the Borghese. The palace on Via del Corso and the villa on the Janiculum were the architectural response to the antiquity of the great Roman families.

## 1671 — The Doria-Pamphili fusion: Genoa and Rome under one name

In 1671, the marriage took place that fused two dynasties: **Giovanni Andrea Doria**, Prince of Melfi and heir of the admiral Andrea Doria, married **Anna Pamphili**, grand-niece of Innocent X and heiress of the Pamphili patrimony.

The marriage was not a mere dynastic union: it was a carefully orchestrated patrimonial consolidation operation. The Doria contributed the most prestigious naval lineage of Italy, the title of Prince of Melfi, the Golden Fleece granted by Charles V, and the immense properties of the family in Genoa and in the Kingdom of Naples. The Pamphili contributed the papal connection, the palace on Via del Corso, the villa on the Janiculum, and a fortune accumulated during the pontificate of Innocent X —and, above all, by the management of Olimpia Maidalchini.

The agreement stipulated that the heirs would bear both surnames, the properties would be fused into a single entail —*fidecommisso*—, and the seat of the family would be Rome, not Genoa. Since 1671, the family was called **Doria Pamphili Landi** —incorporating the surname Landi, from a collateral branch that provided more titles— and became one of the richest and most powerful houses of the Italian aristocracy.

The historian George L. Williams, in *Papal Genealogy* (1998), notes that the Doria-Pamphili fusion was "the most advantageous marriage in the history of the Black Nobility": it combined Genoese naval power with Roman papal power, united the eastern and western Mediterranean, and created a patrimony that no Italian monarch could match in artistic density.

## 17th–18th Centuries — The Doria Pamphili collection: Velázquez, Caravaggio, Titian, Raphael

The art collection of the Doria Pamphili is one of the most extraordinary in the world in private hands. Its original nucleus was assembled by Camillo Pamphili —Olimpia's son who renounced the cardinalate— and expanded generation after generation by the Doria Pamphili.

The masterpieces of the collection include:

- The **Portrait of Innocent X** by **Diego Velázquez** (1650), the undisputed jewel of the gallery and one of the most celebrated portraits in the history of painting.
- The **Rest on the Flight into Egypt** by **Caravaggio** (c. 1597), one of the early masterpieces of the Lombard painter, with the angel musician dominating the composition.
- **Salome with the Head of John the Baptist** by **Titian** (c. 1515), a work from the Venetian master's youth.
- The **Penitent Magdalene** by **Caravaggio** (c. 1594–1596), another of the painter's early works.
- The **Double Portrait** by **Raphael** —also attributed to Andrea del Sarto.
- Works by **Guercino**, **Domenichino**, **Carracci**, **Parmigianino**, **Lorenzo Lotto**, and **Jan Brueghel the Elder**.
- A notable collection of classical sculpture —busts, sarcophagi, statues— accumulated over centuries by the Doria and the Pamphili.

The **Galleria Doria Pamphili** —the great gallery of the palace on Via del Corso— is one of the most spectacular museum spaces in Rome: a hall more than a hundred metres long, with Venetian mirrors, red velvets, crystal chandeliers, and walls literally lined with paintings from floor to ceiling. The gallery, designed by Carlo Fontana in the 17th century and completed in the 18th, follows the model of the great galleries of the Roman palaces —Galleria Colonna, Galleria Borghese— but with a particularity: it remains the property of the family, not of the Italian State.

## 19th Century — The Doria Pamphili in post-pontifical Rome

When the troops of the Kingdom of Italy took Rome on 20 September 1870, the Doria Pamphili —like all the families of the Black Nobility— closed the doors of their palace. But their position was notably more solid than that of other papal families. The Doria Pamphili did not depend exclusively on the Papal States or on curial offices: they had lands in the Kingdom of Naples, properties in Genoa, and an artistic patrimony of immense value.

During the second half of the 19th century, the Doria Pamphili diversified their investments. They bought agricultural lands in the Roman countryside and in Latium, expanded their urban properties in Rome, and maintained the exploitation of their fiefs in southern Italy. The family, which had never been exclusively papal —the Doria half was Genoese and financial—, adapted to the new national order more easily than the Orsini or the Colonna.

In the 1880s, the Villa Pamphili was partially sold to the Italian State, which converted it into a public park. The Casino del Bel Respiro and the surrounding gardens passed into the property of the Italian Republic, although the family retained the usufruct of part of the villa for decades. Today, Villa Pamphili is the largest public park in Rome —more than one hundred and eighty hectares— and still bears the name of the family that created it.

---

## 20th Century — From the Black Nobility to heritage management

During the 20th century, the Doria Pamphili followed the adaptation pattern of the Black Nobility: loss of formal political power, conservation of artistic and real estate assets, and transition towards discreet heritage management.

The palace on Via del Corso maintained its status as a private residence and museum open to the public. The Galleria Doria Pamphili, with entrance at Via del Corso 305, consolidated as one of the most visited private museums in Rome, although its fame is lesser than that of the Galleria Borghese or the Vatican Museums —which, paradoxically, makes it more attractive for visitors seeking a less crowded experience.

The family continued to reside in the piano nobile of the palace —the private rooms, not open to the public— while the rest of the building was dedicated to the exhibition of the collection and the administration of the patrimony. The formula —living in the museum— is the same applied by the Colonna, the Massimo, and other families of the Black Nobility.

In 1946, the Italian Republic abolished noble titles. But, as in the case of all the families of the Black Nobility, the abolition did not affect the true wealth: the palace, the art collection, and the real estate properties. The Doria Pamphili continued to be, as they had been since 1671, one of the richest dynasties in Rome.

---

## 2026 — Palazzo Doria Pamphili: the largest private palace in Rome

In 2026, the Doria Pamphili remain the owners of the Palazzo Doria Pamphili on Via del Corso, the largest private palace in Rome —more than a thousand rooms. The Galleria Doria Pamphili opens to the public every day and receives hundreds of thousands of visitors a year, albeit with a profile of discretion that contrasts with the overcrowding of the Vatican Museums.

The palace also remains a private residence. The family members occupy a part of the building, exactly as their ancestors did since the 17th century. The collection —Velázquez, Caravaggio, Titian, Raphael— remains in the same rooms for which it was acquired, in the same arrangement that Rainaldi and Fontana designed, in the same palace that Innocent X and Olimpia Maidalchini saw rise. It is one of the few great museums in the world that remains the property of the descendants of those who created it.

The contemporary Doria Pamphili maintain a low public profile, participate in Roman cultural life through foundations and boards of trustees, but do not seek media visibility. They do not need it. The name does everything: "Doria Pamphili" immediately evokes the admiral of Charles V and the pope of Velázquez, the popess who governed Rome without a crown, and the palace that still stands when almost all the others have been converted into embassies, state museums, or luxury hotels.

---

## The double heritage: the sea and the throne

The Doria Pamphili occupy a unique position in the history of the Black Nobility because they represent the fusion of two completely different legacies that, nevertheless, complement each other.

On the one hand, the Doria: navigators, admirals, merchants. They represent the Italy of the maritime republics —the Genoa of banks, fleets, and commercial exchanges that connected Europe with the East. Andrea Doria, the man who liberated Genoa and became *Pater Patriae*, was a naval condottiero who operated according to the logic of the Renaissance Mediterranean: flexible alliances, mercenary contracts, loyalty to the highest bidder. His world was the sea, not the court.

On the other hand, the Pamphili: jurists, diplomats, popes. They represent the Italy of ecclesiastical power —the Rome of bulls, conclaves, and papal audiences. Innocent X, the man of Velázquez's portrait, was a jurist who governed the Church from the throne of St Peter. His world was the Curia, not the sea.

The fusion of 1671 united both traditions. And the result is visible today in the Palazzo Doria Pamphili: on the one hand, the portraits of admirals and the maps of the Genoese trade routes; on the other, the portrait of Innocent X painted by Velázquez and the frescoes celebrating the glory of the Pamphili. The palace is the materialisation of the fusion: Genoa and Rome, the sea and the throne, the fleet and the tiara.

In 2026, the Doria Pamphili no longer govern oceans nor elect popes. But they own the largest private palace in Rome and one of the most extraordinary art collections in the world. And as visitors walk through the Galleria Doria Pamphili and pause before the portrait that Velázquez painted nearly four centuries ago, the admiral and the Pope continue to look at each other from the walls of the palace they built together.
