# The Corsini — The Blind Pope Who Built the Trevi Fountain

---

## 13th–15th Centuries — Origins in Florence: Bankers of the Tuscan Hills

The Corsini hail from rural Tuscany, from the hills south of Florence. The surname Corsini appears in Florentine documents from the 13th century as a family of landowners and wool merchants —just like the Barberini and the Sacchetti—. They belonged to the Florentine *popolo grasso*, the enriched bourgeoisie that governed the Republic before the rise of the Medici, and occupied positions in the city magistracies —the priorate, the council of the Twelve, the guilds—.

The first Corsini of documentary relevance was **Andrea Corsini** (1263–1321), a member of the Florentine patriciate and functionary of the Republic. His son, **Saint Andrea Corsini** (1302–1374), was a Carmelite who became bishop of Fiesole and was canonized in 1629 by Urban VIII Barberini. Having a saint in the family was immense symbolic capital in Counter-Reformation Italy: it was equivalent to divine validation of the family in the eyes of the Curia and European society. The Corsini leveraged the prestige of Saint Andrea Corsini to boost their social ascent during the 16th and 17th centuries.

The historian of Florentine nobility R. Burr Litchfield, in *Emergence of a Bureaucracy: The Florentine Patricians, 1530–1790* (Princeton, 1986), places the Corsini among the families of the Tuscan patriciate who survived the transition from the Republic to the Medicean Principality and knew how to adapt to the new dynastic order.

---

## 16th–17th Centuries — Banking Expansion: From Florence to Rome

During the 16th and 17th centuries, the Corsini followed the characteristic trajectory of Tuscan banking families: they diversified their financial operations, established branches in Rome and other European capitals, and lent money to the Curia, to the Roman nobility, and to European courts.

The Banco Corsini operated in Florence and Rome with a network of agents in London, Amsterdam, and Vienna. Its business model was the same as that of the Chigi or the Sacchetti: lending money to popes and cardinals in exchange for the concession of ecclesiastical benefices, supply contracts for the Papal States, and positions in the papal administration. The Corsini did not invent this model —Tuscan bankers had perfected it since the 13th century—, but they applied it with an efficacy that allowed them to accumulate the capital necessary to buy a place in the Black Nobility.

In 1658, the Corsini bought the title of **Marquess of Sismano** and **Marquess of Casigliano**, and in 1685 they were admitted to the Roman patriciate —the civic nobility of the city of Rome—. The acquisition of noble titles and integration into the patriciate were preparatory steps for the definitive leap: the purchase of a cardinalate and, potentially, of the papacy.

The economic historian Carlo M. Cipolla, in *Money, Prices, and Civilization in the Mediterranean World* (1956), documents the integration of Tuscan banking capital into the pontifical economy during the 16th and 17th centuries and mentions the Corsini among the principal financial operators of the Curia.

## 1730 — Lorenzo Corsini Becomes Clement XII: The Nearly Blind Pope

On July 12, 1730, after a conclave of 129 days —one of the longest of the 18th century—, Cardinal **Lorenzo Corsini** was elected Pope. He took the name **Clement XII**. He was 78 years old. He was the oldest cardinal in the Sacred College. And he was almost completely blind.

Lorenzo Corsini had been born in Florence on April 7, 1652, into the bosom of the banking family. He had studied at the Collegio Romano of the Jesuits, earned a doctorate in law at the University of Pisa, and served as governor of Castel Sant'Angelo, treasurer general of the Apostolic Chamber, and cardinal priest of San Pietro in Vincoli. He was a financial administrator, a man of law and numbers —a typical profile of the 18th-century curia, where the theologian popes of the previous century had given way to administrator popes—.

The pontificate of Clement XII lasted ten years, from 1730 until his death in February 1740. It was a surprisingly active pontificate for a man of his age and disability. Blind, bedridden, with frequent gout attacks, Clement XII governed from his bed and commissioned some of the most emblematic works of Baroque Rome.

The historian Ludwig von Pastor, in volume XXXIV of his *History of the Popes*, describes Clement XII as "a man of severe piety and tireless energy despite his ailments." He governed the Church in a period of relative European peace —between the War of Spanish Succession and the War of Austrian Succession— and dedicated his pontificate to public works, financial reform, and the promotion of Catholic missions in Asia.

## 1732 — The Trevi Fountain: Nicola Salvi and the Water the Pope Could Not See

The best-known work of the pontificate of Clement XII is the **Trevi Fountain**, the most famous fountain in the world and one of the universal icons of Rome. Clement XII did not see the fountain completed —he died in 1740, twenty-two years before its completion in 1762—, but it was he who commissioned it, who called the design competition, and who financed its initial construction.

The Trevi Fountain has a history going back to 19 B.C., when the Roman consul Agrippa built the Aqua Virgo aqueduct to supply water to the baths of the Campus Martius. During the Middle Ages, the aqueduct fell into disuse and the fountain marking its terminus —at the intersection of three streets, hence "Trevi," from Latin *trivium*— was a simple basin leaning against a wall. Popes Nicholas V and Sixtus V had restored the aqueduct in the 15th and 16th centuries, but the fountain remained modest.

In 1730, Clement XII decided that Rome deserved a fountain worthy of its history. He called a public competition in 1731, in which the best architects of the time participated —among them, Luigi Vanvitelli, the future builder of the Royal Palace of Caserta—. The winning project was that of **Nicola Salvi** (1697–1751), a relatively unknown Roman architect but of solid training in the classicist and Baroque tradition.

Salvi conceived the Trevi Fountain as a spectacle: a triumphal scenography leaning against the rear facade of the Palazzo Poli, with the figure of Ocean at the center riding a chariot drawn by sea horses, flanked by the statues of Abundance and Health, all fused with the architecture of the palace in a fusion of artificial rocks, Corinthian columns, reliefs, and waterfalls. The fountain measures twenty meters in width and twenty-six in height. It is the largest fountain in Rome.

Clement XII laid the first stone in 1732. He financed the work with the revenues of the **new pontifical lottery** that he himself instituted in 1732 —a public lottery system whose benefits were destined for public works—, inaugurating a tradition of state financing that endures in Italy to this day. The work advanced slowly due to the magnitude of the project and the technical difficulties of excavating in the Quirinal rock. Salvi died in 1751 without seeing it finished. The fountain was completed in 1762, under Clement XIII Rezzonico, with retouches by Giuseppe Pannini.

Clement XII never saw the Trevi Fountain. He had died twenty-two years before its completion. But the fountain bears his name and his coat of arms —a diagonal band on a blue and silver background— decorates the facade of the palace. The blind Pope who never saw the water commissioned the most famous fountain in the world.

## 1733–1736 — The Facade of Saint John Lateran: The Pope's Cathedral

If the Trevi Fountain is the most popular work of the pontificate of Clement XII, the **facade of the Basilica of Saint John Lateran** is the most significant from an ecclesiastical standpoint. Saint John Lateran is the cathedral of Rome —the *mater et caput omnium ecclesiarum*—, the church of the Pope as bishop of Rome. Its previous facade, built in the 16th century by Domenico Fontana, was in poor condition.

In 1733, Clement XII commissioned the architect **Alessandro Galilei** (1691–1737) —a Florentine, like the Pope— to build a new facade for the cathedral. Galilei designed a monumental Neoclassical facade with a large portico of Corinthian columns, five arches, and a central benediction loggia. The facade was completed in 1736 and is, together with the Trevi Fountain, one of the great urban interventions of the 18th-century Roman period.

In the same period, Clement XII commissioned Galilei to do the **Porta del Popolo**, the monumental gate of the Roman wall that gave entry to the city from the north —via the Via Flaminia—. The gate was remodeled in 1734 with a design that combined Baroque and Neoclassical elements and announced the transition from Baroque to Neoclassicism in Roman architecture.

Clement XII's architectural patronage is notable not only for its scale but for its almost obsessive character: a blind Pope, incapable of seeing the plans he approved, supervised —through tactile models, oral descriptions from his collaborators, and dictations to his secretaries— a program of public works that transformed the physiognomy of Rome. The architectural historian John B. Bury, in *Churches of Rome: A Guide* (1996), describes the facade of Saint John Lateran as "the first great example of Roman classicism in 18th-century papal architecture."

## 1730–1740 — The Palazzo Corsini in Trastevere: The Seat of a Florentine Dynasty

In parallel with the public works, Clement XII acquired and renovated the **Palazzo Corsini** in Trastevere, one of the oldest neighborhoods of Rome, on the right bank of the Tiber. The palace, originally built in the 15th century for the Riario family —nephews of Sixtus IV—, had passed through various hands —the Sforza, the Pamphili, the Medici— before being bought by the Corsini in 1736.

The Pope commissioned the renovation of the building to the architect **Ferdinando Fuga** (1699–1782), another Florentine, who transformed the old Renaissance palace into a monumental Baroque residence. Fuga added a new facade to the Tiber, remodeled the inner courtyard, and designed the great central staircase. The palace would become the seat of the Corsini in Rome and the home of their art collection.

In 1883, the Palazzo Corsini was sold to the Italian state and is today one of the two seats of the **Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica** —together with the Palazzo Barberini—. It houses works by Caravaggio, Rubens, Murillo, Fra Angelico, and Luca Giordano. But in the 18th century, it was the house of the Pope's nephew.

The nephew was **Neri Maria Corsini** (1685–1770), whom Clement XII made a cardinal in 1730. Neri served as Cardinal Nephew and was, in practice, the Pope's eyes. He was the one who read documents to Clement XII, who described the architectural plans to him, who negotiated with architects and contractors. Clement XII's nepotism was operational: he needed his nephew to govern. But it was also profitable for the family: Neri Corsini accumulated a considerable fortune and consolidated the family patrimony in Rome.

---

## 18th–19th Centuries — The Corsini Settle in Rome and Florence

After the death of Clement XII in 1740, the Corsini consolidated themselves as part of the Roman Black Nobility without abandoning their base in Florence. They owned the Palazzo Corsini in Trastevere, the Palazzo Corsini al Parione —on the banks of the Arno in Florence—, agricultural properties in Tuscany and Latium, and an art collection that included paintings, sculptures, and one of the best private libraries in Rome —the **Biblioteca Corsiniana**, founded by Clement XII and donated by the family to the Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei—.

The Corsini maintained their position through marriages with the Italian and European nobility —Barberini, Colonna, Boncompagni— and prudent management of their patrimony. They did not produce another Pope, but their name remained indelibly associated with the Trevi Fountain and the facade of Saint John Lateran.

---

## 20th–21st Centuries — The Corsini in Contemporary Italy

In the 20th century, the Corsini —like the rest of the Black Nobility— lost their formal political power but preserved their social prestige and cultural heritage. The Palazzo Corsini in Trastevere was sold to the Italian state in 1883 and is today a public museum. The Palazzo Corsini al Parione in Florence still belongs to the family and is one of the most beautiful private residences in the city.

In 2026, the Corsini continue to exist. The title of **Prince Corsini** is hereditary. The family preserves properties in Florence and in Tuscany, manages its cultural legacy —the Biblioteca Corsiniana remains a reference point in historical research— and maintains a discreet public profile. Their mark on Rome, however, is permanent: each of the millions of tourists who toss a coin into the Trevi Fountain is visiting a work commissioned by a blind Pope who never saw it finished.

---

## The Blind Pope and the City He Could Not See

The Corsini represent a specific type within the Black Nobility: the **city-state dynasty** that transplants its Florentine financial capital to the center of Roman pontifical power. Like the Barberini, the Sacchetti, and the Chigi, the Corsini were Tuscan bankers before being Roman princes. But Clement XII embodies a paradox that no other family can claim: he was a Pope elected at 78, nearly blind, with frequent gout attacks, who governed for ten years and commissioned the Trevi Fountain, the facade of Saint John Lateran, the Porta del Popolo, and the Palazzo Corsini in Trastevere.

Clement XII's blindness was not an obstacle. It was almost a metaphor: a Pope who could not see ordered the building of fountains, facades, and gates that millions of people would see for centuries. The Trevi Fountain —with its roar of water, its artificial rocks, and its triumphant Ocean— is a monument to the will of a man who did not see it but imagined it. And the Corsini, the family of the Florentine banker who sat on the throne of Peter, have been associated for three centuries with the most photographed monument in Italy.
