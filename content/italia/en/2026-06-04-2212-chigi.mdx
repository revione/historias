# The Chigi — From the Bank of Siena to the Palace of the Prime Minister

---

## 14th Century — Origins in Siena: bankers between hills and popes

The Chigi come from Siena, the Tuscan city that rivalled Florence during the Late Middle Ages and whose banking system was, during the 13th and 14th centuries, one of the most sophisticated in Europe. The first Chigi documented with certainty is a family member registered in Siena in the 14th century, linked to the wool trade and incipient banking. The surname Chigi —pronounced *kee-yee* in Italian— belonged to the Sienese urban patriciate, that class of merchant-bankers that governed the Italian city-republics.

Siena had been during the 13th century the seat of the **Monte dei Paschi**, founded in 1472 but whose financial roots went back to medieval credit operations. The Chigi moved within that ecosystem: they changed money, financed wool cargoes, and granted loans to the Republic of Siena and to the Roman Curia. They were not feudal aristocrats like the Orsini or the Colonna. They were money bourgeois. And in Renaissance Italy, money bought everything —including the papacy.

During the 14th and 15th centuries, the Chigi accumulated capital, diversified their banking operations, and established branches in Rome, where the demand for financial services was insatiable: the popes needed loans for wars, construction, art, and diplomacy. The Chigi were there to lend to them.

---

## 1466–1520 — Agostino Chigi, *Il Magnifico*: the richest banker in Europe

The man who transformed the Chigi from provincial bankers to a European financial power was **Agostino Chigi** (1466–1520), known in life as *Il Magnifico* —the same epithet borne by Lorenzo de' Medici in Florence. Agostino was not a noble: he was a banker. But he was the richest banker in Europe during the High Renaissance, with a fortune that rivalled that of the Medici and the Fuggers.

Agostino began his career in the family bank of Siena, but in 1487 moved to Rome with his father Mariano. There he founded the **Banco Chigi**, an institution that lent money to the Curia, financed military campaigns, managed the revenues of the Papal States, and operated with bills of exchange across Europe. His network of agents extended from London to Constantinople. He was the principal banker of two of the most powerful —and most expensive— popes of the Renaissance: **Julius II della Rovere** (1503–1513), the warrior Pope who commissioned the Sistine Chapel from Michelangelo and St Peter's Basilica from Bramante, and **Leo X Medici** (1513–1521), the Pope who financed Raphael and drove the papacy to bankruptcy building the new St Peter's.

Agostino's most famous loan was to Julius II to finance the war against Venice in 1509. The Pope needed money for his military campaigns in northern Italy —Julius II rode in armour at the head of his troops— and Agostino lent it to him. In exchange, he received the monopoly of the alum mines of Tolfa, a mineral indispensable for the European textile industry. Alum was the Renaissance equivalent of oil: without it, dyes did not set in cloths. Agostino controlled the European supply of alum for a decade, obtaining astronomical profits.

Agostino's wealth was legendary. In 1518, he offered a banquet at his Roman villa to Pope Leo X and twelve cardinals. The chronicler Paolo Giovio records that all the tableware was solid silver and that, upon finishing each course, Agostino ordered the silver plates to be thrown into the Tiber. The gesture was calculated ostentation —the Tiber was not so deep beneath the villa and the servants recovered the tableware with nets—, but the legend of the banker who threw silver into the river spread throughout Europe and contributed to cementing the Chigi's fame as the boldest money family on the continent.

## 1508–1511 — The Villa Farnesina: the banker who hired Raphael

The most visible symbol of Agostino Chigi's power is the **Villa Farnesina**, built between 1508 and 1511 on Via della Lungara, in the Roman Trastevere, on the banks of the Tiber. The name "Farnesina" is a historical accident: the villa was bought by the Farnese in 1577 and adopted its current name, but it was built by a Chigi and conceived as the pleasure residence of the richest banker in Europe.

Agostino commissioned the design from the Sienese architect **Baldassarre Peruzzi**, and the interior decoration from the best artists of the Rome of Julius II and Leo X: **Raphael** painted the fresco of *The Triumph of Galatea* (1512) in the main loggia, one of the absolute masterpieces of the High Renaissance, and the cycle of *The Story of Cupid and Psyche* in the entrance loggia; **Sebastiano del Piombo** painted the lunette of *Polyphemus*; and Peruzzi himself decorated the *Hall of Perspectives* with architectural trompe-l'œils that create the illusion of an open loggia over Rome.

The Villa Farnesina was not a fortified palace like those of the medieval Roman barons. It was a suburban villa designed for parties, banquets, concerts, and theatrical performances. Agostino used the villa to impress cardinals, ambassadors, popes, and princes. Each fresco, each loggia, each garden was a message: the Chigi's money bought not only political power, but beauty. Raphael worked for the Pope in the Vatican in the morning and for the banker Chigi in the Farnesina in the afternoon. Money and art merged into a single space.

The Villa Farnesina passed to the Farnese in 1577, and from them to the Bourbons of Naples, and finally —in 1927— to the Italian State, which today manages it as a public museum through the Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei. But its original creator was a Chigi. And the name of Chigi, though it is not in the name of the villa, is in every stone of its construction.

## 1655 — Fabio Chigi becomes Alexander VII: the banker sits on the throne of Peter

On 7 April 1655, after an eighty-day conclave, Cardinal **Fabio Chigi** (1599–1667) was elected Pope. He took the name **Alexander VII**. He was a Chigi on his father's side —descendant of a branch of the family that had remained in Siena— and carried to the throne of Peter all the weight of the banking, diplomatic, and cultural tradition accumulated over two centuries.

Fabio Chigi was not an ecclesiastical upstart. He was a refined intellectual, doctor of philosophy, law, and theology from the University of Siena, trained in the humanistic circles of Tuscany. He had served as inquisitor in Malta, vice-legate in Ferrara, apostolic nuncio in Cologne —where he participated in the negotiations of the Peace of Westphalia (1648) that ended the Thirty Years' War— and Secretary of State of Innocent X. He knew European diplomacy better than any other cardinal of his generation. He had seen the collapse of the Holy Roman Empire, the consolidation of France as a hegemonic power, and the progressive marginalisation of the papacy in international affairs.

His election as Pope was the result of a complex conclave in which the opposing factions —Spanish, French, the cardinal nephews of Innocent X— reached a compromise. Fabio Chigi was acceptable to all because he did not belong to any of the great papal families of the moment: he was not a Barberini, nor a Borghese, nor a Pamphili. He was, fundamentally, a Chigi. And the Chigi were bankers, not territorial princes. No one feared them. No one imagined that a Chigi Pope would transform the physiognomy of Rome forever.

## 1655–1667 — Alexander VII and Bernini: the Baroque Rome that defines the eternal city

Alexander VII carried out an urbanistic and architectural programme that transformed Rome into the Baroque city we know today. His principal collaborator was **Gian Lorenzo Bernini** (1598–1680), the greatest artist of the Italian Baroque, who had already served Urban VIII Barberini and Innocent X Pamphili but found in Alexander VII his most ambitious patron.

The works commissioned or supervised by Alexander VII and executed by Bernini include:

- The **Colonnade of St Peter's** (1656–1667): two elliptical arms of 284 columns and 88 pilasters that embrace St Peter's Square. Bernini conceived it as "the arms of the Church welcoming humanity." But it is also a political message: the Pope —the Chigi who governs— embraces the world. The colonnade is the most visible work of the papacy in any photograph, postcard, or film about Rome.
- The **Cathedra Petri** (1657–1666): the gilded bronze reliquary in the apse of St Peter's containing the supposed chair of St Peter, sustained by the colossal figures of the Doctors of the Church —St Ambrose, St Augustine, St John Chrysostom, and St Athanasius. The light entering through the oval window of the apse is designed to create the illusion of the Holy Spirit descending in the form of a dove.
- The **obelisk of the Piazza del Popolo** (1662): Alexander VII placed the Egyptian obelisk of Ramesses II in the centre of Piazza del Popolo, accompanied by fountains and an urbanistic design that converted the northern entrance of Rome into a monumental gateway.
- The façade of **Santa Maria della Pace**, the church of **Sant'Andrea al Quirinale** —a masterpiece of elliptical Baroque—, the interventions in **Santa Maria in Via Lata** and in the **Quirinal Palace**.

The art historian Richard Krautheimer, in his definitive work *The Rome of Alexander VII*, argues that the Chigi Pope "had a coherent vision of Rome as the stage for papal authority, and Bernini was the instrument of that vision." Alexander VII did not commission individual buildings: he designed an urban landscape. Each square, each church, each fountain was part of a plan that transformed Rome into the theatrical capital of Baroque Catholicism.

The architectural legacy of Alexander VII —and therefore of the Chigi— is the Rome that millions of tourists visit today. The colonnade, the Cathedra, the Piazza del Popolo: all bear the imprint of the banker Pope from Siena.

## 1655–1667 — The patronage of science: Alexander VII and the Collegio Romano

Alexander VII was an unusual Pope in his interest in the natural sciences. During his pontificate he protected and financed the Jesuits of the **Collegio Romano**, where scientists such as **Athanasius Kircher** (1602–1680) —the German Jesuit polymath who investigated Egyptology, vulcanology, optics, and music— worked under papal protection. Alexander VII personally visited the Collegio, reviewed the experiments, and maintained correspondence with European scientists.

The Chigi Pope also protected **Francesco Borromini**, Bernini's great rival, commissioning him to complete Sant'Ivo alla Sapienza —the church of the University of Rome— and other works. His patronage was broad and eclectic: architecture, painting, sculpture, urbanism, and science formed a continuum.

## 1661–1667 — The last nepotism: the Chigi, from Siena to Roman princes

Alexander VII was the last Pope to practise nepotism openly and systematically before the institution of the cardinal nephew began to be restricted by his successors. The Chigi Pope brought his family from Siena to Rome, installed them in palaces, bought them titles, and transformed them into the last great papal family of the Black Nobility.

Alexander VII's nepotism was articulated through two principal figures:

- **Flavio Chigi** (1631–1693), nephew of the Pope, appointed cardinal in 1657 at only twenty-six years of age. Flavio served as Cardinal Nephew —the *Cardinalis Nepos* who controlled access to the Pope, managed diplomacy, and administered benefices—, accumulated an immense fortune, and built the **Palazzo Chigi** in Ariccia.
- **Agostino Chigi** (1634–1705), another nephew of the Pope, who received the **Principality of Campagnano** and married Maria Virginia Borghese, uniting the Chigi —Sienese bankers— with one of the most solid papal families of Rome. This matrimonial alliance cemented the Chigi's integration into the Roman aristocracy.

In 1661, Alexander VII bought the **Duchy of Ariccia** —a locality in the Castelli Romani, south of Rome— from the Savelli, a declining aristocratic family, and handed it to his family. Ariccia became the fief of the Chigi, with a castle, ducal palace, churches, woods, and agricultural lands. The Pope commissioned Bernini to redesign the church of **Santa Maria dell'Assunzione** in Ariccia and build a ducal residence.

Alexander VII's nepotism was intense but relatively contained compared with that of Paul III Farnese or Paul V Borghese. He did not create a sovereign duchy in Parma —he had no territory to separate from the Papal States—, but he did incorporate the Chigi into the Roman elite through marriages, titles, and properties. When Alexander VII died in May 1667, the Chigi were already one of the most important families in Rome, with a cardinal, a prince, a duchy, and several palaces.

## 17th–19th Centuries — The Chigi survive: palaces, marriages, and the long shadow of a Pope

After the death of Alexander VII, the Chigi followed the pattern of all papal families: settle into the Roman aristocracy, preserve the patrimony, and avoid extinction. Cardinal Flavio Chigi continued as an influential figure in the Curia until his death in 1693, and his heirs maintained the Duchy of Ariccia, the Principality of Campagnano, and the Palazzo Chigi in Rome.

The Chigi managed their wealth with banking prudence —after all, they remained the most celebrated banking family in Italian history. They did not squander their fortune like the Borghese or the Barberini on a colossal scale, but administered it in the Sienese manner: reinvestment, strategic marriages, conservation of capital. The Chigi entail —the *fidecommisso*— protected the family patrimony for generations.

In the 18th century, the Chigi intermarried with other papal and princely families through marriages —with the Odescalchi, the Doria Pamphili, the Ruspoli—, consolidating their position in the kinship network of the Black Nobility. The surname Chigi-Albani (since 1852, when it was united with the Albani, another papal family) reflects this strategy of dynastic fusion.

## Early 20th Century — The Palazzo Chigi becomes the seat of the Italian government

The **Palazzo Chigi**, located in Piazza Colonna, in the very centre of Rome, was originally built for the Aldobrandini family at the end of the 16th century. The Chigi bought it in 1659, during the pontificate of Alexander VII, and converted it into their principal Roman residence. The palace was expanded and redecorated by the architects **Felice della Greca** and **Giovanni Battista Contini** —a disciple of Bernini—, adding the façade that today dominates Piazza Colonna.

In 1916, the Chigi sold the palace to the Italian State. The government of liberal Italy needed a seat for the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and, later, for the Presidency of the Council of Ministers —the office equivalent to Prime Minister. Since 1961, the Palazzo Chigi has been the official seat of the **Prime Minister of Italy**. Every Italian knows the name: "Palazzo Chigi" is synonymous with Italian executive power, in the same way that "La Moncloa" is in Spain or "Downing Street" in the United Kingdom.

The irony is perfect: the family of bankers that financed popes and built Renaissance villas ended up ceding their palace to the State. But the name remains. Every time an Italian journalist says "Palazzo Chigi ha comunicato" or "fonti di Palazzo Chigi," they are pronouncing the surname of the Sienese family that began by lending money to the Curia in the 15th century.

The Chigi retained, however, the **Palazzo Chigi of Ariccia**, which remains the property of the family and is partially open to the public. The ducal residence in the Castelli Romani, with its Baroque gardens, its paintings, and its family chapel, is the last physical testimony of the duchy that Alexander VII created for his own.

## 2026 — The Chigi today: the name that every Italian recognises

In 2026, the Chigi are one of the few families of the Black Nobility whose surname remains in daily use in Italian politics. Palazzo Chigi appears on the television news every week. The name that began with Agostino Chigi lending money to Julius II is today the name of the centre of political power in Italy.

The Chigi continue to exist as a family. They maintain ownership of the **Palazzo Chigi of Ariccia** —partially open to the public as a museum—, properties in Rome and in their native Siena, and a cultural legacy that spans from the Villa Farnesina —although they no longer own it— to the colonnade of St Peter's. The park of Villa Chigi, in the north of Rome, bears their name and is one of the city's green lungs. The Palazzo Chigi of Ariccia, with its collection of Baroque paintings, is visited by thousands of tourists each year.

The family remains discreet. The current head of the house of Chigi-Albani della Rovere, Prince **Mario Chigi Albani della Rovere** and his heirs, maintain the institutional profile that characterises the contemporary Black Nobility: presence in the cultural and social life of Rome, management of the historical and artistic heritage, and minimal public visibility. They do not need media exposure. The surname does everything.

Unlike other papal families whose legacy is principally a museum —the Borghese, for example—, the Chigi enjoy a unique phenomenon: their surname is part of the political language of the Italian Republic. When an Italian prime minister speaks from the balcony of Palazzo Chigi, they are framed by the architecture that the Chigi commissioned in the 17th century. The banker who threw silver into the Tiber today has a palace that dictates the destinies of sixty million Italians.

---

## The banker-pope pattern: from Siena to the Quirinal

The Chigi represent a singular pattern in the history of the Black Nobility: that of the **banker-pope**. Unlike the Orsini and the Colonna —medieval feudal lords—, the Farnese —captains of Latium—, or the Borghese —curial jurists—, the Chigi reached power from money. They had no castles, no armies, no military tradition. They had a bank.

Agostino Chigi financed the popes who built the Roman Renaissance —Julius II and Leo X— and commissioned frescoes from Raphael for his private villa. Fabio Chigi, his descendant, sat on the throne of Peter two centuries later and commissioned from Bernini the most visible work of the papacy —the colonnade of St Peter's. The Chigi did not inherit power: they bought it. And then they transformed it into stone, into art, into institutions, and into a surname that is today part of the political topography of Italy.

There is an invisible but deep continuity between the Renaissance banker who lent money to Julius II to wage war against Venice and the palace that houses the Italian prime minister in 2026. Both bear the same surname. Both are Chigi. The history of the Chigi is, ultimately, the history of how financial money transforms into political power, political power into cultural heritage, and cultural heritage into a permanent presence in the collective memory of a nation.
